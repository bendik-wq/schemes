#### The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)

Three things.

One. Ask six questions, not twenty. Contact details first, so you can still reach the people who quit halfway. Then what they sell, revenue in wide bands, what they've already spent, who decides, and when they'd start.

[SCREEN: the six questions as a list, contact details highlighted as "screen 1"]

Two. Give every answer points, and set the points from money, not gut feel. Take your last 50 calls. For each answer, add up the revenue those calls produced and divide by the number of calls. That's the dollar value of the answer. High-value answers get high points.

[SCREEN: simple table: "Answer | Calls | Revenue | Revenue per call"]

Three. Add up the points and send people to one of three places. A: your best calendar, which today is probably yours. B: a calendar too, but after a short video and a second look. C: no call. A free resource and a polite note.

And here's the part most people miss. Only tell your ad platform about A and B. If Meta only hears about good applicants, it goes and finds more of them. Daniel's phrase for this is telling the pixel to "get us the rich kind".

That's the lesson. Next: Daniel's numbers, a form you can copy, and a worked example.

#### The lesson (3:00–9:00)

**What Daniel does**

Daniel Iles runs Viral Coach. In his own videos he says it reached around $5 million a month, almost all through one machine: ads, a page, an application, a call. I've gone through 90 of his videos. The application is where he's most precise.

[SCREEN: his eight fields, listed. Lower third: "Daniel Iles, '8 questions improved my business by 2,629%', 0:31"]

His form has eight fields. First name, last name, business email, phone. "Tell us about your business." Monthly revenue. What they've spent on marketing recently. Years in business. When they plan to start. That's it.

Then he does what almost nobody does. He puts a dollar value, revenue per call, on every answer. Some of what he found:

Email domain. Applicants with a business domain earn far more per call than Gmail, Yahoo or Hotmail. He doesn't block the Gmail leads. He routes them to weaker reps and sends custom-domain leads to his best closers. He says that alone improved throughput about 250%.

Revenue band. Routing by revenue made a 643% difference in his data. But richer isn't always better. Revenue per call stopped rising above about $250K a month.

Time in business. Under one year was worth far less per call than one to three years. A 248% difference.

Booking lead time. Calls booked one to six hours out averaged $49 per call. Further out did much better. That's next episode.

[SCREEN: four bars: "Email domain ~250% · Revenue band 643% · Years in business 248% · Booking lead time 737%". Caption: "His data, his business"]

And combinations matter more than single answers. Applicants under $10K a month in revenue and under $1K in marketing spend: 53% showed up, 43% of those were qualified, 3% closed. About $115K across 179 calls. He decided that wasn't worth the team's time, and that bracket is now always declined.

Three more rules from him you can use today.

Ask about the past, not the future. "What have you spent trying to solve this?" gets an honest answer. "What's your budget?" gets a hopeful one. Past spend predicts future spend.

Read the open-text answer. One to three words means low intent. A paragraph means they're invested. He also scans it for industries he can't help. That removed 12% of his leads.

And don't disqualify in your copy. Don't write "only for businesses over $100K". Ask the question and route quietly.

His summary of all this: "Don't even listen to me. Measure your own data."

**The version for a $30–300K a month service business**

You don't have thousands of applications a month. You might have 40 to 150. That's enough. Here's the six-question form I'd start with.

[SCREEN: the form, one question per card, points shown beside each option]

Question one, contact. Name, business email, phone. On the first screen, so a half-finished application can still be called. Business domain: 10 points. Personal email: 0.

Question two. "What do you sell, to whom, and roughly at what price?" A real paragraph: 10 points. A few words: 0. An industry you can't serve: flagged, straight to C.

Question three. Monthly revenue, in wide bands. Wide on purpose. Daniel's point: a band like $50K to $100K gives you a reason to ask "closer to 50 or 100?" on the call, and people answer more precisely in conversation. Under $10K: 0. $10K to $30K: 10. $30K to $100K: 20. $100K to $300K: 25. Over $300K: 20, not 25, because your data may show what his did. Bigger isn't always better for a one-call close.

Question four. "What have you spent in the last 90 days on marketing, coaching or agencies to grow this?" None: 0. Under $2K: 5. $2K to $10K: 15. Over $10K: 20.

Question five. "Who else is involved in a decision like this?" Just me: 15. Me and a partner: 10, and the partner gets invited to the call. Someone else decides: 0, and the lead can't go above B.

Question six. "If this is a fit, when would you start?" Within two weeks: 20. Within 30 days: 12. Later than that: C, and a free resource. Daniel says that's only about 8% of his leads, but it puts a start date in writing that you confirm on the call.

Maximum score, 100. A is 70 and up. B is 40 to 69. C is under 40, or any hard flag. Two hard caps, straight from his combination rule. Under $10K revenue plus under $1K spend is always C. A start more than 30 days out is always C.

[SCREEN: our own scoring screen in Funnel HQ, demo data. Points per answer, total, tier, the flag that capped it]

This is our own system. Points per answer, the total, the tier, any flag that capped it. A leads see a calendar straight away. B leads watch a short video about the call first, then book. C leads go to a resources page. The ad platform only hears about A and B, and an A is worth more than a B.

Now the important bit. The points I just gave you are a starting guess. In week one, guess. By month two, replace every guess with money.

**Worked example**

Round numbers. You sell a $10,000 consulting package. Last quarter you took 60 calls and closed 9. That's $90,000, or $1,500 per call.

Now split those 60 calls by one answer: email domain. Thirty came from business domains and 7 closed. $70,000, so $2,333 per call. Thirty came from Gmail-type addresses and 2 closed. $20,000, so $667 per call.

[SCREEN: the two rows, "$2,333 per call" vs "$667 per call"]

That's the dollar value of the answer. One answer, three and a half times the money. Do the same for each question, and you have your points.

Then route. If you're the only closer, routing means different slots, not different people. A leads get your best hours. B leads get the end-of-day slots. C leads get no slot at all. Five C calls a week kept off your calendar is almost four hours back.

And when you hire your first closer, which is episode 15, the tiers are already there. Daniel starts new reps on the lowest tier and makes them earn their way up. You can do the same on day one, because the form already sorts the leads.

**One more lever: friction**

Every question costs you applicants. Daniel says halving his discovery questions lifted total applicants about 30%. So treat questions as a dial: stricter when the calendar is full, looser when it's empty. Six is a sensible middle.

#### The lesson (3:00–9:00)

**What Daniel does**

Daniel Iles has talked about this part of his funnel in a lot of detail. Here's what he describes, in his business.

Speed. Every booking gets contacted within five minutes, on three channels: email, text and a real phone call. He cites a HubSpot and MIT finding that contact inside five minutes made a sale about 100 times more likely.

The rep text. About four minutes after the confirmation email, three short automated texts go out "from" the assigned rep. His example: "Hey, this is Jared… can you send me your social links?" The texts include the prep video as a YouTube link. He says that lifted video views about 25% compared with the page alone.

[SCREEN: mock phone, three bubbles from "Jared"]

Then the reply tells them something. No reply within 30 minutes lowers the lead's priority. Leads who do reply are taken out of the extra confirmation steps, because they don't need them.

The engagement numbers, from over a thousand closes. Opened no email and replied to no text: about a 3% chance of showing up qualified. Engaged on one channel only: about 14 to 16%. Opened the email and replied to the rep's text: 42%.

[SCREEN: three bars: 3% · 14–16% · 42%. Caption: "Daniel Iles, his data"]

So he uses it. His lead reps only take calls where the lead did both. Cold bookings go to newer reps or a setter to confirm. And the roughly 20% who do neither are cancelled after a few hours as likely fake, which he says gives reps back about 20% of their day.

The booking window. His early advice was two to seven days out, never weeks. His later data is more specific. Calls booked one to six hours out averaged $49 in revenue per call. Six to 72 hours was much better, and 72 to 96 hours could be better still. A 737% spread. He also says most people don't plan more than four or five workdays ahead, which is why "weeks out" collapses.

Reminders. One version he describes: an email 24 hours before, a text four hours before, an email one hour before, a text at 30 minutes, and a last text at five minutes saying the meeting has started. Every one has the link. Every one should have a reschedule link too. Without it, he says, people cancel and never rebook.

But not too much. He says sending five to eight emails in 24 hours reduced interest. And one week testing a heavy pre-call contact system cost him about $300,000. Heavy nurture suits lower-income consumer offers. It annoys owners of multi-million-dollar businesses.

The people who apply and don't book. About 20% of his applicants finish the form and don't pick a time. If they haven't booked within five minutes, someone texts and calls. Again later that day. Then once a day for three days. Then into a long-term email sequence. No-shows and cancels get contacted daily for five days to rebook.

And the rep makes the difference. His top rep had an 84% show rate against a team average of 48%, by talking to prospects before the call. Same leads. That's why he says, "The leads suck argument is absolutely not true."

**The version for a $30–300K a month service business**

You probably don't have reps or setters. You have you, and maybe an assistant. Good. A text from the founder is stronger than a text from a rep. Here's the stack I'd run.

Four hours before. A text with the link.

#### The map (0:20–0:45)

[SCREEN: box 6, caption "Part 6 of 7, continued"]

This is still part 6 of 7. Last episode was the 45-minute call. This one is the flowchart for when it goes sideways.

Same audience: a $3,000-plus service, sold on a call, roughly $30K to $300K a month. If you're looking for clever closing lines to push people over, this isn't that. Daniel's approach is the opposite. Find the objection early, while you still have leverage, and deal with it then.

#### The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)

Three things.

One. Every objection is one of four: budget, authority, need or timing. Each one has a question you ask in discovery, before the price, that stops it showing up at the end.

[SCREEN: four boxes: B · A · N · T]

Two. If it still shows up at the close, don't argue with it. Isolate it. "Money aside, is this right for you?" And change the terms if you have to, but never the price.

Three. Tag every lost call with one of those four letters. Once a month, find the most common one and fix it upstream: on the form, on the page, in the ads or in the offer. That's how the machine gets better without you getting pushier.

That's the lesson. The rest is the five objections, word for word.

#### Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)

[B-ROLL: founder at a whiteboard with a hand-drawn flowchart]

Quick word on us. We build the full acquisition machine for coaches, consultants, agencies and B2B firms in 30 days. The objection flowchart is part of part 6, written from your own lost calls. If you want yours built, the application is under the video. Now the detail.

#### The lesson (3:00–9:00)

**What Daniel says**

His principle: "every mistake that you commit at the end of the call" reveals a mistake you made earlier in the call. And: "You're not here to overcome the objection. You're here to understand why."

The reason is leverage. He says the rep has the most leverage in discovery, before any price, programme detail or start date. After the pitch, the prospect has it. So you dig in early.

His single biggest fix came from data. His team tags every lost deal as budget, authority, need or timing. Then they find the category that loses the most deals, find the rep who rarely gets it, and copy that rep. For authority, the fix was opening every call with: "Who else from your team needs to be on this call?" He says once that rolled out team-wide, everyone closed more.

The same loop works on money. When budget losses piled up, his sales manager added three new financing options. Not a lower price. Different terms.

And it feeds marketing. He talks about turning objections into ad angles. "Ads don't work for me" became an angle about getting clients without paid ads. When a prospect tells you exactly what would have to happen before they'd buy, he treats it as offer research.

One warning from him. The most dangerous objections are the ones you never hear. His phrase: "The most dangerous objections are the ones that you don't get." "This looks like a scam" never comes up on a call, because those people never book. You fix that one on the page, in the reviews and in the reminders.

**The five objections, and the question that prevents each**

Here's how that translates for a founder selling their own service. Five objections you'll hear. For each: the question you ask early, and what to say if it still comes up.

[SCREEN: each objection appears as a card with "Ask early" and "If it still comes up"]

**One. "I need to talk to my partner."** That's authority.

Ask early, in the first five minutes: "Who else is involved in a decision like this? Should they be on this call?" If yes, reschedule with them there. Daniel's point is that the prospect will pitch it to their partner worse than you would. If no, ask why not, so it can't appear later.

If it still comes up: "If your partner were fully on board, is this the right decision for you? Why?" Then invite the partner to the kickoff, so the work persuades them.

**Two. "It's too expensive."** That's budget, and usually it's doubt about value, not lack of money.

Ask early: "How do you get clients now, and what does that cost you?" You want to be selling a better use of money they already spend, not new money.

If it still comes up: "Money aside, do you think this would work for you? Why?" If the answer is yes and the problem is cash, change the terms. Two payments instead of one. Never cut the price. Cutting the price on the call tells them the first number wasn't real.

**Three. "Not right now. Maybe next quarter."** That's timing.

Ask late in discovery: "When are you looking to get this running? And why now?" Daniel says his team never accepts a quick "right now" either. Their words: "We never accept right now as an answer… despite those being positive answers." They ask for three reasons. Reasons people say out loud, they don't contradict 15 minutes later.

If it still comes up, find out if there's a real blocker. A holiday, a hire, a launch. Then: "If we can handle that for you, would you be open to starting sooner?" Start the prep now, or push the start date. If there's no blocker, just no urgency, go back to the cost of waiting from last episode.

**Four. "Let me think about it."** That's need, hiding.

Ask early: "What's the plan if nothing changes in six months?" If they have a good plan B, Daniel's advice is to push away: "Honestly, that sounds easier. Why not do that?" They'll tell you why it won't work.
