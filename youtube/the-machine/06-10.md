#### [0:45–2:15] The recipe, fast

[SCREEN: three numbered lines build one at a time.]

Three steps.

One. Decide what a client is worth to you. Take 30% of it. That's the most you can spend to win one client. Daniel's rule for service businesses is that acquisition cost stays at or under 30% of lifetime value.

Two. Turn that into a cost per booked call. Multiply it by your show rate and your close rate. That's your maximum cost per booked call. Write it down. That's the target.

Three. Check the total. Daniel keeps ad spend at or under about 25% of revenue. When it creeps above that, he pulls back on testing and feeds the ads that already work. Then forecast. Spend, divided by cost per call, times what each call is worth. If the forecast doesn't work on paper, it won't work in the ad account.

That's the whole lesson. Price, show rate, close rate, one multiplication.

Most founders who run ads never do it. They let Meta decide what a call should cost, and Meta's answer is always "cheaper". The last episode showed where that goes. His cheapest calls were people who were never going to buy.

So we start from the other end. What can you afford to pay for a call, given what a client is worth? Then every ad, every test and every budget decision gets measured against that one number.

#### [2:40–8:55] The lesson

[SCREEN: quote card. "Daniel Iles · 2026 Meta Ads course · 35:31". Link in description.]

Here's Daniel's worked example, from his 2026 Meta ads course.

Lifetime value, $5,000. Thirty percent of that is $1,500. That's what he'll spend to get one client. He assumes a 50% show rate and a 20% close rate. So 20 booked calls become 10 shows become 2 clients. Two clients at $1,500 is $3,000 across 20 calls. That's $150 per booked call.

Then he says adjust it. Commissions, card fees, refunds, and the difference between your reps.

Notice what's not in there. Industry benchmarks. What your friend pays per lead. What an agency calls "good". The number comes from your own value per client and nothing else.

Two more of his numbers, so you can see the range. In his business today, he says a booked call costs about $450, and a qualified call that actually shows up costs about $1,500. When he started, a call cost about $50. Calls got more expensive and the business got much bigger. The right call at $450 is worth more than ten wrong ones at $50.

And the ceiling. In one August, he says he spent $700,000 on Meta and made just under $3 million in new revenue. That's ad spend at 24% of revenue. About four times return. In his $3 million month he says it was 15%. His line is 25%.

[SCREEN: a one-page sheet. Five rows, empty.]

Now let's make it yours. One page. Five rows.

Row one, price. What a new client pays you. If clients reliably renew, use what they pay in the first twelve months. Not the dream number. The number you'd bet on.

Row two, show rate. Of the calls booked, how many turn up.

Row three, close rate. Of the calls that show, how many buy.

Row four, acquisition cap. Thirty percent of row one.

Row five, maximum cost per booked call. Row four, times row two, times row three.

[SCREEN: the sheet fills in as each number is said.]

Worked example, round numbers.

You sell a $10,000 consulting engagement. Row one.

Thirty percent is $3,000. But some of that goes on card fees, software and the odd refund. Call it $500. That leaves $2,500 for ads per client. And $2,500 is 25% of $10,000. Daniel's two rules land in the same place.

Show rate, 60%. Close rate, 20% of the calls that show. So one booked call is worth 0.6 times 0.2. That's 12% of a client.

$2,500 times 12% is $300. Your maximum cost per booked call is $300.

[SCREEN: "$10,000 × 12% = $1,200 per booked call. Paying $300. 4×."]

Look at it the other way. Each booked call is worth $1,200 to you on average. $10,000 times 12%. You're paying $300 to get $1,200. Four times. The same ratio he reported for that August.

Now the forecast. Daniel's version is spend divided by cost per lead gives you leads, and leads times revenue per lead gives you revenue. He calls revenue per lead the number they make every decision from. He says his team once built five separate forecasts for a month. All five landed between $5.1 and $5.3 million, and they hit it. That's the skill. Predict with numbers, then check the prediction.

Yours. Say you want six new clients a month. Each client takes about eight booked calls, so call it 50 calls. Fifty calls at $300 is $15,000 of spend. Six clients at $10,000 is $60,000. $15,000 over $60,000 is 25%. It works on paper.

Three things break this sheet. Let me save you from them.

First, your close rate is probably a guess, and probably too high. If you've mostly closed referrals, those calls arrive half sold. Daniel says for a long time they closed about 40% of organic calls and under 10% of paid ones. He also says that in one $3.5 million month the close rate was 12%, so don't plan on 20 or 30.

So for cold traffic, halve your referral close rate to start. In our example, 20% becomes 10%, and the maximum cost per call drops from $300 to $150. Run both versions. If the business only works at the optimistic one, you don't have an ad problem. You have an offer problem. That was episode two.

Second, close rate is worth more than ad cost. His line: "Going from 12% to 24% close rate is the same as dropping your ad costs by 50%." When the numbers are tight, the cheapest fix is often on the call, not in the ad account.

Third, booked calls are the wrong thing to count once you have data. Some booked calls are people who will never buy. Daniel's bridge number is cost per SQL. That's a lead your closer marks as showed and qualified, whether they bought or not. He says there are roughly three to five times more of those than sales, so you get enough data to decide while spend is still small. Start with cost per booked call because it's all you have. Within a month, switch to cost per qualified call.

One more use for the number. It sets your test budget. Daniel gives each new ad three to five times the target before he judges it. At $150 a call that's $450 to $750. At our $300 it's $900 to $1,500 per ad. Which tells you how many ads you can afford to test each month. Keep that in mind. It's the whole problem in the next episode.

And cash. If clients pay in instalments, part of the money arrives later. Daniel says the typical business collects about 30% of contract value in the first 30 days, and he collects about 85%. Fund ads from cash collected, not revenue booked. We'll go deep on that near the end of the series.

#### [0:45–2:05] The recipe, fast

[SCREEN: three lines build.]

Three rules.

One, volume. Daniel's rule of thumb is one really good new video for about every $5,000 a month of ad spend. And a library of around 100 ads before you call anything a test.

Two, angles before hooks. An angle is a different reason to buy the same thing. A hook is just the first line. Test five angles first. Only make variations of the ones that win. He says they burned about $200,000 testing variations of the wrong angle.

Three, make them cheap and make them true. Founder talking heads, which he says have the highest return in his account. Plain text in a phone's notes app, screenshotted. And hooks lifted word for word from your own sales calls.

Put those together and you get a grid. Two types of buyer, five angles. Ten cells. Three formats per cell. Thirty ads, and you can film them in an afternoon. I'll draw the grid for a real service business in a minute.

Why does volume matter so much? Because results follow a power law. A few ads carry the whole account. You don't know in advance which ones. The only way to find them is to make enough.

#### [2:30–8:50] The lesson

[SCREEN: "$700K month: 96 new + 50 older = 146 ads". Source on screen.]

Start with Daniel's numbers. In one month he says they spent $700,000 on Meta. They ran 96 new videos and about 50 older ones. That's where his rule comes from: "at least one really good video per $5,000 of spend."

And look at how it split. He says three videos took 63% of that budget. The other 143 shared the rest. One more winner of that calibre, he reckons, is worth about a million dollars a month. That's the power law. You make a hundred ads to find three.

Now, honesty. He says different things at different times. His spend-tier table asks for only five to ten new ads a month at $10,000 to $50,000 of spend. In another video he says that under a million a month, creative testing barely matters. Read the 100-ad floor as his newer position, from 2026.

He also does the maths against himself. Say you spend $30,000 a month and spread it across 120 ads. That's about $250 each. If each ad needs three to five times your target to judge, you'll take more than a year to validate one batch. So 100 ads is a library you build up. It's not 100 ads you test at once.

My version, for a founder spending $10,000 to $20,000 a month: 30 ads to launch, 100 inside about 90 days. That's my adaptation, not his rule.

[SCREEN: "The creative is the targeting." 2026 course, 14:22.]

Why diversity, and not just more of the same? Daniel's explanation is that Meta now reads each ad, the words, the text on screen, the setting, and picks the audience from that. The creative is the targeting. Ten ads filmed the same day, saying the same thing, look like one ad to Meta. He gives an example. Swapping "home service business owner with under five employees" for the slang "one-truck Chuck" cut the cost per lead by about 20%. Your buyers' words do the targeting for you.

Angles. He says in that $700,000 month they ran eight: the guarantee, time savings, new media against old media, hiring an editor, views, leads and clients, scaling the business, and testimonials. Same offer. Eight reasons to want it. His line: "If an angle doesn't work with your audience, hundreds of variations of it won't work either."

Formats. In his breakdown of a million dollars of spend, founder talking heads had the highest return and a lower cost per qualified lead. Static images got the cheapest calls, about $424 each, but poor return. Cheap calls again. Keep a mix, but put the weight on you, talking.

Where the words come from. Daniel ran a contest where his own staff made ads. He says the top five came in at about $237 to $239 per qualified call, against a control of about $450. Fifty-three percent lower. His reason: sales reps and account managers already know how prospects talk. One positioning line from that world, "You hire us, then you fire us," closed a $30,000 paid-in-full deal in the first week.

Each ad follows the same four parts. Hook. Then the mechanism, as "First, next, then", because the biggest cold objection is "I can't picture how this works for me." Then one or two pieces of proof. Then one call to action. And edit lightly. He says that in a split test of about 100 videos, minimal editing beat heavy editing.
