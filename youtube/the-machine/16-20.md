**[0:45–2:30] The recipe, fast**

Three things.

One. Revenue per lead is cash collected divided by leads assigned. Not divided by calls. Not by shows. By leads. You paid for every lead, not just the ones who turned up. Daniel calls it the baseline metric every decision in his business is made from.

Two. Close rate lies. It can be defined five honest ways. It ignores the leads a rep never got onto a call. And it's easy to game. A rep who lets weak leads no-show, or quietly disqualifies them, gets a better close rate and costs you money.

Three. Turn the gap into dollars. Take your best rep's revenue per lead. Subtract this rep's. Multiply by this rep's leads. Daniel calls it operational drag. On his team, one rep's gap was worth about 150 to 185 thousand dollars a month. On yours it might be twenty thousand. Either way it's a number, not an opinion.

[SCREEN: "Drag = (best RPL − this rep's RPL) × this rep's leads"]

And one capacity rule, because tired closers drag the number down. Keep closers at 70 to 80% of their calendar, and no more than five calls in a row.

That's the lesson. Now the sheet, and a worked example.

*What Daniel does*

[SCREEN: quote card. "The baseline metric that we make all of our decisions off of is revenue per lead." Daniel Iles. Link on screen.]

Daniel Iles is a former accountant. When his company was half a million dollars in debt, he says he stopped trying to manage sales on psychology, which he didn't understand. In his words, he was "just going to moneyball the whole thing."

Every rep got a row in a shared sheet. About twenty columns. Leads allocated, calls booked, shows, show rate, qualified, deals, revenue, revenue per call, revenue per lead, commission. The leaderboard was sorted by one column. Revenue per lead.

He gives three reasons.

First, close rate can be measured too many ways. In one video he lists five for the same business: about 17% of qualified shows closed, 12% of shows, 8% of confirmed calls, 6.6% of all calls, and 1.8% of leads. Five honest answers. He points out that a Whole Foods cashier closes 99%.

Second, revenue per lead catches everything that happens before the call. Did the rep text the lead? Confirm the slot? Chase the no-show? His top rep had an 84% show rate when the team averaged 48%, because that rep talked to people before the call. Close rate never sees that work. Revenue per lead does.

Third, it puts a dollar figure on the gap. One rep on his team had nearly the highest revenue and the highest commission in his group. Most teams would call him the top performer. He was also taking almost double the leads at the lowest revenue per lead. Daniel put that drag at about 600 thousand dollars. Volume hid it.

[B-ROLL: spreadsheet rows sorting, the "top performer" row sliding down.]

Then he used the number to route leads. Best leads to the reps with the best revenue per lead, first. He says that change took revenue per lead from about $500 to $1,000.

On capacity: in a conversation on Daniel's channel, the sales operator Matt Ryder says calls six, seven and eight in a row closed at about 2%. So the rule is to cap closers at five back to back. And keep them at 70 to 80% of capacity. At 100%, reps cut corners. Daniel says that past a point, more ad spend stopped producing more revenue because the closers were the bottleneck.

*The version for a business your size*

You are probably not running twenty closers. Here's the version for a team of zero to three.

[SCREEN: Google Sheet. Columns appear one by one.]

Shows.

Deals.

Revenue per lead. Cash divided by leads assigned.

Then two more cells. The best revenue per lead on the team. And for each person, best minus theirs, times their leads. That's the drag.

Do it monthly, and also over a rolling 90 days, like Daniel's team. At small volume one big deal swings a month. If someone has fewer than about 20 leads, write the number down but don't act on it yet.

And compare like with like. If the best leads go to you and the new rep gets the leftovers, your numbers aren't comparable. Daniel's answer is lead tiers. Every lead gets a tier from the application, and each rep is compared with others on the same tier. That's episode 11.

*Worked example*

Round numbers. A consultancy selling a ten-thousand-dollar offer, doing about 150 thousand a month. The founder has just hired two closers, like we covered last episode. Each gets 100 leads a month.

Closer B. 100 leads. 30 show. 8 close. $80,000 collected. Close rate on shows: 27%. Revenue per lead: $800.

On close rate, B is the better closer. On the call itself, maybe B is. But 70 of B's 100 leads never turn up. So B makes $800 per lead, and A makes $1,000.

The drag. $1,000 minus $800 is $200. Times 100 leads. $20,000 a month. About $240,000 a year, from one seat.

What do you do with it? Not fire B. Find where the gap is. B's show rate is 30%. A's is 50%. That's a pre-call problem, not a closing problem. Daniel's fix for exactly this is to have the weaker rep copy the top rep's pre-call messages, which on his team are open for anyone to read. If B's show rate gets to 45% at the same close rate, B's revenue per lead goes to about $1,200. Now B is your best rep.
