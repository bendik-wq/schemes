**[0:45–2:30] The recipe, fast**

[SCREEN: three numbered lines appear as they're spoken.]

The whole lesson, in three points.

One. A referral business can't choose its month. Daniel had three businesses before this one, and each hit a ceiling he didn't control: a local market, his own face, a platform. His rule after that was simple. Never get serious about a business where you can't buy customers.

Two. Buying customers takes seven parts, in order, and each part feeds the next. An offer a stranger understands. Ads that find buyers, not clicks. A page that says what you do in the first three minutes. An application that puts a value on every lead. A show-up stack, so booked calls actually happen. A call that closes. And numbers that tell you which dollar made which sale.

Three. When one part is missing, the parts around it get blamed. Founders blame the ads. Daniel's rule is to suspect the offer first. And when the ads do work, the leak is usually somewhere between the click and the call.

So the job today is not to build seven things. It's to score yourself out of seven, find the first part you're missing, and fix that one.

**[3:00–9:00] The lesson**

[B-ROLL: notebook, pen drawing the seven boxes by hand.]

Start with how Daniel built it, because the order matters.

He didn't invent the model. He bought a $15,000 course that laid out a working business, ads to a call to a high-ticket programme, and he copied it. In month one he did about $120,000 on roughly $50,000 of ads, and he closed every call himself.

Then he nearly went broke. Revenue kept rising, but he couldn't see which sales came from ads and which came from his free content. The part he was missing was part seven, the numbers. More on that in a minute.

Once all seven worked, the machine did what he'd wanted from the start. In his words, he can put a million dollars into ads in a month and drive roughly four million in new business. You don't need those numbers. You need the shape.

So here are the seven parts, what Daniel says about each, and a test for whether you have it. One point for each pass. No half points.

[SCREEN: scorecard, seven rows, empty checkbox column. Each row fills as it's discussed.]

Part one, the offer. Daniel: "When everything feels broken, it's usually an offer problem." What sells to referrals often doesn't sell to strangers, because referrals already trust you. The test: one sentence a stranger understands, one price, and you've sold it at least once to someone who'd never heard of you.

Part two, ads. The trap here is cheap leads. Daniel says that out of every 100 "wins" his ads reported to Facebook, 93 weren't buyers. The test: you run paid traffic, and you judge it on cost per qualified call, not cost per lead.

Part three, the page. His rule: in the first few minutes, say what you do, why it's different, show proof, and ask for the action. And say who it's not for. The test: a cold visitor could tell you, after three minutes, what you sell and whether it's for them.

Part four, the application. Daniel puts a dollar value on every form answer. Things like revenue band, a business email address and how far out someone books each swung his revenue per call by 250 to 700 percent. The test: every answer on your form has a score, and the score decides who takes the call, or whether there's a call at all.

Part five, show-up. This is the quietest part and one of the most expensive to skip. In his data, prospects who opened the reminder email and replied to a text showed up and qualified about 42 percent of the time. Those who did neither: about 3 percent. The test: someone contacts every booking within five minutes, and reminders go out on more than one channel.

Part six, the call. Daniel left the phones around month four or five and says he should have stayed a full year. The test: you have a written call structure, and it was built from calls you took yourself.

Part seven, the numbers. Revenue per lead is ticket price times close rate. That's his master number. And he attributes every sale to its source, because blended numbers once hid that about 60 percent of his revenue was coming from organic content while he was scaling ads. The test: you can say, today, what a lead from each source is worth.

[SCREEN: a second scorecard, labelled "Illustration: made-up numbers".]

Now a worked example. These are round numbers I've made up to show the mechanics. Not a client.

A consultant sells a $10,000 engagement. Revenue is $60,000 a month, so six clients. Nearly all referrals. She closes about half her calls, because referrals arrive already trusting her.

Score her. Offer: a custom proposal every time. No. Ads: none. No. Page: her homepage lists four services. No. Application: a contact form. No. Show-up: she emails people back when she gets to it. No. Call: yes. She's good on the phone, and she built her own way of running it. Numbers: she knows revenue, not revenue per lead. No.

One out of seven. And that one point is the part that doesn't scale on its own, because it's her.

Here's why the score matters. Daniel's rule of thumb for a service is that the cost to win a client should be at most 30 percent of what that client is worth. At $10,000, that's $3,000 per client. Say cold calls close at 25 percent, half her referral rate, and half of booked calls show up. Then she can afford about $375 per booked call. Spend $10,000 at that price and she gets about 27 booked calls, about 13 that show, about three clients. Roughly $30,000 back. That's the machine working. Episode six is all about that sum.

But she can't get there by switching on ads. Her first zero is part one. Ads pointed at a custom-proposal offer is how founders decide that "ads don't work in my industry."

That's the general rule. Fix the first zero in the chain, then move right. Offer before ads. Ads before page tests. Application before you buy more leads. And numbers from day one, because they're the only part that tells you whether the others are working.

## Episode 02 · Your offer is the problem, not your ads

### Metadata

- **Title options**
  1. Your offer is the problem, not your ads
  2. Cold traffic won't buy what referrals buy

- **Thumbnail text:** IT'S THE OFFER
- **Runtime target:** 12–13 min (spoken script ≈ 1,740 words)
- **Machine part lit:** Part 1 of 7, the offer (first of three offer episodes)

### Script

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**

[SCREEN: two price cards side by side. One is crossed out.]

When ads don't work, the ads are rarely the problem.

Daniel Iles had two offers. He removed the cheaper one, and revenue per lead went up about 40 percent, straight away. Same ads. Same team. One less thing to buy.

**[0:20–0:45] The map**

[SCREEN: machine diagram. "Offer" lit. Caption: "Part 1 of 7".]

This is part one of seven: the offer. It's the first box on the left, which means everything else in the machine inherits its problems.

This is for service founders selling $3,000 or more on a call, roughly $30K to $300K a month, mostly from referrals. Under $3,000, the rules here don't hold.

**[0:45–2:30] The recipe, fast**

Three points.

One. Suspect the offer first. Referrals buy you. They've heard about you from someone they trust, so a vague offer is fine. A stranger on Facebook has no one vouching for you. They buy the thing, so the thing has to be clear.

Two. Make it a product. Daniel's test: if you can say what the client gets in three words or less, it can sell cold. "Work with me and I'll help you" can't. Then put everything on the table up front: one page, one price, one call to decide.

Three. One offer, one price, per audience. No menu on the call. A menu makes the buyer and your sales rep both hesitate, and hesitation doesn't buy.

That's the lesson. The rest of this video is the evidence, a way to test your own offer this week, and a worked example of rewriting one.

Where this comes from: I studied 90 of Daniel Iles' videos. I'm not connected to him, and his numbers are his, linked in the description.

**[3:00–9:00] The lesson**

[B-ROLL: scrolling a Facebook feed, thumb stopping on an ad, then scrolling past.]

First, how you know it's the offer.

Daniel lists the warning signs. You're under $100K a month and selling several offers. It doesn't sell on ads to cold traffic. You can only close in person. Or doubling sales would break delivery. His view is that one good offer in a big enough market should get you to $100K a month on its own.

He learned this the expensive way. He had an offer that converted well from his organic content. He put it on paid ads, and it didn't convert. He puts the cost at about $500,000 of debt and about six months to rebuild the offer from scratch.

His test is blunt: "Can you sell your product or service to a stranger on the street? If yes, you are going to crush it at paid ads."

[SCREEN: quote card, "Organic vs paid ads, 4:06".]

There's a numbers version of the same test. Look at where your revenue comes from. If about 90 percent comes from referrals and organic, your offer has only been proven warm. You don't yet know if it sells cold.

Second, what a cold offer looks like.

Daniel's split is "service" versus "product". A service is trust in a person. A product is clear enough to sell from a 30-second ad. Viral Coach sells cold traffic a short concrete thing, views, or "we post the videos for you". Warm traffic gets "work with Daniel's team". The delivery is identical. Only the words change.

His anatomy of a cold-traffic offer: easy to understand, he uses the phrase "a one-page contract". Risk reversal. Built to close on one call. And nothing hidden that appears later.

Third, don't invent one. In his words, "originality on cold traffic is extremely expensive." Model an offer that's already selling, from someone a few steps ahead of you. But check they're actually profitable. He admits he once copied coaches who turned out to be broke, leased Lamborghinis and all.

And pick buyers who already pay. Viral Coach first sold to coaches and consultants, and he says most of them couldn't afford it. When he switched to traditional businesses that already spend on marketing, revenue roughly doubled every month for six months. That's partly why our own floor is $30K a month and a $3,000 offer.

Fourth, one offer, one price.

Daniel sold two products, one about six times the price of the other. Prospects got confused and bought neither. Reps didn't know which to pitch. When he removed the cheaper one, revenue per lead went up about 40 percent immediately, and the next month the business went from about $1.4 million to $3 million.

The detail that matters: that cheap offer was about 80 percent of customers but only 20 percent of revenue. After the cut, the number of purchases barely moved. People just bought the main offer. His line: "Once we removed all that confusion and the customer wasn't confused about what they were buying, they just bought."

[SCREEN: "Simplicity scales at every level." — Daniel Iles]

Last, how the offer gets better. Daniel's rule: "Every part of your offer must overcome the objections you hear during the sale." The founder takes the calls, writes down every objection, and changes the offer so it doesn't come up again.

[SCREEN: "Illustration: made-up numbers".]

Now the version for a service business, with a worked example. Round numbers, invented to show the mechanics.

An agency does $90K a month, almost all referrals. On calls, the founder offers three things: a $3,000 audit, a $15,000 build, and "a custom retainer, we'll scope it together".

Rewrite step one: the three-word deliverable. What do clients actually walk away with? Say it's an outbound system that books sales calls. Three words: "booked sales calls."

Step two: one sentence a stranger gets. "We build and run the outbound system that books B2B software founders qualified sales calls, live in 45 days." Who, what, when. No adjectives. Read it to someone outside your industry and ask them to say it back. If they can't, it's not done.

Step three: one price. Here's the maths that scares founders. Say 100 calls a month. Ten people buy the $3,000 audit, four buy the $15,000 build. That's $30,000 plus $60,000. $90,000 from 14 clients.

Now drop the audit. The $30,000 you lose is exactly two build clients. So the whole bet is this: of the ten people who bought the cheap option, do two of them buy the real one when it's the only option? In Daniel's data the purchase count barely moved. Yours may differ. But if two step up, you make the same money with six clients instead of 14. That's eight fewer clients to deliver to.

Step four: test it before you commit. Daniel's method: have one rep, or one stretch of the month, pitch only the single offer, and compare revenue per lead against the rest. If you're the only closer, do your next 20 calls single-offer and compare them with your last 20.

The people who wanted the audit don't vanish. You route them away before the call, to free content or a checklist, instead of selling them something small on it. That's part four of the machine, and it gets its own episode.

**[0:45–2:30] The recipe, fast**

Three points.

One. Judge ads on revenue, not on cheap leads. Cost per lead and cost per booked call are easy to read and easy to fool you. Judge them on cost per qualified call and return on ad spend.

Two. Only tell Meta about the events you want more of. If you report every booked call as a win, Meta finds you more people who book calls. Many of those are people with free time, not money. Send a positive signal only when a lead qualifies.

Three. Move budget by results per ad, on a fixed schedule. One table: spend, booked calls, qualified calls, sales and revenue for each ad. Feed the ads that make money. Starve the ones that only make calls.

Meta decides who sees the ad. You decide what it learns.

All of this comes from Daniel Iles, from the 90 of his videos I studied. I'm not affiliated, and his numbers are his, linked below.

**[3:00–9:00] The lesson**

[B-ROLL: a bank statement, figures blurred. A credit card on a desk.]

Two stories from Daniel first, because they're the reason this rule exists.

The first is early. His topline went from about $120,000 to $350,000, $500,000, then about $700,000 by month five. It looked like growth. But his cost to get a customer was about 150 percent of revenue. He says cost per call held flat as he raised spend, and revenue didn't follow. He ended up with about half a million dollars of credit card debt and, in his words, $400 in the bank.

The second is years later, at scale. A new campaign cut his cost per qualified call from about $400 to about $100. Volume went up four times. And the calendar filled with leads who were broke or didn't show. In one telling he lost about $500,000 of revenue that week. In another, about a million. Either way, the cheaper calls cost him more than they saved.

His summary: "The cheaper the call, the worse the quality."

[SCREEN: quote card. "Out of every 100 wins that we report to Facebook, 93% of them were wrong."]

Why does this happen? Daniel's explanation is that lead generation breaks Meta's "trust the algorithm" advice in three ways.

There are few data points. A thousand dollars might buy one or two qualified calls, where an online shop gets twenty purchases.

The data arrives late. The sale happens days or weeks after the booking.

And the signal is wrong. If you close about 7 percent of booked calls, which is roughly what he does, then 93 percent of the "wins" Meta learned from weren't buyers. He adds a detail I find useful: optimising for booked calls skews toward people with free time. Busy, high-value prospects guard their calendars.

So, his fixes. There are three.

Fix one. Every outcome of the application form lands on its own thank-you page, with its own tracking. Only the qualified page tells Meta "this is a win". His gates are things like revenue, recent marketing spend, how much a client would be worth, and whether they're ready to start now. In his words, "we're going to tell them to get us the rich kind."

Fix two. Before you scale into cheaper leads, make sure the filter can take it. Qualifying questions, some friction on the form, a clear way to disqualify. His cheap-call disaster happened because there wasn't enough of that.

Fix three. Judge each ad on money. He merges three sources: the Meta export, with spend and calls per ad. The CRM, with qualified calls per ad. And sales and revenue per ad. One table. Then he moves budget by return on ad spend. An ad at nine times return goes up, from $3,000 to $4,000 a day. One at five times comes down a little. One at 2.7 times gets cut to $500 or switched off, even though its calls are cheap.

Two cautions from him. Never scale on one sale. Wait for the second or third. And at low spend, lean on qualified calls, because there are roughly three to five times more of them than sales, so you get enough data to act on.

His line that sums it up: "Facebook controls the number of calls... But you control the quality of the appointments."

Now the version for a service business spending, say, $10,000 a month. A worked example with invented round numbers. The offer is $10,000.

Now report only qualified calls. A shows ten wins at $500 each. B shows twelve at about $417. Meta now leans toward B, and so do you.

At this spend, one sale either way is noise, which is why you'd also watch qualified calls. B wins on both.

Two: your application scores each lead, part four of the machine, and qualified leads go to their own thank-you page. A server-side event doesn't depend on the visitor's browser. Four: after each call, mark it qualified or not in your CRM, and add the sale and its value when it closes. Five: once a week, build the table and move budget.

None of this needs a big budget. It needs the form, the tags and the habit.
