### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

A business you buy comes with an enquiry flow nobody has measured.

A clinic or a trade is a great business. Steady work. People need you. Until the phone rings more than anyone can answer. Then it goes from booked out to busy, and somehow not growing.

In a clinic doing 100 enquiries a month, ten more bookings is about $72,000 a year. Same enquiries. No new ads. I'll walk one enquiry through the business, in the order the customer sees it, and show you where the money leaks.

> **On screen:** 100 enquiries · 40 booked · where do the other 60 go? (illustrative)

**STAKES · 0:20**

When you buy at 4× profit, you pay for the bookings the business already makes. You don't pay for the ones it loses. That gap is upside the seller isn't pricing, and you capture all of it. This is for owners and buyers of service businesses: clinics, trades, local services. It isn't for a business that's already at capacity. Fix capacity first. That was episode 26.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

Speed to lead means one thing: every enquiry gets a human reply in minutes, not hours, on every channel it came in on.

There are four stops on the way from first ring to booked job. The door, the first five minutes, the hours, and the chase.

If a business is slow to answer, it has nothing to do with lazy staff, or a bad receptionist, or impatient customers. It has everything to do with one thing. Nobody owns the number.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners buy businesses with little to no money down, then plug exactly this kind of leak. If you want that for yours, book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · The door · 1:40**

Map every way a customer can reach the business. Phone, website form, email, Google messages, Facebook, Instagram, the after-hours voicemail. Next to each, write who answers it and how fast.

Most small businesses have at least one door nobody owns. Then mystery-shop it. Call at 7:30 on a Tuesday night. Fill in the form on a Sunday. Message the Google profile. Time every reply.

If you're buying, do this before you make the offer. It costs nothing and the seller never knows. Then in due diligence ask for the enquiry numbers: how many came in, from where, and how many booked. If the seller can't answer, you've found upside, and you've found a business where nobody watches the front door.

The rule: every door has a name next to it. No name, no door. Shut it, or give it to someone.

**2 · The first five minutes · 2:40**

Three touches. An automatic text the second the form lands, so they know a human saw it. An email with what happens next. Then a real phone call.

Give it one owner. A boring business already has a receptionist, an office manager, or the owner's partner doing the books. Give one of them first response and one number: minutes to first contact. Speed you don't measure drifts back to "I'll call them after this job".

Most owners think growth means more leads. More ads, a new website, more posts. That's not how it works. If you're reaching half the people who already put their hand up, buying more just pours more water into a leaking bucket. Answer what you've got. Then buy more. Episode 54 is the buying.

**3 · The hours · 3:50**

Enquiries arrive when customers are free. Lunch, evenings, Sunday night. Not when the office is open. In a trade it's worse. The person who should answer is on a roof.

So move the hours, not the headcount. An office person on 11 to 7 instead of 8 to 4. An after-hours answering service. In a clinic, someone clearing online booking requests in the evening, because that's when patients sit down and search.

Pull a month of enquiry times and count them by hour. The answer is usually obvious. If a third of enquiries land after 5pm and nobody answers until 9am, that third is going to whoever picks up first.

Speed doesn't mean ringing someone at 9pm. Calling-hour and privacy rules exist. Build them into the system.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice.

**4 · The chase · 4:40**

Some enquire and don't book. Chase on a fixed rhythm: the same day, then once a day for a few days, then a monthly check-in. Cancels and no-shows get the same. Every message carries a link to rebook, because without one people cancel and never come back.

Don't overdo it. A 70-year-old physio patient doesn't want eight emails. One text, one reminder, one call. A customer who replies "yep, see you Thursday" turns up. One who goes silent needs a call.

Then log it. Every enquiry: time in, time of first reply, source, booked or not. Four columns in a spreadsheet. After a month you know your real booking rate, and after three months you have a number the next buyer can check.

**HOOK · 0:00**

Most quotes don't lose on price. They die in silence.

How come a business with good reviews, fair prices and a solid team still loses jobs it should win? The quote goes out, and nobody calls again. The job goes to whoever followed up.

In a trade sending 100 quotes a month, five more wins is about $40,000 a month of revenue, from quotes you've already paid to produce. Here are the five mistakes that kill them, worst last.

> **On screen:** 100 quotes · 25 → 30 wins · × $8,000 (illustrative)

**STAKES · 0:20**

In a lot of small businesses the owner is the sales process. He knows who to ring back and what to say. When he retires, it walks out with him, and your profit drops in year one while you're paying him. This is for owners who've hit the ceiling of what they can sell themselves, and buyers taking over from a founder. It isn't for anyone who wants pressure tactics. They don't work on a 60-year-old getting a quote for a new roof.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

A sales process is the written path from enquiry to deposit, simple enough that someone other than the owner can run it.

Five mistakes break it. If quotes are dying, it has nothing to do with your price, your workmanship or "customers these days". It has everything to do with nobody ever writing it down.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners buy businesses with little to no money down, then build this so the business doesn't hang on one person. Book a call below if that's you. Now the detail.

**Mistake 1 · The process lives in one head · 1:40**

You were told good work sells itself. You were told selling is pushy. You were told if they want it, they'll call. None of that's true. The businesses that win more jobs aren't better tradespeople. They ask better questions, make one clear recommendation and ring back.

So write the stages on one page: enquiry, triage, visit, quote, follow-up, deposit. But sell it yourself first. As the new owner, go on 20 quote visits or sit in on 20 consults. Write down every question, objection and reason someone said no. Then write the page.

The page is short on purpose. If it runs past one page, nobody reads it, and the team goes back to doing it their own way.

Saul, an Owners Academy member, is in early due diligence on an electrical and aircon business: 25 staff, about $3.8 million in revenue, owner retiring in two years. In a business like that, one of the first things I'd ask is who wins the work, and whether it's written down anywhere.

> **On screen:** Saul (Owners Academy member): NDA signed, early DD · electrical/aircon · 25 staff · ~$3.8M revenue.

**Mistake 2 · Pitching instead of asking · 3:00**

Five questions do most of the work. At the kitchen bench, for a ducted aircon quote:

- "What made you call us now?" Broad gets you the real reason.
- "Is it just you deciding?" Partner, landlord, body corporate. Book the visit when both partners are home and "I need to talk to my wife" goes away.
- "What's it costing you?" The power bill. The bedroom nobody sleeps in over summer.
- "Have you had anyone else out?" Either they haven't, or something didn't sit right.
- "When do you need it done?" Last. Asked early, it sounds like pressure.

The customer does most of the talking. By the time you quote, they've told you why they need it, what it's costing them and who signs off. The quote just confirms what they already said. The rule: no price until you've heard the problem in their words.

**Mistake 3 · Leaving without a decision · 4:10**

You're the expert. Say what you'd do, give the price plainly, ask for the go-ahead. No fake urgency. If they say yes, take the signature and the deposit there and then, like we set up in episode 50. More jobs are lost to "I'll sign it tonight" than are won in follow-up.

In a clinic the same bones look different. A physio doesn't sell, they recommend care. But the next appointment gets booked before the patient leaves the room. A lot of clinics lose patients after visit one simply because nobody booked visit two.

**[JOSH: optional, one sentence on how your clinic books the next appointment]**

**Mistake 4 · "Let us know" follow-up · 5:00**

Follow-up is planned, for a month, and tied to the objection. "Did you get a chance to talk to your partner?" "We've got finance options now." Any sign of interest, you ring. You don't start an email thread.

And every lost job gets one reason: budget, authority, need or timing. Once a month, fix the biggest one.

**Mistake 5 · Too many options · 5:40**

This one's counterintuitive. Good, better, best feels helpful. Often it hands the customer a reason to go home and think. One recommended option gets a yes or a no.

I wouldn't rip it out on day one. Test it. One estimator quotes a single recommendation for a month. Compare win rate and average job value against the rest.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Your first salesperson can break the deal before they sell a thing.

There are two types of owners. The ones who still quote every job at night, and the ones who hired someone and paid them for cash in the bank.

On a business making $250,000 a year and paying the seller about $166,000, there's $84,000 of room. A $90,000 salary eats all of it. Here's salary versus pay on cash collected, a test for when to hire, and my verdict.

> **On screen:** $250K profit · $166K to the seller · $84K buffer · $90K salary (illustrative)

**STAKES · 0:20**

In a vendor-financed deal, the cash flow that pays a new hire is the same cash flow paying the seller. The 1.5 DSCR rule applies every month, not just at settlement. Get this hire wrong and you're late on the seller in month six. Get it right and you stop being the business's only salesperson, which is what holds its multiple down. This is for owners whose quote calendar is full. It isn't for someone hoping a hire fixes a business that can't deliver.

> **On screen:** DSCR = free cash flow ÷ debt service. Keep it at 1.5 or above. Education, not financial or legal advice.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

Pay for cash in the bank, not for turning up.

Two options. Option A, a salary. Option B, a smaller base plus commission on cash collected, taken back if the job is refunded. First, a self-test for whether you're ready to hire at all.

If your last hire didn't work, it had nothing to do with being a bad judge of people, or "nobody wants to work". It had everything to do with the setup: no target, no trial, and pay that didn't depend on results.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners buy businesses with little to no money down, then make them run without the owner doing everything, sales included. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · The self-test · 1:40**

Three questions. Have you, or a manager, sold it yourselves and written it down? That was episode 52. Are there more quote requests than you can get to? And do you know what a good month looks like in dollars?

Three yeses, you're ready. Any no, you're not. Without selling it yourself, you can't set a target, you can't judge a trial, and you can't know what to pay.

**2 · Option A: salary · 2:30**

A salary is a fixed cost. You pay it in the month they sell nothing, and month one is usually that month. On our illustrative deal, cover goes from 1.5 to below 1.0 before the first job comes in. That's a deal paying the seller out of your savings.

Salary does buy one thing: loyalty from someone who won't take a commission-heavy role. If you go this way, size the debt for it in the deal model, before you settle.

**3 · Option B: base plus cash collected · 3:20**

A smaller base, plus commission paid when the cash lands. Part when the deposit clears, the rest when the final invoice is paid. If the job's cancelled and refunded, the commission comes back.

Use tiers, so the more they sell the better the rate. A flat amount per job lets people hit a comfortable number and coast. And never change the plan mid-stream. Your best sellers leave first.

Before I knew this, I'd have assumed you need cash to attract good people. At 25 I had none. I built my board by offering founders' equity instead of fees. Good people took upside over a fixed cheque. Pay that grows with results attracts people who expect results.

**4 · Who, and the trial · 4:30**

Hire someone who's already sold in your trade or a close one. Don't hire hopefuls you have to train. Find them like customers: ask your suppliers, your accountant, your best tradespeople. Hiring from a competitor is fine within the law. Hire the skill, never the customer list.

Everyone sounds great in an interview. So the trial is real work: the first 30 quotes, with a pass mark set from your own numbers. Judge them on revenue per enquiry, not close rate. Close rate is easy to game by only chasing the easy ones.

In a clinic, be careful. I wouldn't put a front-desk coordinator on commission for treatment sold. Measure that role on speed to lead, bookings and shows.

> **On screen:** Check employment law and your profession's rules before you set pay. Education, not financial or legal advice.

## EP 54 · Every level of ads for a boring business, from $0 to a full calendar

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | A customer worth $4,000 of lifetime gross profit justifies up to $1,000 to win, which works back to $125 per enquiry; above that, ads lose money (illustrative). |
| **Hook** | "The cheapest lead is often the most expensive one you'll buy." |
| **For** | Corporate Prisoner |
| **Stage** | Grow · Season 2, Act 11 |
| **Runtime** | ~8 min |
| **Copy** | Shape A (ladder of levels) · Saturated Mechanism opener · The Market Has Moved · No Choice + Identity Close + After Close |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

The cheapest lead is often the most expensive one you'll buy.

Every day someone's selling small businesses cheaper leads. "Leads for $10." "We'll fill your calendar." Then the calendar fills with people who don't show or can't pay.

In an aircon business, I'd pay up to $1,000 to win a customer worth $10,000, and not a dollar more. Here's every level of ads for a boring business, and the number that tells you when to stop.

> **On screen:** Customer worth $10,000 · max $1,000 to win one (illustrative)

**STAKES · 0:20**

A business that can buy customers at a known cost has upside most sellers don't price. And one that can show cost per job and revenue by channel is a better story for a bank and for the next buyer. This is for people buying their first business, or who just have, and want growth they control. It isn't for anyone who wants to boost a post and hope, or a business that can't handle more work yet.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

Price your ads from what a customer is worth, then measure every dollar to cash.

Five levels. Each one has to work before you climb to the next. If ads have burned you before, it had nothing to do with your industry or your budget. It had everything to do with being sold the wrong number. Somebody measured leads. Nobody measured jobs.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people buy profitable, boring businesses with little to no money down, then build growth they control. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Level 1 · Strangers buy · 1:40**

Before a dollar on ads: when a stranger calls, do you win the job? If you only win work from mates of existing customers, ads will only make that louder. When ads fail, the cause is usually the offer, not the ad. Fix the offer first. And fix speed to lead from episode 51, or you'll pay for leads that ring out.

**Level 2 · The ceiling · 2:20**

Work out the most you'll pay to win one customer, before you spend. I work it off gross profit, not revenue, because in a trade materials and labour eat a big chunk of every job.

Lifetime value, times gross margin, gives lifetime gross profit. I cap acquisition cost at about a quarter of that. Then work backwards through your win rate on quotes and your booking rate on enquiries. That gives you a maximum cost per enquiry, and anyone selling you leads has to beat it.

**Level 3 · Search, for urgent jobs · 3:20**

A blocked drain or a dead aircon in January, people search. They're comparing two or three firms right now. Google search ads and your Google Business profile matter here. You're paying for intent, and you're competing on speed and reviews. Episode 55.

**Level 4 · Meta, for planned jobs · 3:50**

A new kitchen, solar, a ducted system. People often don't know they're in the market yet. That's where Facebook and Instagram work, in a radius around the business.

And the market has moved. On Meta now, the ad itself does most of the targeting. The platform reads what's in it to decide who sees it. Fiddling with interests matters less. So the ad should sound like your customers. Name the suburb. Show the van. Show the actual team. Say what the job is, never who the person is. No "struggling with…" call-outs. In health, advertising rules are stricter again.
