**HOOK · 0:00**

How come people with a good salary, good credit and real savings so often never buy a business?

My first deal had no deposit. A physio clinic, 100% vendor finance. What it did take was about a thousand cold calls, and four deals that fell over first.

So money ranks last. Time and energy decide it. Here's how to score all three, and what each one is worth in a deal.

The structure carries the price. A retiring owner gets paid over time out of the business's profit, so the purchase price doesn't come out of your account. What nobody can finance is the work that finds the deal. Capital with no hours means you're still reading about this next year. Hours with no capital means slower, not shut out. This is for people who'll give it 40 hours a week. Not for anyone looking for a way to do it in four.

**THE FRAME · 1:05**

One line: you can borrow the money. You can't borrow the hours.

Three inputs, ranked from least to most important. Money. Time. Energy.

If you've been "working on this" for a year with no offer out, it has nothing to do with your intelligence, your background or your bank balance. It has everything to do with nobody showing you which input actually moves a deal.

> **On screen:** You can borrow the money. You can't borrow the hours.

**1 · Money: it buys speed, not permission · 1:50**

You were told you need a deposit. You were told you need a track record. You were told the bank decides.

None of that was true for my first deal. Seven years at 4%. A nine-month payment holiday. Rent in arrears. No deposit.

> **On screen:** Results not typical; records on file. Education, not financial or legal advice.

So the structure buys the business. Money buys three other things. Speed: with cash, an existing business or property behind you, a first company in the next 30 days becomes possible. Room for mistakes, because deal one will have some. And runway, so you can live while the business pays its debt first.

The rule: the money question isn't "do I have a deposit?" It's "how many months can I live without drawing much from the first business?" Write that number down.

**2 · Time: income hours, not busy hours · 2:45**

Time means hours you can name on a calendar from Monday. 40 at the very least. 60 is better. 100 is ideal while you're building.

Most people who say they're giving this 20 hours are giving it 20 hours of reading. Podcasts. Spreadsheets. Another video. Like this one.

Income hours put you in front of a seller, a board member or a lender. Calls, offers, lender interviews. Everything else is support.

An illustrative 40-hour week: 15 hours on calls, 10 on board outreach and lenders, 10 on financials, NDAs and offers, 5 on everything else.

My thousand calls was time. There's no shortcut through it. You can only get through it faster. If the hours aren't on the calendar, they aren't committed.

**3 · Energy: the input that kills more deals than money · 3:35**

Energy is where your attention actually goes. Nobody measures it.

I've had people pay for advisory and still not take action. Paid, signed up, then nothing.

Low energy looks like three side projects. A plan to start "after this quarter." A spouse who hasn't been asked. Another book before the first call. Every hour on a side project is an hour not on the phone.

The rule: one thing. If this isn't the thing, the hours on the calendar won't survive the first hard week.

**4 · The audit: score yourself 1 to 3 · 4:10**

Time. One is "when I can." Two is ten to twenty named hours. Three is 40-plus, on the calendar, from Monday.

Money. One is nothing. Two is some savings. Three is cash, a business or property you could put to work.

Energy. One is "still deciding." Two is "doing it, alongside three other things." Three is "this is the thing."

A one on money is fine. A one on time or energy isn't. I'd rather work with a 3-1-3 than a 3-3-1. The second person has the money and will still be watching videos next year.

Fix them in order. Time first, because hours are the only thing you control this week. Energy follows, because the calls start giving you real conversations. Then the money question answers itself, because you're looking at one business's numbers instead of a fear.

> **On screen:** Money can be a 1. Time and energy can't.

## EP 97 · 3 negotiation mistakes that turn a $2M deal into $2.5M

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | On a business making $300K, a 1.5× walk line caps the price at ~$2M over ten years; holding it keeps $500K off the price, or gets the seller $2.5M over 12.5 years at the same yearly cost (illustrative). |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Two buyers look at the same business, making $300,000 profit.

James has the cash and one deal he's fallen for. The other buyer has five conversations, a number written down, and a board who'll tell him no. The seller asks both for $2.5 million. James pays it. The other buyer says "No worries. Good luck." And ends up with the better deal.

Here are the three mistakes that cost James half a million.

> **On screen:** Illustrative. Same business, $300K profit. $2.0M vs $2.5M.

**STAKES · 0:30**

On this business, $500,000 of price is the difference between the business covering its repayments 1.5 times and 1.2 times. At 1.5 a slow year is uncomfortable. At 1.2 you're borrowing to pay the seller. This is for people sending offers, or about to, especially with capital and a fear of overpaying. It isn't for anyone who thinks negotiating means being nice until the seller gives in.

**THE FRAME · 1:00**

Walk-away power is the ability to say "no worries, good luck" and mean it. It isn't a tactic on the day. It's a position you build before the meeting.

Three mistakes destroy it, ranked. The last one is the one most people get backwards.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:20**

I help people find, structure and negotiate a first business, bought with little to no money down. If you want someone beside you when a seller pushes, book a call below. Now the mistakes.

**MISTAKE 1 · One deal in the pipeline · 1:35**

If you've ever overpaid, or nearly did, it has nothing to do with being soft or bad with numbers. It has everything to do with having one deal.

You've spent three months on it. You've told your partner. You've pictured the first day. The seller asks for more, and you hear yourself say "I'll see what I can do."

A buyer with five live deals can leave, and sellers can tell. That's why you run parallel pipelines and never single-thread a deal. My friend Elliot in the US sent about 40 offers to get three signed LOIs, all at 100% seller finance.

> **On screen:** Elliot, Josh's friend. Results not typical; records on file.

**[JOSH: one deal you walked away from: what the seller asked, what you said, what happened next.]**

The fix: never fewer than three live conversations. If you have one, make 50 calls this week.

Walk-away power comes in an order, and none of it happens on the day. First a pipeline. Then, once you have deals, a number. Then a team, a board and advisers who'll say "this one's not it" when you're too close. And once you have all that, a runway. If you need this deal to pay next month's rent, you can't walk.

**MISTAKE 2 · Walking in without a number · 2:50**

Most people think negotiating is a personality. Hard men win, nice people get taken.

That's not how it works. The buyer who wins did the maths before the meeting.

The business makes $300,000. Free cash flow has to cover repayments at least 1.5 times. $300,000 divided by 1.5 is $200,000 a year to the seller. Over ten years, about $2 million. Interest left out to keep it simple.

The seller says $2.5 million. Same ten years, that's $250,000 a year, and your cover drops to 1.2.

So $2 million over ten years is your walk line. You don't think in the room. You check the number.

> **On screen:** $300K ÷ 1.5 = $200K/yr × 10 = $2M. Illustrative. Education, not financial or legal advice.

When you hear "I've got other buyers interested," the answer is the same whether it's true or a bluff: "That's great, you should talk to them. My offer's here if it doesn't work out." If they have a better buyer, you were never getting it. If they don't, they call back. And to stop a deal drifting for months: "This offer's open for 24 hours."

**MISTAKE 3 · Moving your line instead of the terms · 4:05**

This is the counterintuitive one. You can say yes to $2.5 million.

When a seller wants more, change the shape, not your line. A longer term. A later start to repayments. Part of the price tied to performance.

$2.5 million over 12 and a half years is $200,000 a year. Your cover is back to 1.5. The seller gets the headline number. You keep a business that carries its debt.

Remember what the seller is really comparing you to. Not another buyer. Their own Monday morning. Another three years running the place, or a bank that takes 16 weeks. Never say "vendor finance" to them. Say "getting paid over time."

And if no shape works, you walk. That's what a line is for.

"But if I walk, I lose the deal." Sometimes. A deal you lose by holding 1.5 times is usually a deal that would have hurt you. "The seller will be offended." Not if you're calm. A seller handing over their life's work wants a buyer who knows their number.

> **On screen:** Longer · later · slower. Move the terms, not the line.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:20**

| Illustrative, $300K profit, interest excluded | Seller's ask | Your line | Ask, reshaped |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | $2.5M | $2.0M | $2.5M |
| Term | 10 yrs | 10 yrs | 12.5 yrs |
| Paid to seller a year | $250K | $200K | $200K |
| Cover (profit ÷ repayments) | 1.2× | 1.5× | 1.5× |

Column one is how capital gets lost. Columns two and three are the same deal for you, and only one of them gets the seller to yes.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 5:30**

- Moving the line $100,000 at a time because you're three months in and tired. Two moves and you're at 1.2×.
- Chasing after "other buyers." It tells the seller you have nowhere else to go.
- Letting an offer sit open for months. The seller shops it, and you've stopped calling.
- Needing the deal to pay next month's rent. No runway, no walk-away power.

**CLOSE · 6:00**

You can keep doing it the way most buyers do. One deal at a time, falling for it, the only person in the room who's emotional. Or you can have someone outside the deal who's seen the shape before and says "walk" when you can't.

If you're someone with capital who refuses to overpay, and you'd rather lose a deal than sign a bad one, you've already decided. Thirty days from now you'll be 30 days older either way. Or 30 days older with a written walk line and three live conversations.

## EP 98 · Every stage from 1 business to a 5-business group (year 0 to year 10)

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Owning one business is a phenomenal thing. You set the hours. You keep the profit.

Until it can't grow past you. Then it goes from an asset to a very well-paid job.

A business making $500,000 is worth about $2 million at four times profit. Five of them, run as one clean group, can be worth $20 million to a bigger buyer. Here's every stage between the two.

> **On screen:** Illustrative. 1 × $500K → 5 × $500K. Not a forecast or a promise.

**STAKES · 0:30**

The gap between four times and eight times is where the money in this whole model is made. It's called multiple arbitrage: small businesses sell cheap, groups sell dear. But you only get the higher multiple if the group can run, borrow and be sold without you. This is for owners and buyers who want a group: a roll-up, a legacy, something a bigger buyer wants. If you want one small business forever, this is more plan than you need.

**THE FRAME · 1:05**

One line: the plan runs in stages, not years, and each stage has one constraint.

Five stages. Get ready. Buy one. Buy more. Build the group. Sell or hold. Your job changes at every one. Most people get stuck because they keep doing the last stage's job.

> **On screen:** Get ready → Buy one → Buy more → Build the group → Sell or hold

**SOFT CTA · ~1:25**

I help people build this, starting with a first business bought with little to no money down and structured so it can be the base of a group. Book a call below. Now the stages.

**STAGE 0 · Get ready (about six months) · 1:40**

Build a board of credible operators, paid in founders' equity, 2 to 10% each. Line up lawyers and accountants paid at closing. Interview lenders before you need one. Start the calls.

Your job: caller. The constraint: volume. Every hour is calls, board outreach or lenders.

**STAGE 1 · Buy one (year 1) · 2:00**

The first deal is usually the hardest. Mine took about a thousand calls and four deals that fell over. Pick a business that covers its debt 1.5 times and that you could build on. Asset-rich, boring, bankable.

Your job switches to owner overnight. Keep the staff, keep the customers, keep the seller close for the handover. Then fix price and margin, then a way to bring in customers on purpose.

The constraint is you. Until there's a general manager, you own a job.

**STAGE 2 · Buy more (years 2 to 3) · 2:35**

If you own one business and you're stuck, it has nothing to do with your work ethic, your industry or your age. It has everything to do with nobody showing you that business two is bought differently from business one.

Business two is easier. You have a track record. You have a lender who's watched you make repayments. You have cash flow.

My friend Cam did two deals in 23 days. Both 100% vendor finance, interest-only for ten years with balloons in years three and ten, and $500,000 of working capital from the bank. Not because Cam had advantages you don't. Because he knew the structure.

> **On screen:** Cam, Josh's friend. Results not typical; records on file. Education, not financial or legal advice.

Your job: buyer and manager. Hire a GM for business one so you can source business two. Set up a holding company so each business sits in its own entity. Give minority holders no veto and put a drag-along in from the start, so the group can be sold cleanly.

The constraint is management. Not money. People who can run a site without you.

**STAGE 3 · Build the group (years 4 to 6) · 3:45**

Finance, HR and marketing get done once for every business instead of five times. You add smaller bolt-ons that slot in. Maybe a competitor.

Your job: manager of managers. Monday numbers. Board meetings. Few decisions, made well.

The constraint is cleanliness. A year spent on clean books and businesses that run without their old owners can add more value than another acquisition, because that's exactly what a buyer checks.

**STAGE 4 · Sell or hold (years 7 to 10) · 4:15**

Sell, and you collect the multiple gap. My friend Jason Nagy did the real version: a $5.5 million radiology business with nothing down, sold to private equity for $200 million seven years later.

> **On screen:** Jason Nagy, Josh's friend. Results not typical; records on file.

Or hold. Let the debt get paid down and take the cash flow. Both are fine. Next episode is about choosing on purpose.

The rule behind the whole ladder: one constraint per stage. Volume, then you, then management, then cleanliness, then the decision. Fix the one in front of you and leave the rest. And the test for where you are: if you stopped working for a month, what would happen to the profit? If it falls, you're still on stage 1, however many businesses you own. If it holds, you're ready for the next one.

"Ten years is too long." Think in stages. Getting ready takes months, and nothing says you have to sell at ten. "I can't picture running five businesses." You won't. You'll run the people who run them.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:25**

| Illustrative, debt not counted | |
|---|---|
| Businesses | 5 |
| Profit each | $500K |
| Group profit | $2.5M |
| Bought at ~4× | ~$10M, mostly paid over time |
| Group sold at 8× | ~$20M |
| Multiple gap | ~$10M |

The profit didn't change between row three and row five. The multiple did, because the group can run without you. That's what stages 2 and 3 are buying.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 5:40**

- Buying business two before business one runs without you. Both go backwards, and both lenders notice.
- Structuring deal one in a way the group can't use. Minority holders with a veto can block the sale that pays for ten years of work.
- Staying in the caller's job after you own. The business owns you, and the multiple stays at four.
- Messy books at sale. A buyer discounts what they can't verify.

**CLOSE · 6:10**

Two paths to year ten. Alone, every stage has a playlist in this series. And alone, people lose years: they buy too early, structure deal one for one deal, and stay in the wrong job. The other path is with someone thinking about year ten while you're on year one.

If you're someone who owns one business, knows it's capped, and would rather own the group than be the job, you've already decided. Thirty days from now you'll be 30 days older either way. Or 30 days older with a GM shortlist and a second target on paper.

## EP 99 · Jackpot vs legacy vs flip: the same $2M business bought three ways

| | |

| **The money** | The same $500K-profit business bought for $2M can be a $2.8–3.5M flip in three years, $500K a year held for life, or one piece of a group sold at 8× (illustrative). |

**HOOK · 0:00**

There are only two types of business owners. The ones who decided what the business was for before they bought it, and the ones who find out when they try to leave.

> **On screen:** JACKPOT · LEGACY · FLIP. Illustrative.

**STAKES · 0:35**

The goal sets what you buy, how you finance it, who you hire and when you sell. Get it wrong and the structure fights you: a flip with debt nobody can take over, a legacy business with repayments that eat the income you planned to live on. This is for owners and buyers after a big outcome. It isn't for the lifestyle business: one place, a few hours a week. Nothing wrong with it. It's just not what we do.

**THE FRAME · 1:10**

One line: pick the exit first, then build the deal backwards from it.

Three options. Jackpot, legacy, flip. Then a three-question test, and the verdict.

If you own a business and feel trapped in it, it has nothing to do with how hard you've worked or how good the business is. It has everything to do with nobody asking you what it was for.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:35**

On a call with me, your goal is the first question, because the structure follows it. Book below. Now the three options.

**OPTION A · Jackpot: buy many, sell the group · 1:45**

Here's what's changed. A generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. They don't want to wait 16 weeks for a bank. They want out and they want to be looked after. Getting paid over time can pay them above market, six to seven times against the usual three to five, and close in two to four weeks.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice.

That's why one buyer can now line up several businesses in one industry. Buy at small-business multiples, combine, clean up, sell the group at the multiple a bigger buyer pays. My friend Jason Nagy's first deal was a $5.5 million radiology business, zero down, on a 60/40 structure. Private equity exit at $200 million seven years later.

> **On screen:** Jason Nagy, Josh's friend. Results not typical; records on file.

What it changes: a fragmented industry, bank relationships for many deals, and clean books and a management team from deal one.

**OPTION B · Legacy: buy to keep · 2:45**

Buy something that will still be needed in 30 years. Healthcare. Trades. Services. Structure the debt so the business carries it comfortably. Once it's paid down, the profit is yours.

Debt over dilution matters most here, because the whole point is keeping it. I still own 73% of my company.

What it changes: you can be more patient on price because you're not selling. You care about people and culture more than the multiple. And you set up ownership, often a holding company, so it passes on cleanly. Get advice for your situation.

**OPTION C · Flip: buy one, fix, sell · 3:25**

Buy the $500,000 business at four times, $2 million, paid over time. Over three years, raise prices, cut costs nobody notices, and put in a manager so it runs without the owner. Profit goes to $700,000. Sold at four to five times, that's $2.8 to $3.5 million before paying off what's left of the debt.

What it changes: you hunt for obvious fixes, not perfect businesses. And the debt has to match the exit date. If you sell in year three, know what's owed then, and whether a buyer can take it over or pay it out. A year-three balloon is a gift or a trap, depending on your plan.

**THE TEST · 4:10**

Three questions, in order.

How long will you do this hard? Ten years of intensity is jackpot. Three is flip. A lifetime at a steady pace is legacy.

What do you want at the end? A cheque. A business your family owns. Or capital and a track record for the next one.

What do you have? Cash, a business or property lets you move faster on any path.

Verdict: pick one. You can change later. You can't run two at once. A jackpot buyer reinvests every dollar into the next deal. A legacy buyer takes the cash out. Try both and you build a group you can't sell while taking cash it needs.

And let the goal rise. Plenty of people start with a flip and finish with a group, because the first deal showed them what was possible. Changing goals between deals is fine. Running two inside one deal isn't.

"Jackpot is just for people with money." Jason's first deal was zero down. A roll-up is a structure problem, not a savings problem. "I don't know my goal yet." Pick the one that fits today. A flip is a fine first goal. You'll know more after deal one.
