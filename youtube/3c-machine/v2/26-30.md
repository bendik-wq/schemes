Two new owners buy the same $2 million business on the same seller note.

**STAKES · 0:20**

In a 3C deal the seller and the bank are paid from the business. Your whole structure rests on one ratio: free cash flow covering debt payments at least 1.5 times. That's DSCR, debt service cover. Break the cash flow in the first 100 days and you break the structure, before you've pulled a single growth lever.

This is for people who've bought, or are about to, and plan to own for years. If you want to buy a job and coast, skip this act.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The first 100 days have one job: keep the machine you paid for running long enough to understand it.

Most new owners think day one is where you make your mark. They think the new boss has to look decisive. They think change is what they bought. That's not how it works. You bought cash flow that already exists. Your first job is to not interrupt it.

And if you came out of a corporate job, the urge to change everything isn't your fault. It has nothing to do with your judgement or your ambition. It has everything to do with years of being rewarded for visible change. In a business paying off its own purchase price, visible change is the risk.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

**1 · Restructuring before you measure · 1:45**

Week two, new org chart. Week three, new software. Every change you make before you have a baseline is a guess with borrowed money.

The rule: thirty days of real numbers before your first big change. Revenue by source. Gross margin, meaning what's left after the direct cost of the work. How full the team's week is. Cash in the bank every Friday.

**2 · Going silent with the staff · 2:45**

You paid for the people. They walk faster than customers do. In silence, staff fill the gap with the worst story.

Day one, stand in front of them and say what isn't changing. Pay, roles, how the work gets done. Be specific. Your key people should already be on employment agreements as a condition of closing, not an afterthought. Lose one supervisor and you can lose the customers who trust them.

**3 · Not visiting the top customers with the seller · 3:45**

Pull your top 20 customers and the share of revenue each one is. Then visit or call them with the seller beside you, introducing you as the person who looks after them now.

If one or two customers are over 30% of revenue, that's a red flag in diligence. If you bought one anyway, those customers get the first visits and a call from you every month.

**4 · Letting the seller disappear · 4:45**

Negotiate a written transition, six months to three years. A seller being paid over time wants you to succeed, because their money depends on it. Use that.

Book a weekly handover. Ask for what isn't written anywhere. Who to call when the van breaks down. Which customer pays late but always pays. Then hand each relationship to a named person in the business, not just to you.

**5 · Buying growth before you find the ceiling · 5:45**

This is the expensive one, because it feels like progress.

Ask one question. If sales doubled next month, what breaks first? The one supervisor? Scheduling? Quoting? That answer is your first hire or your first system. It is never your first marketing campaign. Buying customers you can't serve costs you the customers, the staff and the reviews at once.

The order: days 1 to 30, listen. Days 31 to 60, measure and find the ceiling. Days 61 to 100, make one fix.

> **On screen:** Days 1–30 Listen · Days 31–60 Measure · Days 61–100 One fix

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Most professional buyers look at the price list before they look at marketing.

Here's why. On the same $2 million business, 10% more customers adds about $80,000 of profit. A 10% price rise adds about $200,000.

Same revenue. More than double the profit. I'll show you the maths, the test for whether you're cheap, and the order to do it in.

> **On screen:** +10% customers: ~$330K profit · +10% price: ~$450K profit (illustrative)

**STAKES · 0:20**

Profit sets the price of a business, not revenue. Every extra dollar of profit is worth three to five dollars when you sell, and it makes every seller payment safer from the first month.

This is for owners and buyers who want more out of capital already in the business. It isn't for anyone who wants to be the cheapest in town. That's a race you win by going broke slowly.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

Price is the biggest profit lever in a boring business, because a price rise carries no extra delivery cost.

Everything you were told points the other way. You were told the cheapest quote wins. You were told a business grows by adding customers. You were told a price rise loses the customers you've got. And that's exactly why so many boring businesses sit on a price list from years ago while costs rise every year.

If you own one of these businesses, being cheap isn't your fault. It has nothing to do with your skill or your work ethic. It has everything to do with nobody putting the margin maths in front of you.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and then do exactly this. If you want the numbers run on your deal, book a call below. Now the two options.

**Option A · Win 10% more customers · 1:45**

Revenue goes from $2 million to $2.2 million. But every new job carries its delivery cost. At a 40% gross margin, $200,000 of new revenue leaves $80,000.

And it isn't free. You need the capacity to deliver it, and you pay to win those customers. Profit lands around $330,000 before acquisition costs.

**Option B · Raise prices 10% · 2:45**

Same $2.2 million of revenue. Same jobs, same crews, same materials. So the extra $200,000 lands almost entirely as profit. You go from $250,000 to about $450,000.

And the part that matters for a sceptic. At a 40% gross margin, a 10% price rise still breaks even if one in five jobs walks. Most businesses lose far fewer than that.

**The self-test · 3:45**

Three checks for whether you're cheap.

One. When was the last price rise? If it's more than a year ago and your costs have moved, you're almost certainly undercharging.

Two. What's your quote win rate? Count last quarter's quotes and how many you won. If you win almost every quote, you're not great at sales. You're cheap. **[JOSH: confirm]**

Three. Is there a budget option that eats your crew's time? Kill it or price it properly. One offer, one price.

**The verdict · 4:30**

Price first, then volume.

Start with new customers. New quotes, new contracts. Nobody has a reference point. Then move existing customers with at least a month's notice, a short letter, the new rate and no apology. Put the seller beside you on the calls to your biggest accounts, because those customers trust the seller more than you yet.

Move in jumps big enough to read. A tiny change disappears in the noise. Pick a step that shows up in the numbers, run it on new quotes for a few weeks, and watch your win rate. You don't double prices overnight in a trades business. **[JOSH: confirm]**

And never discount to win. If a customer pushes back, change the terms, not the price. Deposit up front, staged payments, a longer contract.

The ceiling is your team and your customers' satisfaction. Push past it and you'll know, because good customers start leaving for the right reasons.

For the capital deployer, here's the opportunity. A business that hasn't moved its prices in years is the cheapest value you'll ever buy. You pay a multiple of today's profit. The price rise is profit the seller left on the table.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

There are two types of business owners: the ones who own a business, and the ones who own a job with a business around it.

They can make the same profit. They sell for very different prices.

Here's the contrarian part. Cutting your profit by $120,000 can add $400,000 to what the business is worth.

> **On screen:** $500K × 3 = $1.5M · $380K × 5 = $1.9M (illustrative)

**STAKES · 0:20**

Small businesses trade at roughly three to five times profit. Where you land in that range has a lot to do with one question: does the business need its owner?

If the owner wins the customers and keeps them happy, a buyer sees key-person risk. If the owner leaves, so do the customers, and the buyer prices that in.

This is for anyone who wants to own more than one business, or sell one for a proper price. It's not for people who want to be the hero who does everything.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

Owner-independence is valuation. Three seats, three named people, and proof that it runs without you.

If you're the owner who's still the business, that's not a character flaw. It has nothing to do with being a control freak or not trusting your people. It has everything to do with how small businesses grow. The founder does every job first, and nobody ever hands them on.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and set them up to run without the owner. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Why people believe the opposite · 1:45**

Owners are paid on profit, so every salary feels like money out of their pocket. "Why pay a manager $120,000 to do what I do for free?"

Because you're not free. Your time is the cost the P&L doesn't show, and the buyer sees it straight away.

Think of two rental properties with the same rent. One has a tenant on a five-year lease. The other only earns if you live in it and run it as a guesthouse. Same income. Nobody pays the same for both.

**2 · The market has moved · 2:45**

A generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. They want out, and they want to be looked after. And most of their businesses run through them. Twenty years of being the first call for every customer, every quote, every problem.

That's often why they're selling. So the buyers who know how to take the owner out of these businesses are the ones who capture the gap between three times and five times. Once you've bought, the owner in the middle is you.

**3 · Three seats, three names · 3:45**

Split the business into three jobs.

Win: everything that brings customers in. Quoting, sales, the big accounts.

Deliver: everything that does the work and keeps customers. Crews, clinicians, account managers.

Control: everything that keeps the other two out of trouble. Finance, payroll, compliance, HR.

Write a name under each. Every seat with the owner's name, or the seller's, is key-person risk. Fix one seat at a time. Low revenue is a Win problem. Churn is a Deliver problem. A missed invoice or a licence that nearly lapsed is a Control problem.

In a 3C deal, your board and external team already cover a lot of Control. Your job is a named person in the business for each seat, not just on a board.

**4 · Build it, then prove it · 4:45**

Set the scorecard before you hire for a seat. A manager who writes their own scorecard writes one they can hit.

Write the SOPs. Every repeatable job written down or screen-recorded, so a new hire does it well in week one. Record one job a week and in a year you have fifty jobs that don't need you.

Watch labour cost per revenue dollar. As systems make jobs easier, it should fall as you grow. If it's rising, you're adding people, not systems.

Use the seller transition. The seller's job isn't just to answer questions. It's to hand their relationships to named people, so the next buyer doesn't depend on you either.

Then prove it. The strongest evidence you can hand a buyer is a stretch where the owner was away and the numbers held. Two weeks is a start. A month is better. Write down what broke and fix that next.

How come people who've made real money elsewhere find it so hard to tell a good small business from a bad one?

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and run them on numbers like these. Book a call below. Now the sheet.

**1 · Survive · 1:45**

Line one. **Cash against your floor.** A simple floor is two months of fixed costs plus the next seller payment. **[JOSH: confirm]** Below it, nothing else on the sheet matters until you're back above it.

Line two. **Debt service cover.** Free cash flow over debt payments. The 3C rule is 1.5× minimum. Track it monthly, look at it weekly, and know your headroom before the bank asks.

Line three. **Customer concentration.** Share of revenue from your biggest customer and your top five. Over 30% in one or two customers is a red flag in diligence. Don't own a business you'd walk away from buying.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice.

**2 · Grow · 3:00**

Line four. **Revenue by source.** Referral, outbound, ads, repeat. Blended numbers hide which channel pays.

Line five. **Cost to win a customer.** Total sales and marketing spend divided by new customers. Include everything: ads, the salesperson's wage, the free first visit. Leave costs out and the number lies to you.

Line six. **Lifetime value to that cost.** From episode 28: at least three to one.

Line seven. **Gross margin.** What's left of each dollar after the direct cost of the work. From episode 27: it's where price shows up.

Read four and five together. A channel can bring in a small share of your leads and a large share of your revenue. Look at lead counts alone and you'll cut the one that pays the bills.

**3 · Sell · 4:15**

Line eight. **Labour cost per revenue dollar.** From episode 29. It should fall as systems make jobs easier.

Line nine. **Churn, or repeat rate.** How many customers you lost, or how many came back. Recurring, sticky revenue is what lenders and buyers pay for.

**4 · The Monday habit · 4:45**

At the bottom of the sheet, one line: what you expect next week, in numbers. Next Monday, compare. Predicting and checking is how you learn the business faster than anyone who just reads reports.

Twenty minutes, same time every week. Update the nine. Anything red gets one question: why, and who owns the fix? Then write next week's guess. If you have a general manager, they run it and you listen. The business reports to you, not through you. **[JOSH: confirm]**

**5 · What the buyer sees · 5:30**

In Act 5 we asked sellers for three years of financials, matching tax returns and revenue by year. These nine are the same lens, pointed at a target. Ask the owner for as many as they have.

Now flip it. Two owners each say their business makes $500,000. One hands over a box of statements. The other hands over two years of these nine, every week, with tax returns that match. The second gets the cleaner offer and the faster diligence.
