**3 · Level 3: three to five, one industry, one playbook · 3:15**

So every hour gets one test: does this fix a site, or the system?

This is also where the lender's view changes. Five sites reporting the same numbers, the same way, every month, is a group a bank can lend to as one. Five sets of books in five formats is five separate conversations.

**THE FRAME · 1:00**

The whole idea in one line: a board meeting exists to make decisions. If it could have been an email, it wasn't a board meeting.

There are six parts, in this order. The pre-read pack. Last month's decisions. The numbers. The decision papers. Capital and deals. The decision list.

You were told a board is a formality for big companies. Compliance and minutes. You were told small businesses don't need one. For a group that borrows money and plans to sell, none of that is true. A working board is how several businesses become something a bigger buyer wants.

And if your meetings drift, it has nothing to do with your board's talent or your charisma. It has everything to do with a meeting that was never designed to decide anything.

**SOFT CTA · 1:55**

I help owners build the board, buy the business and run the group. Book a call below. Now the teardown.

**1 · The pre-read pack · 2:05**

The pack goes out at least two days before the meeting. One page per business, one page for the group. Same numbers, same order, every month, so the trends jump out.

> **On screen:** Illustrative KPI pack.

Per business: revenue against budget, gross margin, profit, cash, debtor days, the one sales number that drives it, staff turnover, and the GM's one problem.

For the group: total profit, debt service cover against the 1.5 floor, cash against your reserve floor, the deal pipeline, and lender covenants.

Every number has a named owner who explains it. If nobody owns it, nobody fixes it. And send the pack to your GMs too. If they can see what the board sees, they fix things before the board asks.

**2 · Last month's decisions · 2:55**

Ten minutes. The meeting opens with the list from last time. Done, not done, why.

Anything that's slipped past two meetings gets discussed on its own. That's what stops "let's look at it next month" becoming the culture.

**3 · The numbers · 3:10**

Twenty minutes. No walk-through. The board has read the pack.

Questions only, and only on what's off budget or trending the wrong way. A site that's on plan gets no airtime. That's the reward. The question a good board asks more than any other: what do you expect to happen, and how do you know?

**4 · The decision papers · 3:35**

Forty minutes, one or two decisions. Each comes with a one-page paper: the options, the numbers, your recommendation.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers.

Here's one. Five clinics, each booking four hundred appointments a month at a hundred dollars. That's two hundred thousand a month for the group.

The paper proposes two moves. Price up five percent at all five: ten thousand a month. Fill two empty practitioner days at the two busiest sites: another ten thousand. Each move has an owner, a date, and the number that shows it's working.

"Grow twenty percent" isn't a decision. That is.

Agree who decides what before the meeting. The operator decides operations. The finance lead decides how the debt is structured. You break ties as the owner, and the minutes record who decided.

**5 · Capital and deals · 4:30**

Thirty minutes. Pipeline, lenders, covenant headroom. Whether to sign the LOI on business six. This is where your finance lead earns their equity.

Then ten minutes on risk: what could hurt the group in the next ninety days.

The board also sets the scorecard for every senior hire before the search starts. Never let a new GM write their own. The person being measured shouldn't choose the ruler.

**6 · The decision list · 4:55**

Ten minutes. Decision, owner, date. Read it out before anyone leaves. That list is item one next month.

I found my board through LinkedIn and cold outreach. The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder in my company. When people like that give you two hours, the meeting has to be worth it. **[JOSH: how often your board meets, for how long, and one decision a meeting changed in your group.]**

## EP 68 · 5 things I'd never sign to buy a business (1.5× DSCR first)

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | A business with $300K of free cash flow carries at most $200K a year of debt, and one panic sale of 10% equity on a $16M exit hands over $1.6M; five lines protect both (illustrative). |
| **Hook** | "This one isn't for most people." |
| **For** | HNW Capital Deployer |
| **Stage** | Capital → Grow · Season 2, Act 14 |
| **Runtime** | ~8 min |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

This one isn't for most people. If "no money down" sounds like "no risk" to you, it isn't for you.

No money down is a capital strategy, not a risk strategy. Deals that blow up rarely blow up on price. They blow up on something someone signed.

Here are five things I'd never sign, ranked from expensive to the one that costs you the exit.

> **On screen:** No money down ≠ no risk. · Education, not financial or legal advice.

**STAKES · 0:30**

When you buy with seller finance and bank debt, your equity is the thin layer on top. A twenty percent dip in profit doesn't cost you twenty percent. It can cost you the business, because the repayments don't dip with it.

This is for owners and capital deployers building something big with borrowed money. It's not for anyone who'll sign whatever's put in front of them to get a deal done.

**THE FRAME · 1:00**

The whole idea in one line: write your risk rules on a calm day, because on a bad day you'll talk yourself into anything.

There are five lines. When an owner loses a business this way, it has nothing to do with being reckless or greedy. It has everything to do with having no written rules before the pressure arrived.

Most people think nothing-down deals are fragile by design. Look at Cam, a good friend of mine. Two deals in twenty-three days, a hundred percent vendor finance, and five hundred thousand dollars of working capital from the bank. Nothing down, with a cushion built in from day one. Structured right, those two go together.

> **On screen:** Cam, Josh's friend. Results not typical; records on file.

**SOFT CTA · 1:45**

I help owners structure deals the business can actually carry. Book a call below. Now the list.

**1 · Mistake 1: a deal below 1.5× · 1:55**

DSCR is free cash flow divided by debt repayments. The floor is one and a half.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal. · Education, not financial or legal advice.

A business throws off three hundred thousand of free cash flow. Divide by one and a half, and the most it should pay in debt service is two hundred thousand a year. The other hundred is your buffer.

Now the bear case. Profit drops twenty percent. Three hundred becomes two-forty. Against two hundred of repayments, that's 1.2. Tight, but you're paying. Close at 1.2 instead, and the same dip puts you under one. You're funding the bank from your own pocket.

Rule: no deal closes below 1.5 in the base case. Check each business on its own, and the group as a whole. A strong business can hide a weak one in the group total, and the bank will find it before you do.

**2 · Mistake 2: settling with no cushion · 2:55**

Being undercapitalised at close is one of the most common ways a good deal goes bad. Wages, a slow debtor and a broken machine, all in month two.

Write a reserve floor. Months of fixed costs plus debt repayments, held in cash or an undrawn facility, that you never go below. The right number depends on your business and your lender. The point is that it's written, and it's on the board pack every month.

Then put a stop-loss on every new bet. A new site or service line gets a budget and a kill line before it starts. Fifty thousand. If it isn't paying back by month four, we stop. Illustrative, and written in the minutes.

**3 · Mistake 3: a personal guarantee by default · 3:45**

On larger deals, often over about five million, an asset-rich business can give the bank its security, and a personal guarantee often isn't needed. Typically. Every lender and deal is different.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice. Every lender and deal is different.

On smaller deals you may have to sign one. Then you negotiate it. Cap the amount. Put a time limit on it. Have it fall away when the debt drops to a set level. Never sign the default. The first draft is the bank's opening position, not its last.

**4 · Mistake 4: unsecured personal debt · 4:20**

Credit cards, personal loans, anything that can follow you home. It's fast, which is the problem. It lets a business that's bleeding look like one that's growing, and it's the dearest money there is.

Never stack it on top of a seller note. You'd be paying the seller from the business and the bank from your house.

Your personal costs should be covered before you sign. Savings, a partner's income, or a salary the business can carry. If your rent depends on the business having a good month, your risk is personal, not commercial. That's when people sign everything on this list.

**5 · Mistake 5: equity sold to plug a cash gap · 5:05**

This is the most expensive line, and it feels like the safest. Selling equity in a panic has no repayments, so it looks like the low-risk option.

It isn't. Debt gets paid off. Equity is forever. Sell ten percent to cover a bad quarter, and if the group later sells for sixteen million, that bad quarter cost you one point six million. Maybe the control over when and to whom you sell, too. A new investor who came in on a bad day has every reason to want a say on the good one.

Debt over dilution, inside the 1.5 rule. It's why I keep 73% of my company. **[JOSH: your own reserve floor or the one line you won't cross, and why; any document you've refused to sign, without names.]**

**THE NUMBERS · 5:50**

| | Illustrative |

| Most debt service at 1.5× | $200,000 |
| Bear case, profit down 20% | $240,000 → 1.2× |
| Same dip if you'd closed at 1.2× ($250K repayments) | 0.96× |

The top rows are why the floor is 1.5. The bottom row is why you cover a gap with debt you can carry, not equity you can never buy back.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:00**

- Running only the base case. The base case never arrives on time.
- Counting property or equipment as the reserve. It doesn't pay this Friday's wages.
- Hiring fixed overhead ahead of proven revenue. Payroll doesn't fall when sales do.

**CLOSE · 6:20**

There are two paths. Learn these lines alone, usually right after you've crossed one. Or have someone check every structure before you sign it, in the exact moment you want to break a rule.

On a call with me we look at your deal or your next one, check it against these five lines, and work out whether the Owners Academy or 1:1 advisory fits.

If you're someone who wants the upside of buying businesses, and refuses to bet everything to get it, this is for you.

Thirty days from now you'll either have a written reserve floor and a never-sign list, or you'll still be meaning to write them.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

James works seventy hours a week and answers email at nine at night. He hasn't had a week off in two years. Chad works hard too, but he stops at dinner and his holidays are booked a year out.

Chad's group is the one that's growing. Not because he works less. Because he decides better.

Running at a hundred percent isn't commitment. In a group, it's the most expensive habit an owner has.

> **On screen:** Two illustrative owners. Not real people.

**STAKES · 0:30**

At one business, hours are output. At five, the owner's output is a handful of decisions a quarter. A GM who isn't working. A covenant getting tight. Whether to sign the next LOI. Tired owners make those calls late, and a late call on one site can cost more than a year of your salary.

If you left a corporate job to own a business, the trap is rebuilding the same job with more debt attached. Same seventy hours. Now the risk is yours.

This is for people who'll put forty to a hundred hours a week into building this. It's not for the four-hour-work-week crowd. I mean that here most of all.

**THE FRAME · 1:20**

The whole idea in one line: decide at sixty percent, don't run at a hundred. They're different things.

There are three parts. Why people believe hours win. What actually moves the numbers once you own the businesses. And a test.

When an owner burns out, it has nothing to do with being soft, or not wanting it enough. It has everything to do with never deciding what they were willing to trade.

**SOFT CTA · 1:50**

I help owners build a group without it eating them. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Why people believe hours win · 2:00**

Because early on, they do. Before my first deal I made about a thousand cold calls, and four deals fell over. That stage is volume. You make the calls, send the offers, do the reps.

The mistake is staying in sprint mode after the job has changed. Once you own the businesses, the volume belongs to your GMs. Your job is the few decisions only you can make.

You were told the owner who works the most hours wins. You were told rest is for later. You were told that if you're not exhausted, you're not serious. None of that holds once you own the business.

**2 · What actually moves the numbers · 2:45**

Decide at sixty percent. There is never a perfect moment. Waiting for a hundred percent certainty isn't safe. It's passive risk. You lose the deal, or the year.

But the decision is the fast part. The pace is what you can hold.

A long-haul driver doesn't arrive sooner by skipping every stop. He arrives later, or not at all. It only takes one bad call at the wheel.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

There are only two types of business partnerships. The ones that wrote down what happens when the money arrives, and the ones that break when it does.

Partnerships rarely break when things are hard. They break when things start working.

On a sixteen-million-dollar exit, four clauses decide who gets what. Here's the handshake against the agreement, and a test to tell you which one you've actually got.

> **On screen:** Handshake vs 4 clauses · on a $16M exit · Illustrative.

**STAKES · 0:30**

In the 3C model you'll have more partners than you think. Co-buyers. Board members on founders' equity. Sellers who roll equity into the new company. Each one owns a slice of the exit.

If the paperwork doesn't say what happens when someone stops, leaves or disagrees, the exit is when you find out. That's the worst possible day to negotiate.

This is for anyone buying with a co-buyer, building a board on equity, or taking a seller's rollover. It's not for anyone hoping a handshake will hold for ten years.

**THE FRAME · 1:05**

The whole idea in one line: contribution changes over time, so the terms have to be able to change too.

Two options. Option A is the handshake. Fifty-fifty, goodwill, sort it out later. Option B is four written clauses: roles and vesting, deadlock, buy-sell, and minority exit. Plus one trigger.

When a partnership breaks, it has nothing to do with bad people, or greed, or one partner getting lazy. It has everything to do with terms written for year one that nobody touched again.

**SOFT CTA · 1:45**

I help owners structure this from the start, so the group survives the money arriving. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Option A · The handshake · 1:55**

People choose it because it's fast, it's friendly, and writing things down feels like you don't trust each other.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal.

Here's how it plays out. Two co-buyers, fifty-fifty, buy a business making four hundred thousand. One runs it full time. The other finds the next deal. By year three the seller note's paid down, the business throws off cash, and the deal-finder has taken a job elsewhere.

Same fifty-fifty. Completely different contribution. The operator is doing all the work for half the profit, and resentment does the rest.

Now add a board member given five percent on a handshake, who stopped turning up after six months. And a seller who rolled in with a veto. The group grows to sixteen million, a buyer wants a hundred percent, and three people can each slow the deal down.

Most people think trust is enough. They think writing it down means you don't trust each other. That's not how it works. Writing it down is what lets you keep trusting each other in year five.

**Option B · The four clauses · 3:05**

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice. Get your own lawyer.

Your lawyer drafts these. You decide what goes in.

One. Roles and vesting. Who does what, how many hours, and what happens if someone stops. Only partner where the jobs don't overlap. One person finds and finances deals, the other runs the businesses. Two people doing the same job is one too many. Equity vests against contribution. For board members, that's founders' equity, two to ten percent, vesting from the signed purchase agreement.

Two. Deadlock. Agree decision rights in advance. The deal person decides on structures. The operator decides on operations. Then a formal last resort, which your lawyer sets out.

Three. Buy-sell. If a partner wants out, dies, divorces or stops contributing, how is their share valued, who can buy it, and how is it paid for? Agree the formula now, while nobody knows which side of it they'll be on.

Four. Minority holders on exit. Drag-along, so a minority can't block a sale of the group. No veto and no voting control for small holders. Set up from the start.

Then the trigger. The terms reopen when the seller note is paid off, or when anyone's hours drop below an agreed level. Maybe the operator gets a salary first, then profit splits. Maybe the partner who stepped back sells some shares under the buy-sell formula. The conversation is already in the contract, not left to resentment.

**Sellers are partners too · 4:45**

Any seller who rolls equity into the new company is a partner who used to be the boss.

My friend Jason Nagy's first deal was a five-and-a-half-million-dollar radiology business, zero down, on a sixty-forty structure. Seven years later he sold to private equity for two hundred million.

> **On screen:** Jason Nagy, Josh's friend. Results not typical; records on file.

At that end of the market, the buyer wants a clean shareholder register. So the same rules apply to the seller. Their role after settlement. What they can and can't vote on. And drag-along, so they come with you when the group sells.

It's also why I care about control. I keep 73% of my company. Equity can be shared. Control should sit where the decisions get made. **[JOSH: how your own shareholders' agreement handles deadlock or exit, or a partnership you've seen break, without names.]**

**The self-test · 5:35**

Two questions. If your partner stopped working tomorrow, could you say in one sentence what happens to their shares, at what price, and who pays? And if a buyer offered for a hundred percent of the group, could anyone block it?

If you can't answer the first, or the answer to the second is yes, you're on Option A, whatever you think you agreed.

**The verdict · 6:00**

Option B, every time you share equity. No exceptions for friends. Friends are who it protects most.

And if you can't find a partner whose job is different from yours, go solo and keep a hundred percent. You'll still have a board and maybe a seller rolling in, so clauses three and four still apply.
