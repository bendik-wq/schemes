# Act 4 · Finding deals

This act covers where good businesses actually come from: off-market owners, reached by phone, worked as a pipeline, in industries a bank will fund. Price and terms are set before the first offer goes out, by who else is bidding and what you said on the first call. This is where most of the money is made or lost.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

There's a well-kept secret in buying businesses. The one you should buy isn't for sale.

It's not on a listing site. No broker has it. It belongs to an owner nobody has rung yet.

Before my first deal I made about a thousand of those calls and watched four deals fall over. Then one closed, with no deposit.

> **On screen:** 1,000 calls · 4 dead deals · 1 clinic, 100% vendor finance

**STAKES · 0:20**

Where you find a business decides what you pay for it. A listed business is an auction: cash buyers, sometimes private equity, a broker paid to push the price up. An owner you call direct has one buyer on the line. You. That's where terms come from, and terms are where the money is. This is for people who'll pick up the phone forty hours a week. If you want a listing to land in your inbox, this isn't your episode.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The rule in one line: go direct to owners, and treat a thousand calls as the unit, not fifty.

Three parts. What happened on my thousand. Why off-market pays. And the system that turns calls into deals.

If you've looked for a business before and found nothing worth buying, it has nothing to do with your salary, your age or your contacts. It has everything to do with where you were looking. Most good businesses never go in the "for sale" window.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people run this, from the list to the first offer, in the Owners Academy or one-on-one. Book a call below if you want help with yours. Now the detail.

**1 · The confession · 1:40**

At 25 I was unemployed. No savings, zero experience, no network.

You were told you need a network to find a deal. You were told good businesses come through brokers. You were told no owner sells to someone with no track record. I had none of that. So I called.

About a thousand calls. Four deals that got close and died. **[JOSH: one line on what killed one of the four failed deals.]**

The one that closed was a physio clinic. 100% vendor finance. Seven years at 4% principal and interest. A nine-month payment holiday. Rent in arrears. No deposit. I pulled about $150K out in year one.

> **On screen:** Physio clinic: 100% vendor finance · 7 yrs at 4% P&I · 9-month payment holiday · no deposit · ~$150K out in year one. Results not typical; records on file. Education, not financial or legal advice.

The thousand calls and the four failures were the training. Nobody puts that part in the thumbnail.

Here's the diagnosis. The calls didn't fail because owners won't sell. Most of them said "not now," not "never." What I didn't have early on was a list I trusted, a fixed time to call, and a date to ring each owner back. Once I had those three, the calls stopped being a mood and became a pipeline.

**2 · Why off-market pays · 3:00**

A generation of owners in traditional industries wants to retire. Clinics, electricians, cleaners, plumbers. Twenty or thirty years carrying the business. They want out, and they want their staff looked after.

Most have never listed. Listing means a broker, months of tyre-kickers and a buyer's bank process that can run sixteen weeks.

Call direct and three things change. There's no auction. You can talk about the seller getting paid over time. And you set the pace. Paid over time, a seller can get above market, six to seven times profit instead of three to five, and close in two to four weeks instead of six to sixteen.

> **On screen:** Listed: auction · 3–5× · 6–16 weeks. Direct, paid over time: one buyer · 6–7× · 2–4 weeks. Education, not financial or legal advice.

Do the maths on that from the seller's side. A higher price, paid from a business they already know makes money, with no auction and no sixteen-week wait. For a lot of retiring owners that beats a lower cash cheque from a stranger.

One rule. Never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time." One sounds like a loan. The other sounds like a pension.

**3 · The system · 4:30**

Volume before judgement. Fifty calls tells you nothing. At call one hundred you're reading off the page. By three hundred you sound like a person. By six hundred you hear an owner's mood in the first sentence. You can't skip to six hundred.

Pick one industry and one city. You want to sound like you know the space by week two.

Build a list of one to two thousand businesses from directories, associations, map searches and company registers. Owner's name, phone, rough revenue. Aim for roughly half a million to ten million in revenue. Under about a million in value, a deal rarely throws off enough cash to pay the debt and pay you.

Block the same three hours every morning. Forty to fifty calls. Afternoons are follow-up.

Run a tracker. Status goes Cold, Warm, Hot, Financials, Offer, Due diligence, Close. The column that makes money is "next action date." Every "not now" gets a date. Health changes. A partner wants out. The kids say no. Whoever calls back in the right month gets the deal.

*[B-roll: tracker with the next-action-date column highlighted]*

It works without a track record. Saul, an Owners Academy member, found an electrical and air-conditioning business by cold call. Twenty-five staff, about $3.8 million revenue, owner retiring in two years. NDA signed, early due diligence. No broker, no auction.

> **On screen:** Saul, Owners Academy member: NDA signed, early DD · ~$3.8M revenue · 25 staff · found by cold call. Results not typical; records on file.

|---|---|

| Blocks per week | 4 |
| Calls per week | 200 |
| Weeks to 1,000 calls | 5 |

Sixty hours to put yourself in front of most of the owners in one industry and one city. People spend longer picking a company name.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:50**

- Stopping at call sixty and deciding the industry's wrong. The first thousand was the cost of entry, and you've thrown it away.
- No follow-up. Every "not yet" goes cold, and the owner sells to whoever rang back.
- Talking price on the first call. You've set the anchor before you've seen a tax return.
- Calling five industries at once. You sound like a tourist in all of them.

**CLOSE · 7:20**

## EP 17 · The 6-word question that finds off-market deals (copy it)

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | Say "four times" on call one to an owner claiming $250K profit and you've anchored $1M; if diligence finds $180K, you're clawing back $280K from your own number (illustrative). |
| **Hook** | "There are two types of people who ring a business owner. The ones who get a conversation, and the ones who get a click." |
| **For** | Trapped Owner |
| **Stage** | Closing · Season 1, Act 4 |
| **Runtime** | ~8 min |
| **Copy** | Shape E (teardown of the call, in the order the owner hears it) · Two-Types opener · That's Not How It Works · No Choice → Identity Close → After Close |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

There are two types of people who ring a business owner. The ones who get a conversation, and the ones who get a click.

The difference is six words, asked inside twenty seconds. Most people bury them under two minutes of small talk, then quote a price to sound serious.

Here's the whole call, line by line, in the order the owner hears it.

> **On screen:** "Have you ever thought about selling?"

**STAKES · 0:20**

The first sixty seconds decide two things: whether you get a second call, and what number you'll be negotiating from later. Get it wrong and you either get hung up on or you anchor a price you can't walk back. If you run a business, you've been on the other end of this call. You know how fast you decide.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The rule: earn ten seconds, ask the question, then ask, don't tell.

Five parts, in order. The opener. The question. The three answers. Discovery. The exit.

If your calls have been dying early, it has nothing to do with your voice, your age or your accent. It has everything to do with your first sentence.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:20**

I help people with this, from the first script to the signed offer, in the Academy or one-on-one. Book a call below. Now the call.

**1 · The opener · 1:30**

Word for word.

> **On screen:** "Hi [Name], this is [your name] with [company]. I'm building a company in [industry] and wanted to introduce myself. Have you ever thought about selling [business]?"

Four parts. Name and company, so you sound like a business. "Building a company in your industry," so you sound like a buyer with a plan. That line only works if it's true, which is why Credibility comes first. "Wanted to introduce myself," which says relationship, not one-off. Then the question.

No "how are you going today." Every owner knows that line means a sales call.

**[JOSH: one line on the opener you actually used on the physio clinic call, if it differed.]**

**2 · The question · 2:30**

"Have you ever thought about selling" is soft on purpose. It doesn't ask them to commit. It's easy for an owner to say, "Yeah, actually, I have." And a fair few do.

Compare "Are you looking to sell?" That's a yes-or-no about today, and today the answer is nearly always no. "Ever thought about it" lets them tell you about the bad year, the sore back, the kids who don't want it.

**3 · The three answers · 3:00**

"Yes, actually." Don't pitch. Ask, "What's got you thinking about it?" Then shut up.

"Not right now." Say, "No problem. I'm building relationships in the industry. Mind if I check back in six to twelve months?" Date in the tracker. A "not yet" owner is still an owner.

"No," or the click. "No worries, thanks for your time." Next call.

**4 · Discovery · 3:40**

Most people think a good first call is a good pitch. That the owner needs to hear about you, your plan, your money. That's not how it works.

You have the most leverage before you've said a number. The moment you say one, that's the number. So on call one you ask.

> **On screen:** Why would you sell? · How long have you run it? · Anyone in the family keen to take it on? · What matters most when you step back? · Who else has a say?

That last one is the one people skip. A spouse, a partner, the accountant. If someone else decides, you want them on the next call, not hearing about it over dinner.

Learn the questions, then have a conversation. It should sound like two business owners talking.

Here's why discovery is worth money. The answers become your structure. An owner who cares about the staff wants a handover period, and that's time for you to learn the business on their watch. An owner who wants steady income is the one who'll take getting paid over time. You can't design that offer if you spent the call talking.

Don't explain the 3C model. Don't say "vendor finance." Don't mention a multiple. And never say you've got no money.

If they ask how you'd pay: "We'd look at options once I've seen the numbers. Some owners like getting paid over time, some want more up front."

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice.

Here's an illustrative call.

> **On screen:** Illustrative call. Not a real seller.

"Hi Gary, this is Josh. I'm building a company in commercial cleaning here in Melbourne and wanted to introduce myself. Have you ever thought about selling?"

"Funny you ask. My wife's been on at me about it."

"What's got you both thinking about it?"

"I'm sixty-three. Back's had it. Kids aren't interested."

"If you did step back, what would matter most?"

"The staff. Some have been with me fifteen years."

"Would your wife want to be part of the conversation?"

"Probably, yeah."

No price, no structure, nothing about me. Gary did the talking. I know why he'd sell, what he cares about, and who decides.

**5 · The exit · 5:40**

Leave every live call with an NDA going out or a date to call back. Send the NDA within the hour, not tomorrow. How fast it comes back is your first read on how serious they are. Once it's signed, you ask for three years of numbers, and that's where pricing actually starts.

**THE NUMBERS · 6:00**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Profit the owner mentions on call one | $250K |
| You say "around four times" | $1.0M anchor |
| Profit after diligence | $180K |
| Four times the real number | $720K |
| Gap you now argue down from your own number | $280K |

On call one you have no tax returns, no revenue breakdown, no idea what the add-backs are hiding. Any number you say is a guess with your name on it. Saying a number early doesn't make you look serious. It hands the seller $280K of expectation that you created and now have to take back.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:30**

- Quoting a multiple to sound smart. The owner anchors on it for the rest of the deal.
- Saying "vendor finance." The owner hears "this buyer has no money." Say "getting paid over time."
- Finding out about the spouse or partner three months in, when they quietly kill the deal.
- Talking about yourself. Calls that die around two minutes usually died there.

**CLOSE · 7:00**

You can keep pitching on call one, quoting a multiple, and finding out who really decides after you've spent three months on the deal. Or you can have someone listen to your calls and tell you what to fix.

On a call with me we work out your strategy, your first structure, and whether the Academy or one-on-one advisory fits.

If you're someone who already knows what it's like to carry a business alone, and you'd rather ask the question than give the speech, you've already decided.

Thirty days from now you'll be thirty days older either way. Or thirty days older with an opener that sounds like you and a few NDAs out.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Every day, almost everyone looking for a business does the same thing. Same listing sites. Same brokers. Same auction.

A broker's job is to get the seller the highest price, in cash, fast. Fair job. It's the opposite of yours.

Same business, two ways to buy it. One costs about a million in cash. The other pays the seller more and costs you nothing up front.

> **On screen:** Listed: ~$1M cash · Direct: ~$1.66M over time, $0 down. Illustrative.

**STAKES · 0:20**

If you've got capital, you can buy a listed business outright and plenty do. The question is whether you should pay auction prices with your own cash when the seller would fund it for you. Every dollar you don't put into deal one goes into deal two. This is for buyers who want terms, not just a purchase.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The rule: at least 80% of your deal-finding goes direct to owners. Brokers get 20%, at most.

Option A, buy listed. Option B, buy direct. A self-test. Then the verdict, and when a broker is still worth a call.

If you've lost listed deals before, it has nothing to do with your offer, your capital or your judgement. It has everything to do with the room you were in. That process is built for one outcome: the highest cash bid at settlement.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people buy with little to no money down, which mostly means getting them off listing sites and onto the phone. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Option A: buy listed · 1:40**

Three costs.

Competition. A listing is an auction. Cash buyers, sometimes private equity. With little down you lose most of them. With money, you pay for the privilege of winning.

Price. The broker is usually paid on the sale. Their incentive is a high headline, cash at settlement, done quickly. An offer paid over time is what that process is built to filter out.

Leverage. The broker sets the timeline. You're one name on a spreadsheet. "Offers by Friday."

Brokers do real work. For a seller who wants the most cash on the day, a good one earns the fee. Just remember who they work for.

And the listing has been dressed for sale. Read it the way a lender would: the profit on the tax return, not the profit in the brochure.

**2 · Option B: buy direct · 3:00**

Here's what a lot of buyers haven't caught up with. A generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. The listing sites still look the same, but many of the owners who'll sell in the next few years haven't rung a broker yet.

For that owner, getting paid over time can be the better deal. Six to seven times profit instead of three to five. Two to four weeks to close instead of six to sixteen. You set the pace, you shape the structure, and you can walk away.

> **On screen:** Paid over time: 6–7× · 2–4 weeks. Typical: 3–5× · 6–16 weeks. Education, not financial or legal advice.

That conversation is hard to start once a broker has told the seller the goal is maximum cash.

And there's no bidding war, so the price is set by what the business can carry, not by the most excited buyer in the room. That's the number you want to pay.

**3 · The self-test · 4:00**

Three questions.

Do you need the seller to carry part of the price? Do you want your capital free for the next deal? Would you rather not wait sixteen weeks on a buyer's bank?

Two yeses and you go direct. Three noes and you've got cash, patience and one deal in mind. Buy listed and pay the market. Just know that's what you're doing.

**4 · The verdict · 4:30**

Direct first. Brokers for three things.

Stale listings. Six months or more on the market. The cash buyers have looked and passed, and the seller is more open than on day one.

Open brokers. Some will put terms in front of their client once they see a credible team behind you. Ask straight:

> **On screen:** "I'm building a company in [industry]. We buy where the owner is open to being paid over time, with a handover. Got anything that's sat a while where the owner might be open to that?"

Market intel. What's selling, at what multiples, which owners asked about value and never listed. Take them for a coffee. Never tell a broker your walk-away number.

Same rules on a broker deal as any other. Three years of financials and matching tax returns before an offer. The 1.5 cover rule. And a willingness to walk.

In hours, that's four mornings a week on owner calls and one block on brokers and stale listings. Do that for a month and you'll know your market's prices better than most buyers, so the stale listing that's actually a bargain jumps out.

| **Direct**: $250K ÷ 1.5 DSCR | ~$166K/yr to the seller |

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:40**

- Trusting add-backs. If "adjusted" profit is miles above the tax return, the price is built on air.

- Letting "other buyers are interested" or a Friday deadline set your pace. You don't do your best work on someone else's clock.
- One or two customers making up a big slice of revenue. One contract leaves and so does your debt cover.

**CLOSE · 7:10**

Two paths. Six months on listing sites, a handful of offers beaten by cash or won at the top of the range, then deciding little-to-no-money-down doesn't work. Or the same six months spent talking to owners, with someone who's read the numbers on the deals you're looking at.

## EP 19 · 1,000 calls to 1 business: every stage of the deal-flow maths

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | In this illustrative funnel, 1,000 calls, about 60 hours on the phone, produce one business; tracking five numbers tells you which stage is costing you the deal. |
| **Hook** | "Two owners both want a second business. One reads listings on Sunday nights. The other writes down five numbers every Monday." |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Two owners both want a second business. One reads listings on Sunday nights and calls owners when the shop's quiet. The other calls in the same block four mornings a week and writes down five numbers every Monday.

A year later the first one has a folder. The second has offers out.

A friend of mine in the US, Elliot, sent around forty offers. Three came back signed, all at 100% seller finance. Forty to get three. That's what deal flow looks like.

> **On screen:** Two made-up buyers, illustrative. Elliot (a friend of Josh's, US): ~40 offers, 3 LOIs signed. Results not typical; records on file.

**STAKES · 0:20**

If you own one business and want a second, the constraint usually isn't capital. The deal gets funded by the structure, the seller and the lender. The constraint is a pipeline you can't measure. This is for owners who like knowing where they stand. If you're counting on one lucky call, it might happen. Don't plan on it.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The rule: work backwards from one close to a weekly number, then track five stages.

Calls. Conversations. NDAs. Offers. Closes. Five levels. Each one leaks for a different reason.

If you've been at this for months with nothing to show, it has nothing to do with your work ethic or your market. It has everything to do with what you were counting. Most people count deals, and deals lag by months.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people build this pipeline and read it every week, in the Academy or one-on-one. Book a call below if you want someone to find your leak. Now the levels.

> **On screen:** ILLUSTRATIVE ONLY. Not a benchmark. Not a promise.

**1 · Level 1: Calls · 1:40**

A call is a dial, answered or not. Say a thousand.

This is the one stage you fully control. If few calls turn into conversations, your list is weak or you're ringing at the wrong time of day. Mornings suit most owners.

Set a weekly call target you can hit four weeks running. Not a heroic one. Two hundred a week is four mornings of fifty. If you've only got evenings, the target is smaller and the timeline is longer. Plan with your real hours.

**[JOSH: rough weekly call count in the run-up to your first deal.]**

**2 · Level 2: Conversations · 2:20**

A conversation is the owner, on the phone, hearing the question. Not the receptionist. Not voicemail. Say two hundred.

If you've got conversations but no NDAs, it's your opener and your questions. That was episode 17.

Track one more thing here: who asked you to call back, and when. Those "not yet" owners are Level 2 conversations you've already paid for. Ring them on the date and they cost you nothing to restart.

**3 · Level 3: NDAs · 2:50**

An NDA counts when it's signed and back, not when you sent it. Say twenty.

A lot of these come from owners you first rang six or twelve months ago. That's why weeks one to five feel like nothing's happening. You're planting.

If NDAs pile up with no offers, you're over-analysing. Get the three documents, then send an offer.

**4 · Level 4: Offers · 3:30**

An offer counts when it's in the seller's hands with a price and a structure. Say four.

This is the number I care about most early on. So, honest question. You've been watching for months. How many offers have you sent?

Sending one feels huge. It isn't. A non-binding indicative offer is exactly that: non-binding. A price range and the shape of the deal, subject to diligence and finance. If diligence turns up problems, you adjust or you walk.

> **On screen:** NBIO = non-binding indicative offer. Education, not financial or legal advice.

I know what you're thinking. "I've already got a business eating my week." Look at Jy Kingston, who I mentor. IT background, no finance experience. Twelve deals active while he closes his first. Not because he had advantages you don't. Because he had volume.

> **On screen:** Jy Kingston (mentee): 12 deals active, closing his first. Results not typical; records on file.

When you've got ten deals moving, you can walk from a bad one. When you've got one, you'll talk yourself into it.

**5 · Level 5: Close · 4:40**

A close is settlement. Keys, not a handshake. Say one.

Offer to settlement can take three to six months. Diligence, finance, contracts. That's the lag, and it's why you never stop calling while a deal is in diligence. The next deal starts at Level 1.

You don't control closes. Owners and lenders do. You control calls, follow-ups and offers sent. Set targets on those three and treat the rest as the scoreboard.

If you've got offers but no closes, either your structures don't work for sellers or you're picking the wrong businesses. More calls won't fix that.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:20**

| Stage | Per business | Per week (200 calls) |
|---|---|---|
| Calls | 1,000 | 200 |
| Conversations | 200 | 40 |
| NDAs | 20 | ~4 |
| Offers | 4 | one every 1–2 weeks |
| Close | 1 | lags 3–6 months |

About sixty hours on the phone for one business, on these made-up ratios. Once you've made two hundred calls of your own, bin my numbers and forecast from yours: calls times your conversation rate, times your NDA rate. Now you can predict next month instead of hoping.

*[B-roll: the Monday sheet. Week · Calls · Conversations · NDAs · Offers sent · Offers signed · Closes · Follow-ups due]*

Fifteen minutes every Monday. Fill in last week, set this week. "Follow-ups due" is the owners whose next-action date lands this week. Often your cheapest deal flow.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:20**

- Fudging the definitions on a bad week. A voicemail isn't a conversation, and a sent NDA isn't a signed one.
- Sitting on twenty NDAs with no offers out. The deal goes to the buyer who sent one.
- Fixing the wrong stage. A month rewriting your script when the leak was your list.
- Working one deal at a time. You overpay for the only thing in your pipeline.

How come smart people with good jobs so often pick the one business a bank won't touch?

The café. The agency. The startup. Then the lender says no.

The best business to buy is usually the dullest one on the street. Here's how a bank picks, and a seven-question test to run before you make a single call.

**STAKES · 0:20**

With little to no money down, the lender and the seller are funding your deal. If they won't back the business, there's no deal, however good it looks. If you want to buy the café you love, this will be a buzzkill.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The rule: the lender is your first customer. Pick the business they'd lend against.

Four parts. Why smart buyers pick wrong. What a lender pays for. Where to look. The test.

If you've been drawn to the interesting business, it has nothing to do with your judgement. It has everything to do with nobody telling you how a lender thinks.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:20**

I help people pick the industry and get the first deal funded, in the Academy or one-on-one. If you want a second opinion before you spend five weeks calling, book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Why smart buyers pick wrong · 1:30**

Before you know how a lender thinks, you pick like a customer. Is it exciting? Would I use it?

After you know, you pick like a bank. Will this cash flow still be here in five years? What's there to fall back on if it isn't?

The customer lens costs you twice. The bank says no, and the seller, who needs the bank or the business to pay them, won't carry an offer either. You've done the work on a deal nobody will fund.

My first business was a physio clinic. Nobody's making a film about it. But the seller, the bank and I could all see exactly how it made money. **[JOSH: one line on why you chose a physio clinic for your first deal.]**

**2 · What a lender pays for · 2:30**

Two things. Cash flow that lasts and assets to fall back on.

A business that's traded twenty years with repeat customers and vans, equipment and receivables gives them both. A two-year-old business with one big client and some laptops gives them neither.

Assets also change your personal risk. On larger deals, over about five million, an asset-rich business often gives the bank its security without a personal guarantee. Typically. Every lender and every deal is different.

> **On screen:** Personal guarantees: often not needed on larger, asset-rich deals. Every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice.

You go into the bank as the interviewer. Part of that is bringing them a business they already like lending to.

One more filter: what caps this business? A location, one platform, one big customer, the owner? Some caps you fix after you buy. The one to fear is the owner. If the owner is the product, the customers might leave with them.

**3 · Where to look · 3:40**

Healthcare. Allied health, GP, dental, imaging. Steady demand, regulated, clinicians tired of the admin. My friend Jason Nagy's first deal was a $5.5 million radiology business with no money down. He sold the group to private equity seven years later for $200 million.

> **On screen:** Saul, Owners Academy member: NDA signed, early DD · ~$3.8M revenue · 25 staff. Results not typical; records on file.

Commercial services. Cleaning, logistics, waste. Contracts, vehicles, repeat work.

Professional services. Accounting practices and the like. Recurring fees.

What they share: traditional, fragmented, lots of owners near retirement. Fragmented is what makes a roll-up possible later. Buy several at around four times profit, combine them, and a group can sell at eight, ten, fifteen times.

**4 · The test · 5:00**

Seven yes-or-no questions. Score the industry first, then each business.

> **On screen:** 1. Trading 10+ years? 2. Owner near retirement, no successor? 3. Assets a bank can lend against? 4. Repeat or recurring revenue? 5. Net margin around 15%+? 6. No customer over ~30% of revenue? 7. Could you grow it without the owner?

Most of these you can answer from the outside. Years trading. Vans in the photos. Reviews naming the same staff for years. A website that talks about contracts, not one-off jobs.

Then a size check. Under about a million in value, a deal rarely has the cash flow to pay the debt and pay you. Five or more yeses and the right size, keep going. Fewer, move on.

Then the order. Score three industries and pick one. Score five businesses in it. Call two lenders and ask their appetite for that sector. Once a lender tells you what they like, build the list and start the calling block from episode 16.
