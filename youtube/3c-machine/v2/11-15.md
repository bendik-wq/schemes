### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

How come the buyer with less money can pay the seller more?

A business making $250,000 a year in profit sells to a cash buyer for about a million. The same business can carry an offer of about $1.66 million, paid over time, out of its own profit.

Not your money. The business's. Here's the two-minute maths, and the three places it lies to you.

> **On screen:** $250K profit → cash offer ~$1M vs paid over time ~$1.66M · Illustrative · Education, not financial or legal advice.

**STAKES · 0:30**

That gap is the whole of Capital. The cash route needs a deposit, a bank's yes and months of waiting. The paid-over-time route has the seller fund the deal, you bring nothing, and you still put the biggest number on the table.

This is for owners, executives and people with capital who want a cash-flowing business without tying up their own money. If you want a four-hour week, this isn't your channel.

**THE FRAME · 1:00**

One line: don't ask what a business is worth. Ask what it can pay.

Two offers for the same business. Option A, the cash buyer. Option B, paid over time. Then a test that tells you which one you can actually make.

And if you've spent years thinking you need a deposit first, that has nothing to do with your salary or your savings. It has everything to do with a question nobody taught you to ask.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners build these structures and put them in front of sellers. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Option A · The cash buyer · 1:40**

A cash buyer pays three to five times profit. On $250,000, that's $750,000 to $1.25 million. Call it a million.

To pay it, they need a deposit and a bank. The bank takes six to sixteen weeks. The seller waits, and the deal can still die in credit.

For a retiring owner that's the worst of both. A lower price, and a slow close with no certainty.

**Option B · Paid over time · 2:10**

Three steps. I call it the speed run.

One. Real profit. Say $250,000.

Two. Divide by 1.5. About $166,000 a year. That's the most this business should pay in debt each year. The other $84,000 stays in the business as a buffer. That rule is next episode.

Three. Multiply by the term. Ten years at $166,000 is $1.66 million. About 6.6 times profit.

> **On screen:** $250,000 ÷ 1.5 = ~$166,000 a year × 10 years = ~$1.66M · Illustrative, before interest

You've just outbid the cash buyer by about $660,000. And you can close in two to four weeks, not six to sixteen.

Now, you were told you need a deposit, a track record and a bank's permission. That's not how retiring owners sell. My first deal was a physio clinic. I was 25, unemployed, no savings. 100% vendor finance, seven years at 4%, a nine-month payment holiday, rent in arrears, no deposit. I pulled $150,000 out in year one.

> **On screen:** Josh's first deal: 100% vendor finance, 7 yrs at 4%, 9-month holiday · Results not typical; records on file.

The seller got paid by the business. Not by me.

**The test · Three checks before you say a number · 3:15**

The speed run is a ceiling, not a price. Three checks turn it into an offer.

Check one. The profit is real. Every seller's profit number is a story. Some tell it high, some tell it low, most don't know it. Get three years of financials, the matching tax returns, and revenue by financial year. If the financials and the tax returns don't match, that's not a negotiation. It's a walk.

Then normalise it. Add back what the owner runs through the business that you won't. Take out a proper wage for whoever runs it after you. Say the books show $280,000. Add $20,000 of the owner's personal costs. Take out a $50,000 manager. Real profit, $250,000. Skip the manager and you'd use $300,000, and offer $2 million for a business that can only carry $1.66 million. That's how people buy a job with a debt attached.

Check two. Interest. The $166,000 is principal and interest together. At 4% over ten years, it repays about $1.35 million of price. That's your real number. Still above any cash offer.

Check three. The levers. Term, rate, a payment holiday before the first instalment, and sometimes a balloon. My good friend Cam did two deals in 23 days, both 100% vendor finance, interest-only for ten years with balloons in years three and ten. Interest-only keeps the payments small. The balloon is the principal in one lump. Never sign one you haven't planned to pay, from cash you've built up or a bank refinance once you have a track record.

> **On screen:** Cam (Josh's friend): 2 deals in 23 days, 100% vendor finance · Results not typical; records on file.

**The verdict · 5:05**

Option B wins when three things are true. The profit survives normalising. The value is big enough to be worth the work, roughly a million and up. And the seller can wait to be paid. If they need every dollar on day one, you blend with a bank. That's episode 14.

Self-test. Can you name this business's real profit, why the seller is selling, and how long they can wait for their money? If not, you're not ready to make either offer.

One more rule. Never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time". Something like: "I can get you more than a cash buyer would, paid over time out of the business you built, and we can close in weeks, not months."

**THE NUMBERS · 6:00**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Real profit | $250,000 |
| Max annual debt service (÷ 1.5) | ~$166,000 |
| Buffer left in the business | ~$84,000 |
| Paid-over-time ceiling, 10 years | ~$1.66M (6.6×) |
| Price that repays at 4% | ~$1.35M |
| Cash buyer at 3–5× | $750K–$1.25M |

Even after interest you're about $100,000 above the top of the cash range. And the seller's money is doing the funding.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:05**

- Using the seller's profit instead of the normalised one. On these numbers that's about $330,000 overpaid.
- Opening with the ceiling. You've got nowhere to move, and the seller anchors on your top number.
- Signing a balloon with no refinance plan. The deal you won becomes the debt you can't pay.

So there are two paths. You can do this alone. My version of that was four failed deals and about a thousand cold calls before the first one closed. Or you can do it with someone who's already made those mistakes.

## EP 12 · Every DSCR From 0.96× to 2× (and Why I Sign at 1.5)

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Two buyers look at the same business making $300,000 a year. James wants it, so he stretches the debt until the seller's price fits. Chad won't sign below 1.5.

A year later, one big customer leaves. James is topping the business up from his own pocket. Chad's capital is still his.

Same business. One number. Here's every level of it, from 0.96 to 2.

> **On screen:** James vs Chad · Two illustrative buyers · Education, not financial or legal advice.

**STAKES · 0:25**

If you've got capital, this number decides whether a business pays you or you pay it. Get it wrong and the downside isn't a lower return. It's writing cheques into a business you bought to receive them.

This is for people who want to own businesses for the long run. If you're trying to squeeze every last dollar of debt out of a deal, this will annoy you. Good.

**THE FRAME · 0:55**

DSCR, debt service coverage ratio: the cash the business makes before debt, divided by every debt payment you owe in a year. Bank, seller, equipment finance, all of it.

There are four levels. 0.96, 1.2, 1.5 and 2. Most stretched deals sit on level two and look fine until the first bad year.

And if you've struggled to tell a safe deal from a dangerous one, it has nothing to do with your intelligence or how long you've invested. It has everything to do with what you're shown. Sellers and brokers show you the good year. Nobody hands you the bad one.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:40**

I help owners and investors structure deals that survive their worst year. Book a call below. Now the levels.

**One term first · The covenant · 1:50**

When a bank lends, it writes a covenant into the loan: a minimum DSCR, tested regularly. Every lender sets its own, so ask. For this video, say 1.25. Drop below it and you're in breach, and a breach hands the lender rights over your business you don't want them to have.

So here's how most people think about a deal. They think it's safe if it pays in an average year. That's not how it works. A deal is safe if it still pays in its worst year.

All the levels below use the same business. $300,000 profit in the base case. A bad year is profit down 20%, to $240,000. One customer leaves, or one key person walks.

**Level 1 · 0.96× · The business can't pay its own debt · 2:40**

James's deal. He stretches to $250,000 a year of payments on $300,000 of profit. In the bad year, $240,000 comes in and $250,000 goes out.

The business can't cover its own debt. He's $10,000 short, in breach, and explaining himself to a bank. That's the bottom of the ladder, and you get there from level two with one bad customer.

**Level 2 · 1.2× · Fine on a good year · 3:05**

Same deal, base case. $300,000 over $250,000 is 1.2. On a good year it pays, just. It's already under a 1.25 covenant, so a bank won't touch it, and it only gets done because the seller carries the debt without stress-testing it. Any dip and it's level one.

This is where most stretched deals sit. They start with the seller's price and work backwards until the debt fits. Then the add-backs grow and the bad year gets gentler until a 1.2 looks like a 1.5.

**Level 3 · 1.5× · The floor · 3:40**

$200,000 of payments on $300,000. For every dollar you owe, a dollar fifty comes in.

The real number is headroom: how far profit can fall before you breach. At 1.5 with a 1.25 covenant, profit can fall to $250,000. About 17%. A 20% bad year takes you to 1.2. Still a breach.

So 1.5 is the floor in the base case, not the target. It's the least that gives you a chance.

**[JOSH: one line on a deal you walked from because it failed 1.5.]**

**Level 4 · 2× · Change the structure, not the spreadsheet · 4:15**

Say the debt is a $1.2 million seller note at 4%. Over seven years, about $200,000 a year. That's level three.

Ask for ten years instead. About $148,000 a year. Same price. Same seller. Base DSCR about 2. Bad year about 1.6. You've bought a bad year just by asking for a longer term.

> **On screen:** $1.2M seller note at 4% · 7 years ≈ $200K a year · 10 years ≈ $148K a year · Illustrative

Other levers do the same job. A payment holiday at the start. An interest-only period, though watch interest cover, profit divided by interest alone, and the year the principal comes due. Part of the price as an earn-out. Or the seller rolling part of their value into shares. That's episode 14.

**The rule · 5:00**

1.5 in the base case. Above the covenant in the bad year. If the bad year fails, change the structure. Never the numbers.

Build the bad year from the actual business. Take out the biggest customer. Price in replacing the one person everyone relies on. Look at the worst of the last three years. Then add year-one takeover costs: systems, lawyers, a manager overlapping with the old owner.

Self-test. If you can't name the biggest customer and the DSCR without them, you're not ready to sign.

And once you own it, check one thing every month. Cash collected, minus costs, minus debt payments. Track DSCR on the last twelve months, so if it slides from 1.6 to 1.4, you see it months before the bank does.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:50**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Profit, base / bad year | $300,000 / $240,000 |
| Stretched, $250K a year: DSCR base / bad | 1.2× / 0.96× (−$10K) |
| 7-year note, $200K a year: DSCR base / bad | 1.5× / 1.2× (+$40K, breach) |
| 10-year note, $148K a year: DSCR base / bad | 2.0× / 1.6× (+$92K) |
| Covenant (example) | 1.25× |

Same business, same price. The term alone moves the bad year from minus $10,000 to plus $92,000.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:00**

- Starting from the seller's price and stretching the debt to fit. You win the deal and fund its first bad year.
- Leaving small debts off the bottom of the fraction. Equipment finance counts.
- Signing interest-only without testing the year the principal lands. The DSCR looks great until it doesn't.

**CLOSE · 6:20**

Or you can keep doing what James did. Keep starting with the seller's price. Keep letting the bad year get gentler. And keep finding out in the first bad quarter. Alone, you fall in love with a business and the spreadsheet starts lying for you. You need someone in the room who isn't in love with the deal.

If you're someone who'd rather walk from a deal than fund it from your own pocket, you've already decided.

Thirty days from now you'll be thirty days older either way. Either you'll still be looking at deals you can't quite judge, or you'll have a base case, a bad year and a floor you trust.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Most business owners have one bank, and they ring it the week they need money. They've got a deadline, the bank can smell it, and they get one answer.

Same business, same rule: one lender will lend you about $1.04 million, another about $1.34 million. You only know which if you asked before the deal.

Here's the system, in the order the bank sees it.

**STAKES · 0:30**

This is for owners who want a second and third business, not just one. If picking up the phone fifty times is a deal-breaker, it's not for you.

**THE FRAME · 0:55**

One line: interview fifty lenders before you need one, and go in as the interviewer.

Four parts, in the order the bank meets you. The email. The signature and the one-pager. The call. Then the hypothetical, which feeds the lender matrix.

And if a bank has said no to you before, it has nothing to do with your business or your credit. It has everything to do with asking one bank, at the worst time, for a favour.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:25**

I build lender lists and lender presentations with owners, so the money's lined up before the deal is. Book a call below. Now the teardown.

**1 · The email · 1:35**

A bank is set up to lend. That's the whole business. But every bank lends differently: sectors, ticket sizes, terms. One will say no to a deal another would happily fund.

So you write to the business banker, not the front counter. Big banks, regionals and specialist lenders. On LinkedIn, look for business banking manager or commercial lending.

> **On screen:** "I'm [name] with [company]. We're building an acquisition platform in [sector], targeting businesses doing $X to $Y of EBITDA. I'm interviewing commercial lenders to understand sector parameters and build relationships before bringing deals. Would you have 20 minutes?"

Notice the word "interviewing". No begging, no life story. You're someone with options, because you're about to have them.

**2 · The signature and the one-pager · 2:10**

The banker's second look is who's behind you. Put your board in your signature. "Board includes a former CFO and a sector operator" reads very differently from a name and a phone number. Send the one-page executive summary ahead: board, sector, size of business, roughly how you'll structure it. That's episodes 6 and 9 paying off.

Now, I know what you're thinking. "Banks don't lend to people like me." Christian Dawes was unemployed, with no higher education and a criminal record. His first deal was $2.4 million, and the bank funded it 100%. Over-financed, in fact. So the question was never whether banks lend to people like you. It's which bank, and on what file.

> **On screen:** Christian Dawes: $2.4M first deal, funded 100% by the bank · Results not typical; records on file.

**3 · The call · 3:00**

Five groups of questions. They're the columns of your matrix.

> **On screen:** 1. Sector · 2. Credit · 3. Structure · 4. Security and guarantees · 5. Process

Sector. How many deals have you done in this industry, at what size, and what worries you about it?

Credit. What DSCR and interest cover do you need? How do you calculate profit here, and what do you add back?

Structure. How much of the price will you lend, over how many years? Balloons? Standard covenants?

Security and guarantees. What do you take as security? What's your personal guarantee policy, under $5 million and over?

Process. Who makes the credit decision, you or a committee? How long from application to money? What do you need to see?

That last one matters more than people think. A banker who decides is worth ten who pass your file to someone you'll never meet.

Run your first five calls with lenders you care about least. Put the one you most want at number fifteen, when you sound like you've done this before. No reply? A short email a week later, then a call. Bankers are busy, not rude.

**4 · The hypothetical and the matrix · 4:15**

Then ask: "Say I brought you a healthcare business, profit around a million, buying at four times. How would you look at it?" Some give you a term sheet. Some give you a conversation. Both go in the matrix.

> **On screen:** Lender matrix · Lender · Contact · Sector appetite · Min DSCR · Amortisation · Balloons · PG policy · Decision: local or central · Timing · Notes

A no is a row, not a failure. "We don't do that sector" is data. By lender thirty you'll know the market better than most people who've bought businesses for years. Sort the rows: who likes your sector, who decides locally, who's flexible on guarantees. That's your top five. Self-test: if you can't name five lenders who like your sector and who decides at each, you're not ready to make an offer. One line: the bank funds about 60%, the seller funds about 40% by not taking it as cash.

> **On screen:** Price $5M (4× $1.25M) · Bank 60% = $3M ≈ $575K a year · Seller 40% = $2M · Illustrative · Education, not financial or legal advice.

Blends work best on businesses like radiology. Expensive equipment, referrals that keep coming. Asset-rich, steady, boring. That's what a bank lends against.

**#3 · Seller note, fully amortising · Fails · 2:20**

The seller lends you the $2 million. Seven years at 4%. About $333,000 a year.

Add the bank and you're paying about $910,000 a year on $1.25 million of profit. DSCR about 1.37. Under the 1.5 floor before anything goes wrong. It fails. Walk away from this version, not from the deal.

**#2 · Seller note, interest-only · Passes, with a catch · 2:40**

Same note, interest-only. About $80,000 a year. Total payments about $655,000. DSCR around 1.9. Passes.

The catch: the $2 million is still owed one day. That's a balloon. It works if you've planned how you'll pay it: cash built up, or a refinance once the business has a track record with you. Never sign one you haven't planned for.

**#1 · Rollover equity · 3:05**

The seller rolls their 40% into shares in the new company. No repayments to the seller at all. Your only debt is the bank's $575,000. DSCR about 2.2. Plenty of room for a bad year.

And the seller now wins when you win. They get most of their money at settlement, and a stake, so when you grow the business and sell it as part of a group, they get paid a second time. The bank likes it too: it's lending only part of the value, with the seller's money sitting behind its own. How big a stake they end up with is negotiation. Your accountant and lawyer build the share structure.

Here's why sellers say yes. A generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. Some will take paid over time. Some want cash now. A buyer with one structure loses every seller who wants cash. A buyer who can blend talks to both.

**Making #1 work · 4:15**

Three things people skip.

Minority rules. A seller with a stake is a minority shareholder. Drag-along, so if you sell the whole thing they come with you. No veto and no voting control for minorities. Set it from day one. It's very hard to add later.

> **On screen:** Minority holders · Drag-along · No veto or voting control · Set up from the start

The bank, early. The bank will want the seller's money behind theirs. Tell the seller up front, so it's not a surprise in legals. And if you can't agree on value, part of the price can be an earn-out, paid only if the business hits a target after you buy. One measure, ideally revenue, because it's harder to argue about.

The order. Agree the headline price first, because that's what sellers focus on. Then how it's paid. Then bring in the bank. And how you say it: not "vendor finance", not "rollover equity". "You get most of your money at settlement, and you keep a share, so when we grow it and sell the group, you get paid again."

Where it breaks. The seller wants 100% cash and won't hear anything else. The bank doesn't like the sector, which you find out in your lender matrix, not the week before settlement. Or the seller wants to stay in charge. Then they're not selling. Walk.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

There are two types of first-time business buyers. The ones who borrow the money and keep the company. And the ones who sell half of it to get started, and spend years working for their investors.

On an $8 million exit, that choice is worth about $4 million.

Debt ends. Equity you give away never comes back.

> **On screen:** "Debt ends. Equity doesn't." · Illustrative · Education, not financial or legal advice.

**STAKES · 0:25**

Your first deal sets the cap table for everything after it. If you're building toward a group and an exit, the share you keep is the most valuable thing you own. Give it away to avoid a few bank meetings and you've sold the exit before you've started.

This is for people who want to own the company, not a slice of it. If you want a lifestyle business, this series isn't for you.

**THE FRAME · 0:55**

One line: borrow the money, keep the company.

Four parts. The claim, in numbers. Why everyone raises anyway. The test for when equity is actually right. And the three protections that make debt safe.

And if your first instinct was to find a partner with money, it has nothing to do with being naive or scared. It has everything to do with how buying a business gets explained. Everyone talks about raising money. Nobody shows you the bill at the exit.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners build first structures they keep control of. Book a call below. Now the numbers.

**1 · The claim, in numbers · 1:40**

Round numbers, illustrative. A business makes $500,000 profit. You buy it at four times, $2 million.

Route one, investors. They put up the money and take half the company.

Route two, debt. Seller note or bank, seven years at 4%. About $333,000 a year. DSCR 1.5, right on the floor. Over seven years you repay about $2.33 million. The interest is about $330,000, and the business pays all of it.

Now you grow it. Profit gets to $1 million. You bolt it into a group and sell at eight times. $8 million.

Investor route: you get half. $4 million. The investors get the other $4 million, and they had a vote on when and how you sold.

Debt route: the debt's long gone. You keep the lot, less your board's equity.

Think of it like a house. A loan is renting money for a few years. Equity is selling a room in your house, forever, to someone who also gets a say in when you sell.

**2 · Why everyone raises anyway · 2:50**

Because raising feels safer. No repayments, someone else's risk. But it's slow. Months per cheque. And you give away equity, and with it control and the exit. A bank is set up to lend. Debt is easier to find than investors, and when it's paid, it's gone.

The other reason: people think you need to give away a big chunk to get serious people around you. Here's mine. My board was found entirely through LinkedIn and cold outreach. The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder. I still keep 73% of my company.

> **On screen:** Josh's company · Board found via LinkedIn and cold outreach · Josh keeps 73%

My board does get equity. Founders' equity, earned by helping build the company. That's paying for credibility, not cash. You can bring in serious people and keep control at the same time.

**[JOSH: one line on whether you were offered investor money early and why you said no.]**

**3 · The test · When equity is right · 3:45**

Debt is only safe inside the 1.5 rule. Debt the business can't comfortably cover isn't leverage. It's a trap.

So here's the test. If the debt won't fit inside 1.5 however you structure it, and the deal is good enough, a small amount of equity can close the gap. But try the longer term, the payment holiday, the blend and the earn-out first. Equity is the last tool, not the first.

And if you do bring investors in, give them economics, not control. Drag-along, no veto, set up from the start. A lender wants its money back with interest. An investor wants a share of everything, forever.

**4 · Three protections that make debt safe · 4:30**

One. Personal guarantees. A guarantee lets the lender come after you personally if the business can't pay. On larger deals, over about $5 million, you often don't need one. An asset-rich business gives the bank its security. That's typical, not universal. On smaller deals, expect to be asked. Don't just sign. Ask what else would satisfy them: tighter reporting, a cash reserve requirement, security over specific assets. Buy through a company set up for that deal, and have your lawyer read every line of what you're guaranteeing.

> **On screen:** Personal guarantees · Over ~$5M: often not required · Typically; every lender and deal is different.

Two. Working capital. Buying the business is one loan. Running it is another. Get a working capital line approved before settlement and drawn on the day. It pays fees at close, integration and runway. As a rough guide, often around 10% of revenue: about $300,000 on a $3 million business. My good friend Cam got $500,000 of working capital from the bank on his two deals. The word that matters is approved. Not "should be fine".

> **On screen:** Cam (Josh's friend): $500K working capital from the bank · Results not typical; records on file.

Three. A reserve floor. A number the business's cash never drops below. A few months of payroll and debt payments. No distributions under it. And never stack credit cards on top of a seller note to get through a bad month.
