### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

One business is easy to own. You know every number and every person.

Until you buy the second one. Then a structure you never thought about decides two numbers: how much the bank lends you, and what a buyer pays for the lot.

In the example I'll run today, that's the gap between four million and eight million. Same businesses. Different paperwork.

**STAKES · 0:20**

This is for owners who plan to own more than one business. If you'll only ever own one, a single company may be fine, and your accountant can tell you. Everyone else: the structure is cheap to set up before deal two and expensive to fix after deal four. It's also the first thing a lender and a buyer's diligence team look at.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice. Talk to your accountant and lawyer.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The whole idea in one line: one deal, one company, with a holding company on top.

There are four levels of structure, and most owners are stuck on level two without knowing it.

If your first business went into whatever company was handy, that has nothing to do with how careful you are. It has everything to do with timing. Nobody plans for deal five while they're scrambling to close deal one.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners set this up, from a first deal with little to no money down to a group a buyer will pay a premium for. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Level 1: the partnership · 1:45**

You and the business are the same thing legally. Every debt and every claim can reach you.

Rule: don't buy a business this way. Ever.

**2 · Level 2: one company · 2:15**

You own a company, the company owns the business. Fine for one deal.

The trap is deal two. Buy the second business into the same company and both now share one set of debts, one set of books and one set of risks. The bank sees one pile and takes security over the whole pile. A bad year in business two can now reach business one.

Most people think structure is admin you sort out after the deal. That's not how it works. Lenders price your structure. Buyers price it. It's part of the deal.

**3 · Level 3: holdco plus one company per business · 3:15**

This is where most groups should live.

A holding company sits on top. It's the permanent vehicle. Your board's founders' equity sits here. Every business you buy goes into its own new company underneath, with its own bank account, its own lender and its own seller note.

Why the lender cares: they want to see this business's profit paying this business's debt. Ring-fenced, they can lend to that company alone and take security over its assets, not over everything you own. Don't sign a guarantee where one company stands behind another's debt unless your lawyer has read it. That one clause turns five ring-fenced companies back into one pile.

Why the seller cares: in a vendor-finance deal, the seller's note sits in the company that owns their old business, limited to that company where you can get it, and behind the bank. Your lawyer will call that non-recourse and subordination. Ask them to show you both on your own structure.

Why the buyer cares: clean pieces. A buyer can take one business or all of them. Tangled, their diligence team spends months unpicking shared accounts, and every question they can't answer comes off the price.

**4 · Level 4: the octopus · 4:45**

Under the holdco you run arms. One per region or one per industry, each with its own manager, its own businesses, sometimes its own lenders. Lose an arm, the animal lives.

You won't need it for a while. The rule is to build level three so it can grow into level four without being torn down.

**5 · Day-one settings · 5:15**

Two things to set up before anyone else owns a share.

Issue plenty of shares. A million, not a hundred. It's far easier to hand a board member ten thousand out of a million than to reissue later.

Set minority rules from the start. Drag-along, so a minority can't block a sale of the group. No veto and no voting control for minorities. Board members, sellers rolling over equity and managers all sign up to the same agreement.

That's how I've done it. My board sits at the top. The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder. I keep 73% of my company. **[JOSH: one line on how your healthcare group is structured, e.g. holdco and number of operating companies, or cut.]**

The multiple gap is the roll-up. Buy at around four times, combine and grow, sell the group at eight, ten, sometimes fifteen. It only shows up if a buyer can see clean, comparable numbers. The structure is how you keep them that way.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical.

- Cross-guarantees signed at settlement without a lawyer reading them. Cost: one bad business can take down the rest.

- Fees between companies with no real service behind them. Cost: a tax problem. Management fees need real services at a fair price; your accountant decides the detail.

## EP 62 · Centralise vs keep local: the $50K back-office decision

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Three businesses. Three bookkeepers, three payroll systems, three insurance brokers.

How come owners with three good businesses often feel busier than when they had one? Because they're paying for the same work three times, and they're the glue holding it together.

In a three-business group, fixing that is worth about fifty thousand a year. At exit, about three hundred thousand. Customers never notice.

> **On screen:** 3 bookkeepers. 3 payroll systems. 3 brokers. ~$50K/yr. Illustrative.

**STAKES · 0:20**

This is for people who own two or more businesses, or are about to. The saving looks small at three. At five it's real money, because the same shared team absorbs each new business for a small extra cost. And at sale, a buyer sees one finance function and one set of books instead of five little kingdoms. If you're hoping a back office will fix a business that doesn't make money, it won't. Fix that first.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

One line: centralise what customers never feel, keep local what they pay for.

So every function in your group is Option A, centralise, or Option B, keep local. I'll give you both, the test, and the order.

If your back office is a mess right now, that has nothing to do with discipline. It has everything to do with how you got here: one deal at a time, each business arriving with its own setup.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners build groups like this, starting from a first business bought with little to no money down. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Option A: centralise · 1:45**

You were told every business needs its own bookkeeper, its own office manager doing payroll, its own broker. That was never a rule. It's how a standalone business has to run. Inside a group, it's a choice.

Centralise anything that's the same job in every business. Bookkeeping. Payroll. HR admin. Insurance. Compliance. Software. Buying.

One accounting system. One chart of accounts, so wages are wages and rent is rent in every business. One person or firm closing the month for all of them. That matters more than the saving. Until every business reports the same way, you can't compare their margins, and you can't see which one is quietly losing money. One payroll, with one set of contracts and policies, adjusted where the law or your industry's award needs it. One broker. Group buying on fuel, consumables, phones and software seats. Suppliers give better terms to a bigger customer, and now you are one.

**2 · Option B: keep local · 3:00**

Keep anything a customer would notice changing. The person who answers the phone. The tech on the tools. The clinician. The local name on the van.

That's what you bought. Don't rename a forty-year-old local business after your holdco because it looks tidy on a slide. Centralise the engine behind marketing if you like, one person running ads and reviews for every business, but the customer still sees the local name.

**3 · The test · 3:45**

Ask one question of every function: would a customer notice if this changed tomorrow?

No: centralise it. Yes: leave it local.

Then a second question for anything you centralise: will the person who uses the tool help pick it? Let the people who'll use a system test two options before you buy. Don't put the fanciest software on a twelve-person plumbing business because it looked good in a demo.

The usual pushback is "my businesses are too different to share anything." Every business pays wages, does its books and buys insurance. Start there. The other one is "my staff will hate it." Some will, if you do it in one go without telling them. Do it one function at a time and explain why.

**4 · The order · 4:30**

Books first. You can't run a group whose numbers arrive in five different shapes. Then payroll and HR, where staff notice change, so tell them what's changing and what isn't before you do it. Then insurance and buying. Marketing last, and carefully.

Verdict: both, in that order, one function at a time. Not before you have two businesses running steadily. Never all four in the same month.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:15**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Revenue: A / B / C | $4M / $3M / $1M |
| Back office before, each paying its own | $140K + $100K + $50K = $290K |
| Shared team at holdco (controller, bookkeeper, payroll/HR) | $240K |
| Split by revenue share | $120K / $90K / $30K |
| Saving | $50K a year |
| Value at a 6× exit multiple | ~$300K |

Fifty thousand back in profit, plus one set of numbers you can trust. And notice it isn't huge on day one. That's normal. The bigger win comes at business four and five. A fourth business on its own would need its own bookkeeper and payroll. Inside the group, the same team takes it on with a small top-up, not a new hire. Every dollar it doesn't spend on back office is a dollar of profit, and every dollar of profit is six at exit.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical.

Groups often charge this through a management fee from the holdco. It has to cover real services at a fair price, and how it's taxed depends on where you are.

> **On screen:** Management fees: real services, fair price. Education, not financial or legal advice. Talk to your accountant and lawyer.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:15**

- Centralising everything in one month. Cost: every business stops working properly for a month, and staff leave.
- Buying software because a friend uses it. Cost: a migration, then a rollback, paid twice.
- Taking the one competent office manager out without a plan. Cost: the hundred small things she did that nobody wrote down.

## EP 64 · 4 mistakes when buying your competitor (the $1.05M deal)

|---|---|
| **The money** | Buy a $300K-profit competitor for $1.05M paid over time and add about $390K a year to your profit, if you avoid four mistakes (illustrative). |
| **Hook** | "Two owners. Same town, same trade." |
| **For** | Trapped Owner |
| **Stage** | Closing → Grow · Season 2, Act 13 |
| **Runtime** | ~8 min |
| **Copy** | Chad vs James opener · That's Not How It Works · Two Paths → Identity Close → After Close · Shape B (ranked mistakes) |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Two owners. Same town, same trade.

James runs more ads, sends more quotes and works more hours every year, and still wins half his jobs against the business across the road on price. Lorenzo doesn't cut his price or work more hours. A few years ago he bought the business across the road.

In the deal I'll run today, that's one point oh five million, paid over time, for about three hundred and ninety thousand a year of extra profit. And four ways to wreck it.

> **On screen:** Illustrative characters and numbers. "James outworks his competitor. Lorenzo bought his."

**STAKES · 0:20**

Your competitor is the one business you already know almost everything about. That makes it the easiest deal to value. It also makes it the easiest to wreck, because they know you too. This is for owners with a running business who've thought, "I should just buy that lot down the road." If you want to buy them to punish them, stop. You'll overpay.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

One line: approach carefully, price on their profit as it stands, and plan the people before you close.

Most owners think the way to beat a competitor is to outwork them. In a boring local industry, with owners retiring and no successors, that's not how it works. Buying them gets cheap when you pay over time.

If you've been fighting the same rival for years, that has nothing to do with your effort. It has everything to do with a market that has two of you in it.

Here are the four mistakes, ranked by what they cost.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down, including the one across the road. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Mistake 4: handing over your secrets · 1:45**

Competitors know each other. Same suppliers, same association, same gossip. They'll take your call. They'll also wonder what you're after.

Be direct: "We've both been at this a while. Have you ever thought about selling? If you have, I'd like to be the first call."

Then protect yourself. Mutual NDA before numbers move either way. Ask for their three years of financials, matching tax returns and revenue by year. Release yours in stages. Before an LOI they need to know you can fund and close. They don't need your customer list, your pricing or your staff names.

A competitor who stalls on the NDA, then wants to "just have a coffee and compare notes", may be fishing. In some industries and sizes, a competitor deal can raise competition-law questions. Ask your lawyer early.

> **On screen:** Stage 1: NDA, their 3 documents, your credibility. Stage 2, after LOI: deeper diligence. Your customer list stays locked. Education, not financial or legal advice.

**2 · Mistake 3: losing the people · 3:00**

You'll have two of some roles. Two office managers. Two senior techs who both think they're the senior tech.

Decide before you close who stays and in which role. Meet their key people before settlement, with the seller's agreement. For the ones you can't lose, a retention bonus at around ninety days and again at a year is common. Small next to losing the person every customer asks for. Your staff are watching how you treat theirs. So is the town.

Then the culture. You're not just changing owners. You're merging two teams who spent years competing for the same jobs. Pick one way of doing things, explain why, and give it time. The staff are most of what you're buying.

**3 · Mistake 2: assuming every customer stays · 3:45**

Some customers used both of you on purpose, so they always had a second option. They'll find a new one.

Ask for revenue by customer under the NDA and match it against yours at the right stage, with your accountant handling the comparison if the seller's nervous. Revenue from customers you already serve is worth less to you than it looks. And if one big customer is a large share of both businesses, the merged business has a bigger concentration problem than either did alone.

**4 · Mistake 1: paying for your own savings · 4:30**

This is the expensive one.

Price the deal on their profit as it stands. Three to five times. Count only the savings you control: one premises instead of two, one back office, one set of software and insurance. Treat extra sales from cross-selling as a bonus. Never in the price, never in the lender's model.

Check cover on their numbers alone at one and a half times, before any savings. The savings are your margin of safety, not your plan. Paid over time, payment holiday if you can get it, its own company under your holdco.

And never say "vendor finance" to them. Say "getting paid over time". A retiring owner who doesn't want to wait sixteen weeks for a bank often prefers a buyer who knows the trade, will look after the staff, and can close in weeks. If they won't move on price, your best tactic is the one most owners won't use: walk away. Second best is a time limit on the offer.

|---|---|
| Your profit | $400K |
| Competitor's profit | $300K |

| Savings you control: premises + back office | $80K + $60K = $140K |

**STAKES · 0:20**

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

One line: the rules don't change at deal three, but the bank's offer tries to change them.

If it feels like you don't have the capital to buy more, that has nothing to do with your savings or your salary. It has everything to do with what your businesses can now prove.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

**1 · Why the bank says yes now · 1:45**

You were told the bank decides how big you get, and that each new deal needs more of your own money. That's not how a group gets funded. The businesses fund the businesses.

On deal one you borrowed credibility from a board. By deal three you've got two businesses of monthly numbers, a lender who's watched you hit them, and sellers who've been paid on time. That's why deal three usually moves faster than deal one.

**2 · What the offer costs · 2:45**

The bank says: "We'll refinance A and B and fund part of C in one facility. Lower rate, bigger limit. We just want security over all three, and the loans cross-default."

Cross-collateral means security over all your businesses. Cross-default means if one business breaks its loan terms, every loan is treated as in default.

Separately, a bad year in C is C's problem. C's lender and C's seller talk to you about C. A and B keep running. In one group facility, C's bad year is everyone's problem.

**3 · How to take the money without the knot · 3:45**

First, every new deal stands on its own numbers anyway. If it only works because the bank lends across the group, it doesn't work.

Second, push back on security. Common fallbacks: security only over the business being funded; security over named assets, not everything; or a higher cover covenant, say one point seven five, in exchange for less security.

Third, check the seller notes. They usually sit behind the bank. Make sure a group facility doesn't change that in a way the sellers' documents don't allow, and that a default in one place doesn't trigger defaults in your seller agreements.

Fourth, personal guarantees. On larger deals, over about five million, one often isn't needed, because the business's assets give the bank its security. Typically. Every lender and deal is different. Ask again at every deal.

> **On screen:** PGs often not required over ~$5M. Typically; every lender and deal is different.

**4 · Make lenders compete · 4:45**

Lenders lend on trust, and trust is a forecast you hit. Every quarter, send each lender what you said each business would do, what it did, and why. A year of that and your next credit application half writes itself.

I did over thirty million dollars of deals in banking by twenty-one. **[JOSH: one line from your banking years on what made a lender say yes to a repeat borrower, or cut.]**

> **On screen:** Josh: $30M+ in deals in banking by 21. Results not typical; records on file.

Keep two or three banks that know your group, plus your sellers. Keep updating the ones you didn't use. Competition is your best rate. And never fill a cash gap in one business with a credit card or a short-term loan. Keep a reserve, separate.

Two objections I hear. "Cross-collateral is standard, I can't avoid it." Sometimes you can't. You can usually limit it. Ask. And "my bank's been great, I trust them." Good. Trust them and read the documents anyway. Bank staff change. Clauses don't.
