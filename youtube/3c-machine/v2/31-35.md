Four layers.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

> **On screen:** You / family trust → HoldCo → Company 1, Company 2, Company 3… Education, not financial or legal advice.

**1 · The top: you and the holding company · 1:40**

At the top is you, often through a family trust or your own company. Under that, the holding company: the permanent vehicle. Your board holds its founders' equity here.

Two things to set on day one. Issue a lot of shares, a million not a hundred, so handing a director ten thousand is easy later. And give minority holders drag-along, with no veto and no voting control. If a director with three per cent can block a sale, a buyer will price that in.

I keep 73% of my company. **[JOSH: one line on how your healthcare group's holding structure is set up.]**

> **On screen:** Josh's own company. Results not typical; records on file.

**2 · One company per deal · 2:50**

Every business you buy goes into its own new company under the holding company. One deal, one company. Its own bank account. Its own facilities.

That's the ring fence. If business seven has a bad year or a lawsuit, it stays in business seven. Lose one arm, the octopus lives. And each company's numbers are clean on their own, which is how a buyer wants to see them.

Once you're across several industries or regions, add one more level: separate arms under the holding company, each with its own businesses, its own manager and its own lenders.

Read any cross-guarantee with your lawyer. On larger deals, often over about five million, an asset-rich business can give the bank its security without a personal guarantee. Typically. Every lender and deal is different.

**3 · The shared back office · 3:50**

Split every business in two. The front wins and serves customers: sales, service, the team on the tools. That stays local.

The back is the same everywhere: bookkeeping, payroll, HR, insurance, legal, compliance. Move it up to the holding company and run it once for all of them. That's where a group gets cheaper to run than its parts. One set of systems also means your Monday numbers arrive in the same format from every business.

Build it in order. First one strong finance person who can run the books for three or four businesses. Then, as the group grows, split that job into specialists: a controller, a head of people, eventually your own legal and compliance. Hire slowly, and hire people who've done it in bigger companies.

**4 · A manager in every business · 4:40**

Each business gets a general manager who owns its results. Not you. Not the seller, long term. Often the best GM is already inside the business: the senior person staff go to when the owner isn't there.

Set the numbers before they start. Never let a senior hire write their own targets. Pay a base, plus a bonus tied to that business's profit and a few of its Monday numbers.

Then your job fits on one line. Read the numbers every Monday. Meet each GM once a month. Run a quarterly board. Keep lenders and the sellers you're still paying informed. Find the next deal. You allocate capital and choose people. The group gets easier once every GM is better at running their business than you would be.

A GM in every business and a back office at the holding company is what makes this check pass. So does size and stability: no single customer carrying the place, and profit that doesn't swing year to year.

**5 · Check 5: numbers they can verify · 4:10**

The buyer's accountants run a quality-of-earnings review. Every add-back gets tested: owner expenses, one-offs, things you say won't happen again. Anything they can't verify comes out before they multiply.

And if your books aren't ready for that, it's not your fault. It has nothing to do with being careless. It has everything to do with books kept for the tax return, not for a buyer. Nobody tells owners the difference until a buyer does.

They'll also ask where every dollar of revenue came from. Which channel, which referrer, which contract. If you can't show it, they can't see where growth comes from, so they won't pay for it.

You checked the seller's quality of earnings on the way in. One day someone checks yours. I spent my early years in banking and I've been involved in more than a hundred million dollars of transactions. The buyer's side tests every number, and the ones that can't be proven don't get paid for.

How come smart, capable people find it so hard to buy their first business? They have the salary, the skills, and can read a set of accounts better than the owner they'd buy from.

The expensive mistakes aren't the deals that fall over. I had four of those. The expensive ones are quiet: waiting, thinking, and the people you let near your decisions.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The idea in one line: in this game, time costs more than mistakes. Most people only count active risk, the chance a deal goes wrong. They never count passive risk: doing nothing while the years go by anyway.

What I had was volume. Around a thousand cold calls. Four deals that fell over. Then a physio clinic: 100% vendor finance, seven years at four per cent, a nine-month payment holiday, rent in arrears, no deposit. I pulled a hundred and fifty thousand out in year one.

**2 · Mistake 1: waiting for perfect · 2:40**

There is never a perfect moment. Decide at sixty per cent. When you have sixty per cent of the information and the deal passes the rules, move. The other forty is what due diligence is for.

And the market has moved. A generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. They don't want to wait sixteen weeks for a bank. They want out, and to be looked after. Paid over time, a deal can close in two to four weeks. Every month you wait, those owners sell to someone else.

Count both risks. Active risk is real: you might buy a deal that struggles. The rules exist to manage it. Passive risk is real too: another year in the same job, no ownership, and a business's profit paying down someone else's debt instead of yours.

**3 · Mistake 2: analysis instead of action · 3:40**

This one gets engineers, accountants and consultants. A beautiful model of a deal they haven't found. Six months reading about vendor finance. No cold calls.

And if that's you, it's not your fault. It has nothing to do with your intelligence or your discipline. It has everything to do with a habit your career trained into you: learn first, act second. It gets you promoted. In deals, it keeps you at zero.

So here's the test. You've been watching for months. How many offers have you sent?

If it's zero, you don't have a knowledge problem. I've had people pay twenty to thirty thousand for advisory and still not take action. Money wasn't the issue. Where their energy went was.

**4 · Mistake 3: the wrong people · 4:50**

On your board, friends and family who've never done a deal make you feel supported. They don't make a bank say yes. No relevant experience, no seat.

I had no network at 25. I found my whole board through LinkedIn and cold outreach, and the founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is now a shareholder. The right people are reachable. You just have to ask them, with equity instead of cash.

In your advice, check who's actually done the thing before you learn from them. That includes me. Ask what I've done.

In your evenings, if everyone around you thinks buying with no money down is a scam, you'll start to believe it. Change the room. It's easier than changing yourself.

**5 · The rule · 5:30**

Set a quota before you allow yourself more research. Fifty calls this week. One real NBIO to a real seller this month. Hit the number, then read whatever you like.

Careful is having rules: one and a half times cover, the three documents, walk away when it's wrong. Waiting is having no deadline.

Where this doesn't apply: if a deal fails the rules, walking away isn't waiting. That's the best negotiating tactic you have. The quota is for offers, not for closing bad deals.
