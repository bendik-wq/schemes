## EP 44 · 4 reasons sellers walk before settlement (ranked)

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | A seller paid over time can net $1.66M instead of a $1M cash offer (illustrative), so when they walk it's rarely price. Find the real reason in the first meeting and the deal survives. |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

There are only two types of buyers. The ones who get the seller to settlement day, and the ones who get a phone call the week before.

The difference has nothing to do with price. A seller rarely pulls out on the day they pull out. They pull out weeks before. They just tell you on the day.

> **On screen:** A signing page. The pen never moves.

**STAKES · 0:20**

Here's why price is rarely the reason. Take a business making $250,000 profit. A cash buyer at four times offers about $1 million. Paid over time inside the 1.5 rule, the seller gets about $166,000 a year. Over ten years that's $1.66 million. Illustrative.

When a seller walks from that, something other than money got louder. If you've taken one business as far as it goes and the next step is buying a second, this is the episode that keeps that deal alive.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The idea in one line: the objection at the end is a question you didn't ask at the start.

Sellers walk for four reasons. I'll rank them from least to most dangerous, by how late they show up and how hard they are to fix. Staff. Advisers. Remorse. Family.

You were told sellers sell to the highest bidder. You were told the price is the deal. You were told a seller who signs an LOI has decided. None of that is the full story. A lot of sellers sell to whoever makes them feel safe about their name, their family and their people. Every one of those is something you can deal with.

So if a seller has pulled out on you, it has nothing to do with your offer being too low or you being too young. It has everything to do with a conversation nobody taught you to have in the first meeting.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:40**

I help people run their deals so the seller's still at the table on settlement day. Book a call below. Now the four.

**4 · Staff · 1:50**

Owners often care about their people more than they let on. A deal can die because the owner pictures telling the team.

The fix: show them the plan for the staff before they ask. You keep the team. You meet key people before close, with the seller's permission, at the right time. Retention bonuses for key staff can go into the structure.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice.

It's last on the list because it's the easiest to fix. Once the seller knows their people are looked after, it usually goes away.

**3 · Advisers · 2:35**

The seller's accountant, lawyer, or a mate at the golf club says, "You could get more than that."

Sometimes they're right. More often they're comparing your offer to an all-cash price that doesn't exist for this business.

The fix: get the adviser in the room early and walk them through the maths. A higher multiple paid over time out of profit looks very different once someone explains it properly. And never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time."

**2 · Remorse · 3:20**

*[B-roll: an older owner walking through an empty workshop after hours.]*

Thirty years. Their name on the van. Their number on every invoice. Then someone tells them, nicely, that in six weeks it won't be theirs.

That isn't about money. It's about who they are on Monday morning.

The fix: ask about life after the sale in the first meeting. "What does your week look like a year after this?" If they can't answer, you've found your biggest risk. Then build them a role. A transition, typically six months to three years, with a title and dates. A seller with somewhere to go is far less likely to walk.

**1 · Family · 4:10**

The most dangerous one is the person you never met. A spouse who wants the cash now. A son or daughter who assumed they'd inherit it. A sibling who owns 20% that nobody mentioned.

It's first because it surfaces last, when they can only say no.

The fix is one question, early. "Who else has a say in this? Who would you talk to before you signed?" Then you meet them. Over coffee in week two, not at the signing.

**[JOSH: optional: one of your own deals where a seller pulled out late, and which of these would have caught it. Cut if none died this way.]**

**The five questions · 4:50**

Every reason shows up in the first or second meeting, if you ask.

> **On screen:** The five questions, one per line

"What would you do with your week after this?" That's remorse.

"Who else has a say?" That's family.

"What worries you about your team?" That's staff.

"Who do you trust for advice on this?" That's advisers.

"When do you want this done, and why then?" That's timing.

You're not there to overcome the objection. You're there to understand it, before it's an objection.

**Keep the clock tight · 5:30**

Time feeds all four. The longer from yes to settlement, the louder they get.

First, an exclusivity period in the LOI, often 60 to 90 days. Then diligence, 90 days at most. And once diligence is done, legals, about four weeks. Push the lawyers on scope and dates, so fees don't creep and the deal doesn't drift.

And run more than one deal. A seller can feel the difference between a buyer who needs this deal and one who doesn't. If a seller says "there are other buyers," most of the time there aren't. Stay calm. The buyer who can walk away has the most leverage.

| Over 10 years | about $1,660,000 |

The seller is better off on paper. So when they walk, look for what got louder, not for a higher number.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:45**

- **Meeting the family at the signing.** By then they can only say no.
- **Arguing with the adviser.** Explain the structure instead. An adviser who understands it often becomes your best seller.
- **Letting diligence drift.** Every week past the date is another week for remorse, family, staff or advisers to get louder.

Or you can keep doing what most buyers do. Keep focusing on the bank and the numbers and never meet the seller's spouse. Keep arguing with the adviser. Keep running one deal, so the seller can tell, and the price starts moving.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

Everyone buying a business gets the same advice. Hire a good accountant, do your due diligence, and you'll be fine.

The only problem is, by the time diligence starts, most dead deals are already dead. Nobody's told you yet.

So instead of one big check at the end, here's one page you run at every stage. Five gates. If a line goes red, you fix it, price it in, or walk.

> **On screen:** Dead-deal checklist · 5 gates · one line goes red

**STAKES · 0:20**

The money here is what you don't overpay for. Miss one line and you can own a business whose cash flow covers its repayments 1.33 times instead of 1.5. That's one slow quarter from trouble, with your capital in it.

This is for people with capital who want a process they can run every time. If you'd rather go on gut, this won't suit you.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The idea in one line: a checklist read once at the end is a post-mortem. Run at every stage, it's a filter.

Five gates, in the order a deal moves. Credibility. Capital. Before the offer. The offer and diligence. Legals and settlement.

Every line gets one of three actions. Fix it. Price it in. Or walk. Decide which before you're emotionally in the deal.

And if you've ever missed a red flag, it has nothing to do with intelligence or how carefully you read. It has everything to do with when you looked.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people run this list on real deals, so the red line shows up in week one, not month four. Book a call below. Here's the list.

**Gate 1 · Credibility · 1:40**

First you check your own side.

Red flag: your board is friends and family. If they've no relevant deals or exits, a bank and a seller can tell. Fix it before you make offers.

Red flag: no engagement letters. Advisers who said "happy to help" aren't a team. Get deferred or rolled fees in writing.

Red flag: you've never spoken to a lender. If the first time a bank hears of you is with a live deal, you're asking, not choosing.

**[JOSH: one Credibility gap that helped kill one of your early deals, in one line. Otherwise cut.]**

**Gate 2 · Capital · 2:30**

Next, the money.

Red flag: the deal doesn't clear 1.5. Free cash flow covers repayments less than one and a half times. That's not a deal. That's a slow month away from trouble.

Red flag: no working capital line approved before settlement. You can close and run out of cash in the same week.

Red flag: too small to carry itself. Under about a million in value, there often isn't enough cash flow to pay the seller and pay you.

Red flag: a personal guarantee you didn't plan for. Above about $5 million, banks often don't need one. Typically. Every lender and deal is different. Know where you stand before the term sheet.

> **On screen:** Typically; every lender and deal is different. · Education, not financial or legal advice.

**Gate 3 · Before the offer · 3:30**

Here's why this gate matters more every year. A generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. Clinics, trades, services. When that many owners want out, the scarce skill isn't finding a deal. It's knowing which ones to drop, early.

Red flag: one deal in your pipeline. You'll overpay and ignore the signs. Run several.

Red flag: the seller won't send three years of financials, the matching tax returns and revenue by year. That request is the motivation test. No documents, no offer.

Red flag: the financials and the returns don't match. Not a negotiation point. Walk.

Red flag: a broker process with inflated numbers and "other buyers interested." Most of the time there aren't. Stay calm.

**Gate 4 · The offer and diligence · 4:25**

In the LOI, red flag: no conditions. Financing approval, quality-of-earnings sign-off, the lease, licences, key staff and the seller's transition should all be in there. No exclusivity period is another.

> **On screen:** Flag · Severity · Action

In diligence, the big ones. One or two customers above about 30% of revenue. Above half, walk or discount hard. Gross margin slipping behind accounting changes. Tax debt or liens. Key contracts that can't move to a new owner. Licence or compliance problems in a regulated industry. Very high staff turnover.

And a seller who wants out the day you settle, with no transition. High severity. Walk.

**Gate 5 · Legals and settlement · 5:15**

Red flag: legals drifting past about four weeks. Fees creep and sellers get cold feet. Keep scope tight and dates clear.

Red flag: the lease isn't assigned or renewed.

Red flag: last episode's seller signs. Remorse with no plan for their week after. A family member you've never met. An adviser saying they could get more.

And the lines my own dead deals added to the list.
