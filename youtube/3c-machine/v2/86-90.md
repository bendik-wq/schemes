### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

How come buyers with real capital still overpay for businesses?

They've got money, advisers and patience. But when the seller also owns the building, a lot of them miss one line in the accounts. In the example I'll show you, that line is worth $240,000.

Here are the four mistakes, cheapest to most expensive.

> **On screen:** One rent line = $240K at 4× · Illustrative

**STAKES · 0:20**

In owner-run businesses, the owner often owns the property too. That changes the profit you're paying a multiple on, the cash you need at settlement, and how keen the seller is to carry finance. Get it right and the building helps fund your deal. Get it wrong and you pay for profit that doesn't exist. This is for buyers who want to operate businesses. If you'd rather be a landlord, that's a different game.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

When the seller owns the building, the lease is part of the price. Four mistakes, ranked.

When a buyer overpays here, it has nothing to do with being careless or bad with numbers. It has everything to do with accounts prepared by an owner who is also the landlord. They were never built for a buyer.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:15**

I help buyers structure deals where the property is part of the picture. If your target's owner also owns the building, book a call below. Now the detail.

**Mistake 1 · Missing the above-market rent · 1:30**

The cheapest mistake is the one you miss in your favour. Some owners charge their own business above-market rent, because it's a tax-effective way to pay themselves. Profit looks worse than it is. Normalise it and you can be the buyer who sees value the others don't. That's money left on the table, not lost. It's still a mistake.

**Mistake 2 · Treating the lease as paperwork · 2:15**

When the seller is your landlord, the lease is negotiated in the same conversation as the price. That's leverage.

Rent in arrears: you pay after the month, not before. On my first deal, a physio clinic, the rent was in arrears. No deposit, a nine-month payment holiday on the vendor finance, and rent paid after the month.

No bond: cash at settlement you don't have to find. A deferral: six months' rent deferred and paid back over two years. At $60,000 a year, that's $30,000 that stays in the business while you settle in. And a long lease with options, because lenders won't lend comfortably against a short one.

How to ask: agree the headline price first. Then raise the lease. "Given the transition, I'd like rent in arrears, no bond, and the first six months deferred. You still get every dollar."

> **On screen:** First deal: rent in arrears, no deposit, 9-month holiday. Results not typical; records on file.

> **On screen:** Arrears · No bond · Deferral · Long term + options

**Mistake 3 · Pricing on profit with no rent in it · 3:45**

Illustrative. A business shows $300,000 of profit. The owner owns the building and charges the business nothing. Market rent is $60,000 a year.

Once you own it, you pay rent. So real profit is $240,000. At four times, that's $960,000, not $1.2 million. A $240,000 swing from one line.

Before any offer you want three years of financials, matching tax returns and a revenue breakdown. Add one question: who owns the building, and what does the business pay for it? Then get a local agent's market-rent opinion. Your coverage maths has to include the rent too. If it only works with no rent, it doesn't work.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal.

> **On screen:** $300K shown − $60K market rent = $240K real profit. At 4×: $1.2M vs $960K.

**Mistake 4 · Buying the building · 4:45**

Buyers with capital think the building is the safe part. Property is the real asset, the business is the risky bit, so owning both is conservative.

That's not how it works. The business is the asset. Cash flow pays everyone, and cash keeps a newly bought business alive. Tie that cash up in bricks early and you've slowed the part that builds value: cash flow now, and multiple arbitrage when you sell a group.

And the seller keeping the building works in your favour. Sell the business, keep the property, and they have two incomes: your payments and your rent. The rent only keeps coming if the business keeps going. So their retirement depends on you doing well. That's alignment you can't buy.

It also shrinks what you fund. If you're not buying the property, you're not borrowing against bricks. The price is just the business. That's a smaller number for the seller to carry and a smaller number for a bank to look at.

Default: buy the business, lease the building, revisit the property in a few years. You'll also hear "sale-and-leaseback": sell a property to an investor and lease it straight back. Cash now, rent for years. It only makes sense if the rent still fits inside 1.5 coverage.

> **On screen:** Sale-and-leaseback: cash now, rent for years. Education, not financial or legal advice.

Priced on real profit, the deal clears 1.5 comfortably and the seller still gets about $184,000 a year from you. Priced on the flattered number, you've paid $240,000 for rent you'll be paying anyway.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:45**

- Closing without the lease signed. The seller can raise the rent once you own it. Set rent reviews and options in writing first.
- A short lease with no options. The bank won't lend against it, and the deal stalls at finance.
- Forgetting the rent in your coverage. Seller payments plus rent, against what the business really makes. If it only clears 1.5 with free rent, it doesn't clear.
- A third-party landlord ignored. The lease still has to be assignable and long enough for a lender. You just have less to negotiate.

A newly bought business usually runs out of working capital before it runs out of profit. Wages, suppliers, the gap between doing the work and getting paid. Fund that from your pocket and you've put your own money in after all. Fund it from investors and they get a say in your company. Asset-based lending funds it from the business's own balance sheet. It suits businesses that invoice other businesses on terms. It doesn't suit a café.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

Asset-based lending, ABL, is a line of credit secured on the business's receivables and stock. The lender advances a percentage of what's eligible. That total is the borrowing base.

When a newly bought business runs dry in month four, it has nothing to do with the new owner's effort. It has everything to do with a structure that funded the price and forgot the working capital.

Three parts: why the receivables are the security, how the base is worked out, and the test.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help buyers put the full stack together: seller, bank, working capital. If your target carries big receivables or real stock, book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · The lender isn't lending against you · 1:45**

Before you know how a lender reads a business, receivables look like admin. Money that turns up eventually. After you know, they're a facility. Every invoice under 90 days is borrowing power. Every invoice that ages past it is borrowing power you just lost.

That's why this works for a buyer with no track record. The lender is looking at the invoices, the stock and your board. It's still credibility first, but the asset carries most of the weight.

It suits manufacturers, distributors, logistics, trades doing commercial work on 30 or 60 days. On bigger deals, roughly over $5 million, banks often don't need a personal guarantee, because an asset-rich business gives them their security. Typically. Every lender and deal is different.

> **On screen:** Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice.

**2 · How the borrowing base works · 3:00**

Illustrative. A distributor turns over $8 million and makes $800,000 of free cash flow.

Receivables are $1.2 million. Lenders usually exclude very old invoices, say over 90 days, money owed by related parties, and anything above a limit for one big customer. Say $1 million is eligible. Advance rates on receivables are often around 70 to 85%. Use 80%: $800,000.

Stock is $1 million. It's harder to sell if things go wrong, so the rate is lower. Use 40%: $400,000.

Borrowing base: about $1.2 million. It's a ceiling, recalculated every month as receivables and stock move.

Use it for working capital first. A working capital line at settlement is often around 10% of revenue. Here, about $800,000. My good friend Cam got $500,000 of working capital from the bank on top of 100% vendor finance. That's the job this line does.

Then, maybe, part of the price. Some buyers draw on the line to pay part of the price so the seller carries less. It can work, but only with real headroom. If you spend the line on the price, there's nothing left for the slow month.

> **On screen:** Illustrative. Every lender sets its own advance rates and eligibility rules.

> **On screen:** Cam, Josh's friend: $500K working capital from the bank. Results not typical; records on file.

**3 · The test · 4:15**

Before you count on a line, answer three questions about the target.

What are the debtor days? If customers pay in 75 days and the cut-off is 90, one slow quarter pushes invoices out of the base.

What share of receivables is the biggest customer? One customer at a third of the ledger gets capped, and if they stop paying, the line wobbles.

Who owns collections after settlement? With ABL, your receivables ledger is the loan. Collections stops being admin and becomes a finance job. Somebody owns the aged-debtors report every week.

If you can't answer all three, you don't know the size of your line yet. And if the target has almost no receivables or stock, ABL isn't your tool. Vendor finance and a normal working capital line are.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:00**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Receivables | $1.2M |
| Eligible (under 90 days, no related parties, within concentration cap) | $1.0M |
| Advance at 80% | $800K |
| Stock × 40% | $400K |
| Borrowing base | ~$1.2M |
| If $200K of invoices age past 90 days | Line falls by $160K |

The line moves with the business. When sales grow, it grows. When sales drop, it shrinks, right when you'd want it most. So it never replaces the 1.5 rule. Seller payments, bank payments and ABL interest, against free cash flow. 1.5 minimum.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 5:45**

- Spending the line on the price. Then there's nothing for the slow month, and a bad quarter puts you over your limit.
- Springing subordination on the seller in week ten. An ABL lender wants first security over receivables and stock, so the seller's note ranks behind it. Explain it at the LOI: "The bank gets paid first on the invoices and stock. You still get every payment we agreed."
- Ignoring the costs and covenants. Interest on what you draw, set-up fees, sometimes an unused-limit fee. Break a covenant and the lender can freeze the line.

**HOOK · 0:00**

There are only two types of buyers on bigger deals. The ones who fill the gap with debt and keep the company. And the ones who fill it by giving the company away.

**STAKES · 0:20**

Bigger deals are where the real money is. The roll-up is buying businesses at three to five times profit, combining and growing them, and selling the group at eight, ten, fifteen times. But on bigger deals the seller and the bank rarely reach the full price. How you fill the gap decides how much of that exit you keep. This isn't for a first-time buyer looking at a $500,000 business. Park it. It'll matter later.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

A deal is a stack of layers. Each one ranks in order of who gets paid first, and the further down you go, the more it costs. Five levels.

When owners stall at one business, it has nothing to do with ambition or work ethic. It has everything to do with only being shown two options: save more, or sell equity.

**Level 1 · Senior bank debt · 1:30**

**Level 2 · The seller note · 2:30**

The seller carries 30%: $3 million, interest-only at 5% for the early years. $150,000 a year. Often the cheapest layer of all, and the one most buyers under-use.

Before you reach for anything more exotic, push this one. A lot of people assume bigger deals need a big cheque. **Level 3 · Working capital · 3:30**

The line that keeps the business running, often around 10% of revenue, sometimes against receivables and stock, like last episode. It's runway, not price. Don't spend it closing the gap.

On a $10 million deal that's real money, and it's also where lenders look hardest at you. Your lender presentation has to show the line is sized to the business, not to the gap.

Bank plus seller is 80%. There's still a $2 million hole.

**Level 4 · Mezzanine · 3:45**

Mezzanine sits below the bank and above your equity. It's usually subordinated, ranking behind the bank. Often interest-only, principal due at the end. Sometimes part of the interest is added to the balance instead of paid, which helps cash today and costs you more later. Sometimes the lender wants a small slice of equity on top, called a kicker. Pricing varies widely. It's typically well above bank debt, often in the double digits.

Illustrative: $2 million at 14%, interest-only. $280,000 a year.

**Level 5 · Equity · 4:45**

Bottom of the stack. Last paid, all the upside. Fill the $2 million gap by selling equity and an investor might want, say, 40% of the company. Illustrative. Now they get a say in when and how you sell.

Run it to the exit. Say you grow the group and the equity is worth $20 million in five years. 40% of that is $8 million handed over. Five years of mezzanine interest is about $1.4 million.

Debt over dilution. Mezzanine is the most expensive debt you'll put in a deal, and it can still be the cheapest option on the table. Use it when three things are true: the cheaper layers are maxed out, selling equity would cost you control or most of the exit, and the business still clears 1.5 with the mezzanine in.
