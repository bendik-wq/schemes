## EP 81 · Every Way to Fund a $2.4M Manufacturer, From Debtors to the Seller

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

A $2.4 million manufacturer can carry about half its own price.

The invoices, the stock and the machines are security a lender can see. Most buyers never ask for it, so they put their own cash at the front of the deal.

Here's every layer a factory gets funded with, from the debtors to the seller.

> **On screen:** $2.4M purchase · ~50% against the balance sheet · Illustrative

*[B-roll: factory floor, forklift moving pallets, a stack of invoices on a desk]*

**STAKES · 0:20**

This is for people with capital to deploy, or a business behind them, who'd rather own cash flow with hard assets under it than sit in low-yield paper. Done right, the business funds its own purchase and your capital stays free for the next one. Done wrong, you've bought a shed full of problems and borrowed your own buffer to do it. If you want a four-hour week, a factory is the worst place you could look.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The whole idea in one line. In a manufacturer the lender isn't only asking "does profit cover the repayments?" It's asking "if this goes wrong, what can we sell?"

Answer both questions and most of the deal funds itself. There are four levels to the stack. Most buyers stop at level one.

If a bank has knocked you back on a deal like this, it had nothing to do with your CV or your savings. It had everything to do with asking a cash-flow lender a question only an asset lender answers.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:20**

I help owners and investors structure deals like this with little to no money down. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Level 1: The cash-flow lender · 1:30**

A cash-flow lender lends against profit. One rule decides how much: free cash flow has to cover all debt service at least 1.5 times. That's the 1.5 rule, and it covers every dollar of debt in the deal, bank and seller combined.

Most people think that's the whole conversation. Profit, a multiple, then a yes or a no. That's not how it works in an asset-heavy business.

Level one sets the ceiling on your repayments. It doesn't decide where the money comes from.

**2 · Level 2: Debtors and stock · 2:15**

Asset-based lending. ABL for short. The lender advances a percentage against what's already on the balance sheet.

Debtors first. The invoices customers owe the business. Usually only the good ones. Old or disputed invoices are often left out.

Stock second. Often advanced at a lower percentage, because it's harder to sell. Finished goods and raw materials can be treated differently to half-made product.

No percentages from me. Advance rates vary a lot from lender to lender. Ring two and ask for theirs.

> **On screen:** Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice.

**3 · Level 3: Plant and equipment · 3:00**

The machines. What they lend against depends on the valuation, the age, and how easy the machine is to resell. A common machine other plants want lends better than a custom line built for one product.

This is also where personal guarantees change. On bigger deals, roughly over $5 million, banks often don't need one, because an asset-rich business gives them their security. Typically. And if a lender wants security over everything, you have fallbacks. Security on specific assets only. Or partial security, say on the equipment but not the debtors and stock.

**4 · Level 4: The seller · 3:45**

Whatever the assets don't cover, the seller funds. Getting paid over time. Never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time."

Plenty of these sellers will listen. A founder started making one thing thirty years ago. Now it's 30 staff, a leased factory and a customer list built on handshakes, and nobody lined up to take over. Too small for most private equity, and a lot of individual buyers get scared off by the machinery. A thin buyer pool and a tired owner is exactly when "paid over time" sounds good.

One more thing the lender wants: credibility. Put someone who's run a plant on your board. Founders' equity, typically 2 to 10%, not a salary.

*[B-roll: an older owner walking the floor, hand on a machine]*

**THE NUMBERS · 4:45**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $5,000,000 |
| Profit, after paying a floor manager | $600,000 |
| Price at 4× profit | $2,400,000 |
| ABL against debtors and stock | $1,200,000 |
| Seller, paid over time | $1,200,000 |
| Max debt service ($600K ÷ 1.5) | $400,000 a year |
| Bank repayments + seller repayments | $250,000 + $150,000 |
| Headroom | $0 |

Exactly on the line. No headroom. So the maths tells you what to negotiate: a payment holiday on the seller's piece, a longer term, or a lower price. A payment holiday means payments start later and the full price is still paid.

And then the value. It's common to find small manufacturers that haven't repriced properly in years. Every dollar of price rise on the same volume is pure margin. Fix price first, then add the salesperson these businesses never had, then buy the competitor down the road. Buy at around 4 times, combine, and a group can sell at a higher multiple than any one plant.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:00**

- **Borrowing your own buffer.** Debtors and stock are your working capital. Use them to fund the price and that facility can't also carry a slow month. Line up a separate working capital line, and make sure it's there on the day of settlement.
- **One customer over about 30% of revenue.** Common in manufacturing. Check whether their contract survives a change of owner. Some don't.
- **Margin that's quietly slipping.** Material costs move. Ask for gross margin by product line for three years, not one blended number.
- **Deferred maintenance.** A seller planning to exit can stop spending on the machines. Profit looks better, then a machine dies in your third month. Compare three years of equipment spend with depreciation, and pay an engineer to walk the floor before you sign.

**CLOSE · 7:00**

Two paths from here. The hard one: you ask your usual bank, get a no, and decide factories can't be funded. Or you find an asset lender, spend all of it on the price, and run dry in month four. The other path: someone who's done the structures sits next to you, so you know which lender to ring, what to ask, and how to keep the buffer.

## EP 82 · 5 Mistakes That Overpay for a $4M Transport Business

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | On an illustrative $4M business, pricing on profit after truck finance turns a $1.6M headline offer into a ~$1.16M ceiling the cash flow can actually pay. |
| **Hook** | "How come owners who already run a business overpay for their second one?" |
| **For** | Trapped Owner |
| **Stage** | Closing · Season 3, Act 17 |
| **Runtime** | ~8 min |
| **Copy** | How Come opener · What You Were Told Was Lying · No Choice → Identity Close → After Close · Shape B (ranked mistakes) |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

How come owners who already run a business overpay for their second one?

They can read a P&L. They've survived the bad years. Then they look at a transport business doing $4 million and price it on the revenue.

Here are five mistakes that turn a $1.6 million offer into a deal the cash flow can't carry.

> **On screen:** $4M revenue · $400K profit · Illustrative

*[B-roll: depot at dawn, trucks warming up, a dispatcher on the phone]*

**STAKES · 0:20**

Transport is thin-margin, asset-heavy and full of tired owners. That's a buyer's market for anyone who reads the numbers properly, and a trap for anyone who doesn't. On today's example, getting it right is the difference between a $1.6 million offer and the roughly $1.16 million the business can actually pay a seller. This is for owners who want a second business that doesn't need them at the depot at four in the morning. If you won't read boring spreadsheets, skip this industry.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

One line. In a thin-margin business, small moves in cost are big moves in profit, so you price on profit after every debt, never on revenue.

If you've ever overvalued a business, it had nothing to do with being bad at maths. It had everything to do with how businesses get talked about. You were told a bigger top line means a bigger business. You were told the trucks in the yard are assets. You were told a twenty-year customer is a safe customer. None of that is true until you check it.

Five mistakes. Roughly in order of what they cost.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:20**

I help owners buy their second business with little to no money down, and check the numbers hold before they sign. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Mistake 1: Pricing on revenue · 1:30**

$4 million of revenue. $400,000 of profit. That's 10%. Thin margins are common here.

Ask for margin after the big three costs, fuel, drivers and repairs, by customer or by run. You'll often find one or two customers the business is quietly losing money on.

Rule: the offer is built on profit. Revenue is context.

**2 · Mistake 2: Forgetting the truck finance · 2:10**

This is the expensive one. Trucks are usually on equipment finance. When you buy the business, that finance either comes with it or has to be paid out. Either way it's debt service, and it eats the same profit you're using to pay the seller.

Run the 1.5 rule. Free cash flow has to cover all debt service 1.5 times. $400,000 divided by 1.5 is about $266,000 a year for all debt. Truck finance is $150,000 of that. About $116,000 a year is left for the seller. Over ten years, about $1.16 million.

That's your ceiling, not your opening offer. The truck loans are part of the price.

Get every finance agreement and payout figure before you write an offer. And check fleet age. Old trucks mean repairs now and replacements soon.

> **On screen:** $400K ÷ 1.5 = ~$266K · minus $150K truck finance = ~$116K a year to the seller · Education, not financial or legal advice.

**3 · Mistake 3: Modelling last year's fuel · 3:15**

Say fuel is $1.2 million a year. It goes up 5% and the contracts don't let you pass it on. That's $60,000. Profit drops from $400,000 to $340,000. A 5% move in one cost took 15% of profit.

So check how much revenue sits under written contracts, how long they run, and whether they pass on fuel increases. That matters more than the names on the customer list.

It's also your first move after you buy. Reprice the runs that lose money, or let them go. Put fuel clauses in every new contract. Once one depot runs without you, add bolt-ons: small fleets with retiring owners, one depot, one dispatch team.

**4 · Mistake 4: Buying the owner's handshakes · 4:10**

The owner started as an owner-driver and built it truck by truck. Plenty of the work sits on a handshake with a customer who's used him for twenty years. A handshake isn't nothing. But a bank can't lend against it, and the loyalty may leave with him.

If one or two customers are over about 30% of revenue, that's a red flag. Meet the big customers with the seller before close. Keep the seller in for the transition. And talk to the senior drivers yourself. Drivers can be hard to find and keep. Losing three in month one costs more than any price you chipped off.

Check the compliance record too. Safety, fatigue and maintenance logs. In regulated freight a bad record is a risk to the whole business, not just a fine.

> **On screen:** Operator licences, fatigue and safety rules vary. Check your local licensing rules.

**5 · Mistake 5: Starting with an empty tank · 5:10**

Customers often pay on 30 days or more. Fuel and wages go out every week. So you want a working capital line in place at settlement.

And negotiate a payment holiday on the seller's piece. Payments start later, full price still paid. The first months are when you're learning the business, and a slow patch then shouldn't break you. Sellers tend to agree once the headline price is settled.

My good friend Cam got a $500,000 working capital line from the bank on his deals. Different industry, same principle.

> **On screen:** Cam, Josh's friend. Results not typical; records on file.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:50**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $4,000,000 |
| Profit (10%) | $400,000 |
| Max debt service ($400K ÷ 1.5) | ~$266,000 a year |
| Existing truck finance | −$150,000 a year |
| Left for the seller | ~$116,000 a year |
| Over 10 years | ~$1,160,000 |
| Headline offer at 4× profit | $1,600,000 |
| Gap | ~$440,000 |

The headline offer is about $440,000 more than the cash flow can carry once the trucks are paid. And a 5% fuel move takes another $60,000 a year off the profit that whole stack rests on.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:30**

- Finding the fleet debt late in due diligence, after the price is agreed. Ask for it before the offer.
- Assuming the twenty-year customer stays. Their loyalty was to the owner.
- Losing key drivers in the first month because nobody spoke to them before close.

**CLOSE · 6:50**

You can keep doing it the way most people do. You can keep pricing businesses on revenue. You can keep finding the truck finance after settlement. You can keep modelling deals on last year's fuel price. Alone, that usually takes years and costs deals.

Or you can have someone go through the numbers with you before you sign anything.

If neither fits, you'll hear that from me.

If you're someone who already runs a business and knows it can't be the only one, and you'd rather check the finance agreements than trust a big revenue number, you already know the next step.

Thirty days from now you'll be thirty days older whatever you do. The difference is whether you've got a list of 20 fleets and a question sheet in your hand, or you're still wondering if a second business is possible.

## EP 83 · Plumbing Job vs Plumbing Business: The $480K Question

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | On an illustrative $400K-profit plumber, pricing on profit after replacing the owner takes the price from $1.6M to $1.12M, a $480K difference. |
| **Hook** | "A plumbing business is a good business. Until it gets to the size where the owner can't quote every job." |
| **For** | One-Business Ceiling |
| **Stage** | Grow · Season 3, Act 17 |
| **Runtime** | ~8 min |
| **Copy** | From-To opener · Proof That Changes the Story (Saul) · Two Paths → Identity Close → After Close · Shape D (X vs Y) |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

A plumbing business is a good business. Local work, customers who come back, and nobody can fix a burst pipe from overseas.

Until it gets to the size where the owner can't quote every job. Then it goes from a full diary to a phone nobody answers.

On one illustrative deal, telling those two apart is worth $480,000 of price.

> **On screen:** $1.6M vs $1.12M · Same business · Illustrative

*[B-roll: a plumber's van pulling into a driveway; a roofer on a ladder]*

**STAKES · 0:20**

Two trades businesses can show the same $400,000 profit. One is a business. The other is a job with a crew attached. Pay a multiple for the second and you've bought profit that walks out the door with the seller. This is for owners at the ceiling of one business who want the next one to grow without them. If you want a van and a quiet life, this isn't that.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

One line. You only pay a multiple for profit that stays when the owner leaves.

Option A is the trades job. Option B is the trades business. Then what the lender sees, a three-question test, and a verdict.

If every trades business you've looked at seemed too tied to the owner, it had nothing to do with your judgement or not having a trade. It had everything to do with what's out there. A lot of them really are jobs.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:15**

I help owners buy trades businesses with little to no money down and put a growth plan behind them. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Option A: The trades job · 1:25**

The owner quotes every job, wins the work and does the hard ones. Ninety per cent of new work is word of mouth. Reported profit looks strong, because the owner takes almost no wage.

The seller is real. Thirty years on the tools, back gone, knees gone, kids in other careers. But part of that profit is his labour. Replace him with an ops manager and estimator at $120,000, and $400,000 of profit is really $280,000.

And a word-of-mouth business can't choose to grow in a given month. The phone rings or it doesn't.

**2 · Option B: The trades business · 2:20**

Someone other than the owner quotes. Much of the work repeats: maintenance plans, commercial and strata accounts, builder and insurance work that comes back every month. And some new jobs can be bought, through local search ads, emergency-call ads or outbound to property managers.

That last part is the growth lever. If you know what a booked job costs you and what that customer is worth, you can turn volume up or down. That's what makes buying the next one, and the one after, worth doing.

The usual belief is that owners like this only sell to someone they know. A nephew, a long-time employee, a mate from the trade. Saul, an Owners Academy member, has an NDA signed and is in early due diligence on an electrical and aircon business. 25 staff, around $3.8 million in revenue, owner retiring in two years. He found it with a cold call, straight to the owner. Not plumbing, but the same kind of business and the same kind of seller.

> **On screen:** Saul, Owners Academy member. Deal in progress; not closed. Results not typical; records on file.

**3 · What the lender sees · 3:30**

Trades are light on assets. Vans, tools, maybe some stock. So a lender leans on cash flow and on how stable the work is. Repeat and contracted work helps. One-off jobs from people who know the owner hurt.

Two more checks. Licences: in many places this work has to be done or supervised by a licensed person. If the owner holds the only licence, that's solved before close, not after. And seasonality: roofing can drop away in wet months, emergency plumbing can spike in cold snaps. Ask for revenue by month for three years. A business that makes its profit in six months needs a bigger buffer and a repayment schedule that survives the quiet months.

> **On screen:** Licensing rules vary by state and country. Check your local licensing rules. Education, not financial or legal advice.

**4 · The test and the verdict · 4:30**

Three questions to the owner. Who quoted the last 20 jobs? Where did the last 20 jobs come from? How many callbacks last year, crews going back to fix work for free?

If the answers are "me," "they all know me" and "we don't track it," you're looking at Option A.

The verdict. Buy B at a fair multiple. Buy A only if you price it as A, on profit after replacing the owner, and you have a plan to turn it into B in year one: an estimator, a booking process that answers fast, maintenance plans. Never pay a B price for an A business.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:15**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $2,000,000 |
| Reported profit | $400,000 |
| Cost to replace the owner | −$120,000 |
| Real profit | $280,000 |
| Price at 4× reported profit | $1,600,000 |
| Price at 4× real profit | $1,120,000 |
| Difference | $480,000 |
| Max debt service ($280K ÷ 1.5) | ~$186,000 a year |

One question took $480,000 off the price. And the 1.5 rule runs off the real number, so about $186,000 a year is all you can pay the seller and the bank together.

> **On screen:** Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:00**

- **The owner leaves and the phone stops.** The customers were calling him. Keep the seller in for a proper transition, at least six months, often longer.
- **The best crew leader leaves.** He wanted to buy it himself. Meet crew leaders before close and think about retention bonuses.
- **A warranty claim lands in year two.** In roofing especially, claims can turn up years later. Write down who carries pre-sale work in the contract.
- **Pricing on the growth plan.** Ads may not work in your market. Test before you rely on it, and price the deal on today's profit.

**CLOSE · 6:45**

Two paths. Alone, the mistake in trades is buying the owner. You see a strong profit number, never ask who quotes the jobs, and pay a multiple on profit that leaves with the seller. Or someone who's looked at these businesses sits next to you and asks the uncomfortable questions before you sign.

On the call, after a short application, we work out your strategy, your first structure, and whether the Owners Academy or 1:1 advisory fits. The Academy is where Saul is working his deal: the community, the full course, every template, daily deal discussion. 1:1 is me working your structures and lender presentations with you. If neither fits, I'll say so.

If you're someone who's hit the ceiling of one business, and you'll ask an owner where the last 20 jobs came from, you've made the decision already.

Thirty days from now you'll be thirty days older either way. Or thirty days older with five owner conversations behind you, knowing which business is a business and which is a job.

## EP 84 · Cutting Staff Is the Wrong Way to Profit From Childcare

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | On an illustrative 100-place centre, lifting occupancy from 70% to 80% adds about $300K of revenue in rooms that may already be staffed. |
| **Hook** | "There are two types of childcare buyer." |
| **For** | Corporate Prisoner |
| **Stage** | Credibility · Season 3, Act 17 |
| **Runtime** | ~8 min |
| **Copy** | Two-Types opener · That's Not How It Works · No Choice → Identity Close → After Close · Shape F (contrarian talk) |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

There are two types of childcare buyer. The ones who plan to cut staff to make it profitable, and the ones who plan to fill the empty places.

The first type runs into the law. The second finds about $300,000 of revenue already sitting in the building.

> **On screen:** +10 places × ~$30K = ~$300K revenue · Illustrative

*[B-roll: an empty childcare room early in the morning, small chairs, name labels on hooks]*

**STAKES · 0:20**

Childcare and education pull in a lot of corporate people who want to own something that matters. Good. It's also regulated, people-heavy and tied to one site. So the money is in occupancy, and the risk is in approval and the lease. Get those right and the growth plan is already in the rooms. Get them wrong and you sign an offer you can't get approved. If you want something you never have to think about, look elsewhere.

> **On screen:** Rules vary by state and country. Check your local licensing rules.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

The claim. In childcare, profit doesn't come from cutting wages. It comes from filling places in rooms you're already staffing.

Four parts. Why people believe the opposite. The empty-seat maths. The regulator. And the test.

If you've been told you can't buy a regulated business without having worked in it, that had nothing to do with your background. It had everything to do with the team around you.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:10**

I help people buy businesses like this with little to no money down, and build the team that gets them approved. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · Why people believe the opposite · 1:20**

Wages are usually the biggest cost by a distance. So cutting them looks like the lever.

That's not how it works here. Ratios set a minimum number of educators per room. You don't go below the floor. Ever. Push against it and you lose staff, then families, then possibly your approval.

**2 · The empty seat · 1:50**

A flight with empty seats still takes off. The pilots, the crew and the fuel are paid either way, so every extra passenger is mostly margin. A room staffed to ratio works the same, right up to the child that needs one more educator.

A centre is licensed for 100 places. Say each full-time place brings in about $30,000 a year. At 70% occupancy that's 70 places and $2.1 million. Lift it to 80% and that's ten more places and about $300,000 more revenue. If those places land in rooms that already have enough educators, a large part drops to profit. If they need another educator, less does.

So ask for occupancy by room and age group for three years, not one average. A centre can be full for older kids and half-empty in the baby room, and those rooms staff differently.

Then fees. Many independent centres set fees once a year without looking hard at what's nearby. Families mostly choose on trust, the educators and the location, so a well-run centre doesn't have to be the cheapest in the suburb.

Now flip it. If occupancy falls 10 points, the same maths works against you. So you price the deal on the occupancy you have, not the occupancy you hope for.

**3 · The regulator is your other buyer · 3:30**

In many places the approval to operate sits with the operator, and a change of owner can need regulator sign-off. So credibility here isn't only for the bank. The regulator wants to know who the approved operator is, who's responsible day to day, and whether they have the experience and qualifications.

So the order matters. First, the right person on your board: a former centre director or area manager, on founders' equity, typically 2 to 10%, not a salary. Then make regulatory approval and lease assignment conditions of your offer. No approval, no deal. Then it closes.

**[JOSH: optional. One line on how regulator or licence approvals worked when you bought in healthcare; cut if not.]**

The lender wants occupancy history, the lease and the compliance record. A full centre on a long lease with a clean record is a very different loan to a half-full one with two years left.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice. Check your local licensing rules.

**4 · The test · 4:40**

Three numbers. Occupancy by room against licensed places. Wages as a share of revenue, and whether they're rising faster than fees. And the lease: years left, renewal options, rent as a share of revenue, and whether it can be assigned to you. If it can't, or it runs out in two years, the business may not be what it looks like.

For education outside childcare, tutoring, training colleges, music schools, swap occupancy for enrolments and how many students come back each term. Then ask how much revenue rides on one accreditation or one funding body. If that changes, how much is left?

**THE NUMBERS · 5:30**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Licensed places | 100 |
| Revenue per full-time place | ~$30,000 a year |
| Revenue at 70% occupancy | $2,100,000 |
| Revenue at 80% occupancy | $2,400,000 |
| Extra revenue from 10 places | ~$300,000 |
| Revenue at 60% occupancy | $1,800,000 |

Ten points either way is $300,000 of revenue. Up is your growth plan. Down is why you price on today's occupancy and run the 1.5 rule on it.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:05**

- **Approval takes longer than planned** and the seller's patience runs out. Build the team before the offer.
- **The director families trust leaves after the sale.** Interview the director and senior educators before close, think about retention bonuses, and keep the seller involved for six months at least.
- **A lease clause or compliance issue nobody mentioned.** Get a lawyer who knows the sector to read both.
- **Subsidy policy changes.** In many places government subsidies are a big part of fees. Treat that like one big customer and price the risk in.

**1 · Buyable: lines 1 to 5 · 1:30**

Line 1, bankable. Will a lender fund a business like this? Assets give the bank security. Steady cash flow gives it comfort. Don't guess. Ring three lenders and ask their appetite for the sector. Twenty minutes, and it's the most important line on the sheet.

Line 2, boring. Traditional, stable, still needed in ten years. Score down anything technology is about to replace, and anything that's a fad.

Line 3, fragmented. Lots of small owners, nobody dominant. That's what makes a roll-up possible: buy at small-business multiples, around 4 times, combine, and sell the group at a higher one. An industry owned by three giants scores a 1.

Line 5, margins. Is there enough profit left to pass the 1.5 rule after the debt? Thin isn't automatically a no. Transport often runs thin. It just leaves less room.

**2 · Growable: lines 6 to 8 · 3:15**

Three caps stop a business growing. Score each one.

Line 6, the location cap. Can this business only ever serve one suburb, one building, one town? A single childcare centre scores low on its own. A group of them doesn't, because you get past the cap by buying more sites.

Line 7, the key-person cap. Does revenue usually depend on one person? The owner who quotes every job. The practitioner patients ask for by name. Score it low, or write down how you'll fix it in year one.

Line 8, the platform cap. In the businesses we buy, the platform is one big customer, one funder, one supplier or one referral source. A manufacturer living off one contract. A training college living off one funding stream. Score it low.

And to break a tie: can revenue be bought, with ads or outbound? If yes, you can grow on purpose.
