### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

A clinic reporting $500,000 profit can be worth $800,000 less than the asking price. The numbers aren't wrong. A third of them just walk out the door with the owner.

My first deal was a physio clinic. No deposit. Seven years of vendor finance at 4%. Here's the teardown I'd run on any clinic, in the order a bank reads it.

> **On screen:** Josh's first deal: physio clinic, 100% vendor finance, 7 years at 4%, no deposit. Results not typical; records on file.

**STAKES · 0:20**

Clinics sell at roughly 3 to 5 times profit. So every $100,000 of "profit" that's really the owner's wage adds $300,000 to $500,000 to the price. You pay that once, and you service the debt on it for seven years.

The sellers are there. Healthcare is full of practitioner-owners who treated patients all day for twenty years and did the payroll at night. They don't want to wait 16 weeks for a bank. They want out, and they want their patients and staff looked after. That's why getting paid over time lands well with them.

This is for owners whose one clinic has hit the ceiling of their own diary, and for buyers who want a group, not a job with a lease. Not for anyone after a clinic they visit on Fridays.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

A clinic's value is the profit that stays after the owner leaves. That's the whole idea.

There are five lines to read, in the order a lender reads them: the headline profit, billings by practitioner, the owner's hands, utilisation, and the debt test.

And if your own clinic has stalled, it has nothing to do with how good a clinician you are or how many hours you put in. It has everything to do with a business built around one person. That's a design problem. The next owner can fix it.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners and buyers do this with little to no money down. Book a call below. Now the teardown.

**1 · The headline profit · 1:40**

Start where the seller starts. Three years of financials that match the tax returns.

Here, illustrative: $2 million revenue, $500,000 profit. At 4 times, the seller's number is $2 million. Write it down. Then go looking for what's propping it up.

Lenders often like healthcare, because demand tends to hold when the economy doesn't. What they check is predictable: revenue that doesn't sit with one person, practitioners staying, a lease that can be assigned with enough term left, registrations and billing in order. And they look at who's behind you. A board with a finance lead and someone who's run clinics carries real weight.

**2 · Billings by practitioner · 2:20**

The name is on the door. The revenue sits with people. Ask for billings by practitioner, three years.

Here, the owner bills $600,000. That's 30% of the clinic. Treat any one person above about 30% like customer concentration, because that's what it is. Those patients may follow that person out.

**3 · The owner's hands · 3:05**

A lot of owner-practitioners pay themselves through the profit, not a salary. So the profit looks bigger than it is.

Replace the owner's clinical work with a practitioner on a fair rate. Say $200,000 a year. True profit: $300,000. At 4 times, that's $1.2 million. The $800,000 gap is the price of the owner's hands. Don't pay it.

Most buyers think a clinic's value is its profit, its name, its location. That's not how it works. It's the profit after the owner goes. Price that, and the rest is negotiation.

**4 · Utilisation · 4:05**

Booked appointments against available appointments, per practitioner. This is your growth line.

A clinic at 60% has room to grow without hiring. At 95% it's capped until you recruit. Same profit, very different upside, and the seller rarely prices it.

Lift five practitioners from 60% to 75% and you've added a quarter more appointments on the same rent, the same front desk, the same software. That's the cheapest profit you'll ever buy. You don't pay for it. You find it.

**5 · The debt test · 4:35**

Now the bank's question. Can the clinic pay for itself? Free cash flow should cover debt repayments at least 1.5 times.

$300,000 divided by 1.5 is $200,000 a year to the seller. Over seven years, $1.4 million. Your $1.2 million offer fits with headroom.

Then the regulation. Healthcare is licensed. In some places the rules decide who can own a clinic and how it bills. Check before you spend a week on a target.

The $800,000 between the two prices is the owner's job. Protect the 30% you do pay for with a handover, and part of the seller's payments tied to those billings holding.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 6:05**

- The owner is the top biller and leaves early. The handover was a handshake. The billings go with them.
- A practitioner walks across the road with their patients. Whether a restraint holds depends on where you are and how it's written. Ask your lawyer.
- Billing problems with a government scheme or insurer come back after you own it. Get someone who knows healthcare billing to look.
- The team hears about the sale in the car park. Talk to the key people before close, with the seller's agreement.

After you own it, the order is simple. Fill the empty appointments before you hire, starting with answering every enquiry fast. Episode 51 covers speed to lead. Then recruit, because every practitioner with a full book makes the clinic less dependent on any one person. Then a second clinic nearby, sharing the practice manager, the marketing and the bookkeeping. Buy at small-business multiples, combine, and a group can sell at a bigger buyer's multiple.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

A trade business is worth its licence, its recurring work and its cash cycle. Not its busiest year.

You were told the good businesses are the new ones. You were told you need to know the trade to own a trade business. Neither was ever true. **1 · Not checking who holds the licence · 1:40**

In a lot of places, an electrical or refrigeration business must have a licensed person nominated on its licence. If that's the retiring owner, the licence can walk out with them.

Find out who the nominee is in week one. Then plan the replacement: a licensed manager in place before the owner leaves, written into the deal.

This one is first because it doesn't cost you money. It costs you the deal. A business that can't legally trade on settlement day has no profit to multiply. And it's the check people leave to the week before settlement.

> **On screen:** Check your local licensing rules.

**2 · Paying for installs like they repeat · 2:30**

Split revenue into recurring and one-off. Maintenance contracts and service agreements come back every year. A big install year doesn't.

Banks like contracted revenue. The next buyer pays more for it. So price the recurring share at a full multiple, and treat a bumper install year as a good year, not a new baseline.

Illustrative: an install boom adds $100,000 of profit for one year. At 4 times, paying for it as if it repeats adds $400,000 to the price for profit that may not come back. Ask for three years, split by job type, and check gross margin on each. Owners often know the business makes money. They don't always know which work makes it.

**3 · Leaving the business in the owner's head · 3:15**

Two things live there. The quoting: thirty years of instinct nobody else can do. And the senior techs' loyalty.

Get the quoting out while the owner is still there: price lists, a simple estimator, recorded walk-throughs. Talk to the key techs before close, with the seller's agreement. Licensed staff are hard to replace.

**4 · Ignoring debtor days · 4:00**

The counterintuitive one. Trade businesses can be profitable and short of cash at the same time. Customers pay late. Materials get paid up front.

Debtor days is how long customers take to pay, on average. It's where the cash hides. And at settlement there's usually a working capital line to agree. Get it wrong and you fund the gap yourself.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 5:35**

## EP 78 · Cash vs paid over time: buying a $1.2M accounting firm

| | |
|---|---|
| **The money** | On an illustrative $1.2M practice, tying the price to retained fees means a 15% client loss cuts what you pay by about $180,000, instead of costing you it |
| **Hook** | "There's a well-kept secret in accounting firm sales." |

| **Runtime** | ~8 min |
| **Copy** | Secret opener · That's Not How It Works · Two Paths → Identity Close → After Close · Shape D (X vs Y with a self-test) |

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

There's a well-kept secret in accounting firm sales. The buyers who do well don't pay for the client list. They pay for the clients who stay.

Buy a 400-client practice for $1.2 million in cash. The partner retires, 60 clients follow them out, and you've paid about $180,000 for people who left. Here's the other way to pay.

> **On screen:** Illustrative numbers. Not a real deal.

**STAKES · 0:20**

An accounting firm looks like the safest business there is. Same clients, same work, every year. That's why people with capital like it.

And the sellers are there. A lot of practices are owned by partners in their sixties with forty tax seasons behind them, no obvious successor, and young accountants who don't want to buy in. They want out, their clients looked after, and their staff kept on.

But it's a people business. The asset is relationships, and relationships can leave. This is for people with capital who want steady cash flow and a structure that protects it. Not for anyone after a quiet practice to coast in.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

In a people business, the price should follow the people who stay.

Two options. Option A: pay a fixed price in cash on day one. Option B: pay over time, out of fees, with the price tied to retention. One self-test. Then the verdict.

And if a deal like this has burned someone you know, it had nothing to do with their intelligence, their capital or how carefully they read the numbers. It had everything to do with a structure that paid for clients before anyone knew who'd stay.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help people structure deals like this with little to no money down. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · What you're buying · 1:40**

Recurring fees. Annual compliance, tax returns, bookkeeping, payroll. Work that comes back without a sales call. Don't pay for one-off projects like they repeat.

Three numbers. Recurring share: what percentage of fees repeat. In a lot of practices it's high. Check it, don't assume it. Three-year retention: of the fees clients paid three years ago, how much is still there. That tells you how sticky the firm is before you arrive. Lock-up: unbilled work plus unpaid invoices. Some firms do a year's work and bill it in one hit, and you fund that gap.

Lenders will want the same three years of financials, fees by client and by year, and proof the recurring fees really recur. Some have specific professional-practice lending. Ask.

And it's a regulated profession. Who can own a practice, sign returns or hold a practising certificate depends on where you are.

> **On screen:** Check your local licensing rules.

**2 · Option A: cash on day one · 2:45**

Most people with money think the safe move is to pay cash, take a discount, own it outright. That's not how it works in a people business.

Paying it all on day one moves all the retention risk onto you. The partner has your money. The clients have a choice. If 15% of fees leave, you've paid full price for a smaller firm, and there's no one to send the bill to.

**3 · Option B: paid over time, tied to retention · 3:30**

Lenders tend to see an accounting firm as asset-light. Little equipment, often no property. So the seller often carries more of the price anyway.

Use that. The seller is paid over six years, out of fees. After year one you measure recurring fees again. Above an agreed line, say 90%, the price stands. Below it, the price comes down in proportion. Arrangements like that are common in professional-practice sales. The wording is your lawyer's job.

Now the seller wants the handover to work as much as you do. They stay a year, introduce the top clients in person, and get paid when those clients stay.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice.

**4 · The self-test · 4:30**

One question. What share of fees does the retiring partner personally look after?

If it's small, and three-year retention is high, Option A is a defensible bet. If it's most of the fees, Option A is a guess with your money on it. Fees by partner tells you which one you're looking at, in one line.

The verdict: Option B by default. Pay over time, out of fees, tied to retention. Move toward cash only when the firm passes the test, and even then keep a slice deferred until year one is measured. Your capital still has a job. It sits behind the deal as comfort for the lender and the seller, not in the seller's account on day one.

And if the partner won't accept any link to retention, that tells you what they think of their own client list. Walking away is the best negotiating tactic you have.

**THE NUMBERS · 5:00**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Fees billed | $1,500,000 |
| Recurring fees | $1,200,000 |
| Profit after a manager's wage | $300,000 |
| Price at 4× | $1,200,000 |
| Seller payment at 1.5 DSCR | $200,000 a year × 6 = $1.2M |
| Recurring fees fall to 85% in year one | Option A: price stays $1.2M · Option B: price ~$1.02M |

Same firm, same client loss. Under A you've paid about $180,000 for clients who left. Under B, the seller absorbs it, because they held the relationships.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 5:50**

- The handover is one email. The top clients follow the partner to a mate's firm.
- Settlement lands in tax season. The team is flat out and the new owner is asking questions. Time it with the calendar.
- Lots of small, low-fee clients that take as long as the big ones. Revenue looks fine. Profit per hour doesn't.

After you own it, move clients to fixed monthly fees in advance to cut lock-up. Add advisory work for the clients who already trust you. Then roll up: there are partners retiring in every town.

**CLOSE · 6:30**

Two ways to put money into a practice. Alone, you pay a fixed price, a chunk of the fees leave with the partner, and you're paying full price for a smaller firm. Or you ask for so much protection the partner walks. Or you structure it with someone who's done it, so the price follows the fees.

That's the call. Owners Academy, or 1:1 advisory where I work your structures and lender presentations with you. The NBIO template you get on joining is where a retention clause like this first gets written down.

If you're someone with capital who wants it in a real business, and wants the risk in the contract, not in your gut… thirty days from now your money is either still sitting where it is, or you've got ten practices on a list and a structure on paper.

### Script

**HOOK · 0:00**

How come service firms with good contracts, good staff and good margins still sell for less than their owners expect?

Because in a lot of them the real asset isn't the contracts. It's the owner's phone. And a firm with $600,000 profit and one customer at 30% can fail its debt the month that customer leaves.

> **On screen:** Illustrative numbers. Not a real deal.

**STAKES · 0:20**

Commercial cleaning, facilities maintenance, IT support, security. Asset-light, often contracted, often owned by someone who sold every contract personally.

The light's on Exit, because what you check before you buy is exactly what the next buyer pays for when you sell. Buy around 4 times while the firm runs on its owner. Fix that, combine a few, and a group can sell at a bigger buyer's multiple. For owners who want a portfolio, not a phone that never stops.

**THE FRAME · 0:45**

A service firm is worth what it does without its owner. Concentration isn't a reason to walk. It's a reason to structure.

Four parts: the claim, why owners believe the opposite, the test, and the structure.

**SOFT CTA · ~1:30**

I help owners buy and structure firms like this with little to no money down. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**1 · The claim · 1:40**

Most owners think a firm is worth more the harder they work in it. That's not how it works. It's worth more the less it needs them.

A buyer pays for revenue that stays after settlement. If the top clients ring the owner at 9pm, they're loyal to the owner, not the firm. That's key-person risk, and the buyer prices it in.

**2 · Why owners believe the opposite · 2:20**

Because it's how the firm was built. One person won every contract and kept every relationship. The firm grew around their phone number.

And if that's your firm, it has nothing to do with how hard you've worked or how good you are at sales. It has everything to do with a business that grew around one person. Nobody designed it that way. Which means it can be redesigned.

That's also why the seller is often open to staying on for a proper handover, paid over time. Their fatigue and your risk point at the same answer.

**3 · The test · 3:10**

Three numbers on the first call.

Top-two customer share. Above about 30%, it's a risk you deal with in the structure.

Contracted revenue and term left. What share is under signed contracts, how many months remain, and can they be assigned? Some let the customer walk on a change of control. Your lawyer reads every major one.

Owner-managed revenue. What share comes from customers whose main contact is the owner. That's how much can walk when the owner does.

And if the service is licensed, security or electrical work for example, check the licence too.

> **On screen:** Check your local licensing rules.

**4 · The structure · 4:15**

Lenders look hard at concentration in an asset-light firm. So the seller often carries more of the price here. Good. Make part of it depend on the thing that worries everyone.

Size the fixed debt off the profit without the top customer. Make part of the seller's payments depend on that customer renewing. If they stay, the seller gets paid more. If they go, you're not broke. Episode 74 covers payments tied to performance.

> **On screen:** Education, not financial or legal advice.

**THE NUMBERS · 4:55**

| | Illustrative |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $4,000,000 |
| Profit | $600,000 |
| Top customer (30% of revenue) | $1,200,000 |
| Profit on that customer at 25 cents a dollar | $300,000 |
| Debt sized on full profit (÷ 1.5) | $400,000 a year |
| Profit if they leave | $300,000, short by $100,000 |
| Fixed debt sized without them (÷ 1.5) | $200,000 a year, plus a renewal-linked payment |

Same firm, two structures. One is fine until month four. The other survives the bad case and pays the seller well in the good one.

**WHERE PEOPLE LOSE MONEY · 5:45**

- The top customer has a change-of-control clause and uses the sale to retender.
- The handover is one lunch per client. Put a manager on every big relationship and introduce them while the owner is still there.
- Most contracts are month-to-month and nobody checked.
- The account manager the clients actually like resigns, because nobody told them about the sale.
