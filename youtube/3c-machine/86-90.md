**Why a seller says yes (3:00)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME · MARKET HAS MOVED*

When offers get ignored, it has nothing to do with the buyer's age, bank balance or lack of a track record. No. It has everything to do with the shape of the offer. Most buyers still send the old shape: a price, a deposit, a wait for the bank.

Here's what's actually happening. A whole generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. Their problem usually isn't price. It's the burden of owning the thing. They want out, and they want to be looked after.

"Looked after" is the bit buyers skip. Sell for cash, and a thirty-year owner is suddenly holding a pile of money to invest, protect and make last. Most have never managed a lump sum. They've managed a business that paid them.

So the pitch isn't "I'll buy your business." It's "the business keeps paying you, like it always has. You just stop working in it."

The buyers who get this send offers that look nothing like anyone else's. Everyone else is still fighting over price.

Never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time," or, as Elliot puts it, a pension from your business. That's a nickname, not a regulated pension product, so never present it as one.

**Build your own (5:00)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Let's build one from scratch. Round numbers, illustrative, not a real deal. First you find the real profit. Then you work out what it can pay. Once you have that, you compare it with the cash offer.

A business makes $300,000 a year in profit, after paying a manager to run it. The seller is 65.

A normal offer at four times profit is $1.2 million, after a wait for the bank, and then they've got the lump-sum problem.

The lifetime offer: profit divided by 1.5. That's $200,000 a year, for life.

If the seller lives another twenty years, that's $4 million paid over time. More than three times the cash offer, in total. And the reason they lean in is simple: it's more money, and it arrives the way they've always been paid.

[SCREEN: "Cash offer: 4 × $300K = $1.2M once. Lifetime: $300K ÷ 1.5 = $200K a year. 20 years = $4M."]

For you: $100,000 of headroom from day one. No deposit. No bank. And here's the real prize. If the payment is fixed and you grow profit from $300,000 to $600,000, the seller still gets $200,000. You keep $400,000.

But don't kid yourself on the cost. You may pay out more in total than any normal deal would cost you. You're trading a bigger total for no deposit, no bank and the upside. That's a fair trade only if you're actually going to grow it.

**The three clauses (6:15)**

Every lifetime offer needs an answer to three questions before you send the LOI.

First, profit drops. A fixed payment hurts in a bad year. Your options are a payment that's a share of profit, a floor and a cap, or a holiday at the start. Fixed is better for you if you grow. A share is safer if you don't. Pick on purpose, not by accident.

Second, the seller dies. Do payments stop? Go to a spouse for a set period? Go to the estate? That clause changes the deal's value more than any other. Have the answer in writing.

Third, you want to sell. Whoever buys your group later won't want a lifetime obligation attached. So negotiate the right to pay it out for an agreed lump sum after an agreed year. Agree it up front, while everyone's friendly.

Then security and control, which is top-tier lawyer territory, not a template off the internet.

**Where it fits (7:45)**

It fits when the seller is older, wants income more than a cheque, and profit is steady. It doesn't fit when the seller needs cash now, or profit swings wildly.

Never lead with "ninety-nine years." Lead with the income. One way to frame the LOI: two options side by side, paid over a set number of years or paid for life. Let the seller choose. Either way you're inside 1.5.

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Capital" lights up. Corner tag: "Act 18 · Advanced structures · 2 of 5"]

Last episode was the lifetime offer: paying a seller for life. This one's the opposite shape. Small payments for years, then big ones on set dates.

In the next few minutes I'll show you how to shrink the yearly repayment so the business keeps its cash, so you're never stuck choosing between paying the seller and growing. All without a discount, and without getting caught by the lump sum in year three.

Who this is for: people putting in 40-plus hours a week, aiming for a roll-up, a legacy business or flips. Not for anyone who wants a business they never have to think about. A balloon makes you think about it.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Interest-only means you pay the seller only the interest each year. The price itself doesn't come down. That keeps your yearly payment small, so the business has far more cash left over to run and grow.

Two. A balloon is a lump sum of the price that falls due on a set date. In Cam's structure, one lands in year three and the rest in year ten. The seller still gets the full price. They just get most of it later, with interest along the way.

Three. Every balloon needs a plan the day you sign, not the year it's due. Save for it, refinance it with a bank once you've got your own track record, or sell before it lands. Ideally two of the three.

Interest-only with balloons is a cash-flow tool. It's not a discount. You owe every dollar.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why the yearly payment matters so much (3:00)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME · WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING*

When a deal feels too tight to grow, it usually has nothing to do with how hard the owner works or how well they run it. It has everything to do with the repayment schedule they signed.

You were told debt has to come down from day one. You were told the responsible thing is principal and interest from settlement. For a home loan, maybe. For a business you plan to grow, that isn't the only way. Some of the best structures pay almost nothing off for years, on purpose.

Go back to the rule that runs this whole series. Free cash flow has to cover your debt payments at least one and a half times. The 1.5 rule.

There are two ways to pass it. Buy something with more profit, or make the yearly payment smaller. Interest-only is the second one. It doesn't change what you owe. It changes when you owe it.

[SCREEN: "Coverage = free cash flow ÷ yearly debt payments. Minimum 1.5×."]

**The numbers side by side (3:45)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Round numbers, illustrative, not Cam's deal.

A business makes $400,000 a year in free cash flow. The price is $2 million, carried by the seller at 5%.

Version A, principal and interest over ten years. Roughly $259,000 a year. Coverage: $400,000 divided by $259,000 is about 1.54. You pass, barely. One bad year and you're under.

Version B, interest-only. 5% of $2 million is $100,000 a year. Coverage: four times. You have about $300,000 a year left over to run the place, fix what's broken, and grow.

[SCREEN: "A: P&I ≈ $259K/yr → 1.54×. B: interest-only $100K/yr → 4.0×."]

Same business. Same price. Completely different first three years.

**The balloons (4:45)**

Now the catch. In Version B you haven't paid off a dollar of the $2 million. So the balloons do that.

Say $500,000 is due at the end of year three, and the remaining $1.5 million at the end of year ten. Again, illustrative. [JOSH: the rough size of Cam's year-3 and year-10 balloons, as dollars or a share of the price, if he's happy to share]

[SCREEN: timeline. Years 1–10: $100K interest a year (falling to $75K after year 3). Year 3: $500K balloon. Year 10: $1.5M balloon.]

And the reason a seller agrees is simple. They get interest every year. They get a real chunk of money in year three. They get the full price. And they get a buyer who can close fast. Cam did two deals in 23 days. Speed and certainty matter to a retiring owner more than most buyers think.

**Three ways to pay a balloon (5:30)**

You've got three tools, and the best plans use more than one.

Save for it. In the example you have about $300,000 a year of headroom. Put roughly $170,000 a year into a separate account you don't touch, and you've got $500,000 by year three. Boring. Reliable.

Refinance it. By year three you're not a stranger to the bank any more. You have three years of your own financial statements and tax returns, and the business has kept performing under you. That's a very different conversation from day one. A bank may lend you the balloon over a normal term. May. Rates, your numbers and their appetite all decide that.

Sell before it lands. If you're building a roll-up, the year-ten balloon might be paid out of the exit. Buy at three to five times, sell the group at a higher multiple, settle the note on the way out.

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

So the order looks like this. First you open the savings account in month one. Then, once you have two years of your own numbers, you start the bank conversation. And once the group is big enough, the exit settles what's left.

[SCREEN: "Save · Refinance · Sell. Pick two."]

**Daniel's warning about refinancing (6:30)**

There's a lesson from Daniel Iles here. He built Viral Coach to around $5 million a month, and in one video he talks about nearly going broke on the way. He had a plane, a Porsche and home equity. None of it helped. The refinance on the house would take 30 days, and he got lowballed.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'How I went broke trying to get rich…' · 14:30 · link in description"]

Illiquid assets don't save you when the money's due. If your whole balloon plan is "I'll refinance in year three," you've made the bank's mood your plan. Start the refinance conversation twelve months before the balloon, not two. And keep the savings account going anyway.

He has a second point that fits. His team once financed twelve-month programmes on 24-month loans, and it hurt renewals badly. His fix: match the financing term to what you're financing. For you that means asking whether a ten-year interest-only term actually matches how long you plan to own the business and how long it takes to grow it.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Here's How We're Scaling to $10M/Month' · 1:09:55 · link in description"]

**What can go wrong (7:30)**

A balloon you can't pay is a default. Depending on what you signed, the seller may be able to take security over the business or take it back. Read that clause before anything else.

So three rules. Stress-test year three with profit 20% lower and see if the plan still works. Negotiate a right to extend the balloon for a fee or a higher rate if the bank says not yet. And use the breathing room. Interest-only gives you three years of spare cash. If you spend it on yourself, you've just delayed the problem.

The working capital line matters here too. Cam got $500,000 of working capital from the bank on top of the vendor finance. That's runway for the business. It isn't balloon money, and you shouldn't treat it as balloon money.

[SCREEN: "Cam, Josh's friend: $500K working capital from the bank. Results not typical; records on file."]

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**What mezzanine actually is (3:00)**

[SCREEN: the capital stack. Bottom: owners' equity. Above: seller note. Above: mezzanine. Top: senior bank debt. Arrow: "who gets paid first".]

Picture the deal as a stack. At the top, senior debt from a bank. Cheapest money, first in line. At the bottom, equity: you and anyone you've given shares to. Last in line, all the upside.

Mezzanine is a layer in between. It's usually subordinated, meaning it ranks behind the bank. It's often interest-only, with the principal due at the end. Sometimes part of the interest isn't paid in cash but added to the balance each year, which helps cash flow today and costs you more later. And sometimes the lender asks for a small slice of equity on top, often called a kicker.

What does it cost? It varies a lot by market and lender. As a broad guide, it's typically priced well above bank debt, often in the double digits. Treat any number you hear as a starting point for questions, not a quote.

[SCREEN: "Costs vary widely by lender and market. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**When it's worth using (4:00)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME*

When owners stall at one business, it usually has nothing to do with ambition, work ethic or how well they run it. It has everything to do with only ever being shown two options: save more, or sell equity.

Mezzanine is a third option, for bigger deals. Most mezzanine lenders want real, stable profit and a clear plan. It's usually not a tool for your first small acquisition.

*// PROOF THAT CHANGES THE STORY*

And before you reach for it, try the cheaper layers properly. A lot of people assume bigger deals need a big cheque. So look at my friend Jason Nagy. His first deal was a $5.5 million radiology business with zero down, on a 60/40 structure. Seven years later he sold to private equity for $200 million. That first deal didn't need a big cheque. It needed a structure, and no exotic layer on the way in.

[SCREEN: "Jason Nagy, Josh's friend. Results not typical; records on file."]

[JOSH: whether Arthur's $23M deal, or any other deal you know first-hand, used mezzanine; if so, one line on why, and confirm how to describe Arthur (friend or student)]

Use mezzanine when three things are true. The cheaper layers are maxed out. Selling equity would cost you control or most of the exit. And the business still clears 1.5 with the mezzanine in.

**The stack, worked through (4:45)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

Round numbers. Illustrative, not a real deal. First you take what the seller will carry. Then what the bank will lend. Once you have both, whatever's left is the gap.

A business makes $2 million a year in free cash flow. The price is $10 million, five times.

The seller will carry 30%. $3 million, interest-only at 5% for the early years. That's $150,000 a year.

The bank will lend 50%. $5 million at 7% over ten years. Roughly $712,000 a year.

That's 80%. There's a $2 million gap.

Option one: sell equity to fill it. You might be giving away a big share of the company, permanently, and someone else gets a say in when and how you sell.

Option two: mezzanine. $2 million at an illustrative 14%, interest-only. $280,000 a year.

Total yearly debt payments: about $1.14 million. Coverage: $2 million divided by $1.14 million. About 1.75.

[SCREEN: "Seller $150K + Bank $712K + Mezz $280K ≈ $1.14M a year. $2M ÷ $1.14M ≈ 1.75×."]

That passes. And the reason you don't stop there is that good years aren't the test. Now stress it. Profit drops 20% to $1.6 million. Coverage falls to about 1.4. Under the line. So this deal only works if you're confident in that profit, or you negotiate something else. A longer bank term. A payment holiday on the seller note. Part of the mezzanine interest rolled up for the first two years.

That's the real skill with bigger deals. Not finding a lender. Making every layer fit inside 1.5, in a bad year as well as a good one.

**Who gets paid first (6:15)**

With three or four lenders in one deal, someone has to decide the order. That's what an intercreditor agreement does. Who ranks first. Who can enforce, and when. What happens to the seller's payments if the bank or the mezzanine lender is unhappy.

Your seller needs to understand this before they sign anything. If they're carrying $3 million and ranking behind two lenders, they deserve their own lawyer and a plain explanation from you.

The good news on bigger deals: banks often don't need a personal guarantee once you're over roughly $5 million, because an asset-rich business gives them their security. Typically. Every lender and deal is different.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**Why bigger is worth the trouble (7:00)**

Daniel Iles built Viral Coach to around $5 million a month, and he makes a point about valuation that fits here. Multiples rise with size. A business with $1 million of profit might sell around four times. One with $10 million of profit might fetch six to eight times. More profit means more stability and more problems already solved.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Why $10k per month is a dangerous place to be' · 3:35 · link in description"]

That's the roll-up in one line. Buy smaller businesses at three to five times. Combine and grow them. Sell the group at a higher multiple. Mezzanine often shows up in the middle of that journey, when the group is big enough to borrow against but the next acquisition is bigger than the cheaper layers can fund.

So it's a growth tool. Not a rescue tool. If you need mezzanine to make a weak deal work, the deal is wrong.
