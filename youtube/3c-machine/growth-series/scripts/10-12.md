A bloke in a work shirt, squinting into the sun, standing in front of the first truck or the first shopfront. Nobody who walks past it knows what the business turned over the year it was taken. What they know is what he was like. That he paid on time. That he kept people on through a bad winter. That the apprentice he took on runs the place now.

That's the whole distinction. The numbers are how a business is measured. The person is how it's remembered. And the strange thing is, the person is also what decides how big the numbers ever get.

So let's take it apart. There are four pieces to who growth asks you to become. The first is what you call yourself. The second is how you decide. The third is how the work gets done. And the fourth is what you're looking after.

Start with what you call yourself.

Whatever you call yourself becomes the size of the box you work in. "A plumber who owns a business" has a ceiling, and it's set by his own hours. "An owner of plumbing businesses" is a different job, with a different ceiling, even if he's the same person on the same Monday.

Think about rowing and sailing. A rower who wants to go faster pulls harder. The speed comes straight out of their arms, so the limit is how strong they are and how long they last. A sailor who wants to go faster doesn't pull anything. They read the wind, trim the sail, pick the line. Same water, same boat length, a completely different person at the helm.

That's the difference between an operator growing and an owner growing. Operators row. Owners learn to sail. Neither is a better person. But only one of them is going to cross open water.

Now, part of you is pushing back on this, and fair enough. You like being on the tools. You're good at it. It's half the reason you started. And the question underneath is a real one. If I become this other person, what do I lose?

So let's run that properly, because it's a fair question and it deserves numbers, not a pep talk.

Ask yourself three things. What do I lose if I move forward? What do I gain by losing it? And how could I keep both?

The first answer is usually something like the hands-on part, or the certainty of knowing every customer by name. The second is usually time, and range, and a business that doesn't stop when you do. And the third is where most owners surprise themselves. You can keep the part of the work you love. You just stop being the only one who can do it.

Then there's the money fear, and it deserves the same care. Could buying a second business put the first one at risk? Could it cost me the house?

Here's an illustrative example. Say a business makes three hundred thousand a year in profit. The price is three and a half times that, about one point zero five million, and it's funded by a lender and by the seller being paid over time out of the business, not out of your savings.

The rule over all of it is one and a half times. The profit has to be at least one and a half times what the business pays in debt each year. Three hundred thousand divided by one and a half is two hundred thousand. That's the most it should ever be asked to repay in a year.

Now the worst case. A bad first year, and profit drops by a third, to two hundred thousand. The repayments are still covered.

And the walls. The new business carries its own debt and doesn't lean on the one you already have. There are three years of financials, matching tax returns and proper due diligence before you sign. Whether a lender wants a personal guarantee is in the terms, in front of you, before anything's signed. And if it doesn't stack up, you walk away.

A word of caution. No money down doesn't mean no risk. The risk sits in the debt and in the seller's terms, and you read both. This is general information, not financial advice, and every lender and every deal is different.

The second piece is how you decide.

Most owners I meet make a decision in about two minutes, and then take two years to give themselves permission to act on it. Not because they're slow. Because every decision feels like it has to be made fresh, in the moment, with everything riding on it.

Think about how a pilot takes off. There's a speed on the runway called V1. Below it, if something goes wrong, they stop. Above it, they keep going, even with a warning light, because stopping would be more dangerous than flying. And here's the part that matters. V1 isn't decided on the runway. It's worked out before the plane leaves the gate, from the weight, the weather and the length of the strip.

That's what deciding like an owner looks like. You set your criteria before the moment arrives. One and a half times cover. Three years of financials. Tax returns that match the books. If a deal clears them, you go. If it doesn't, you stop. The decision was already made at the gate.

There's never a perfect moment. Owners who wait to be a hundred per cent sure usually wait forever. Owners who decide at about sixty per cent, on criteria they set in advance, are the ones who end up owning more than one business.

I'm not saying that from a distance. At twenty-five I was unemployed, with no savings and no experience. My first deal was a physio clinic, one hundred per cent vendor finance, seven years at four per cent, a nine-month payment holiday and no deposit. Before it landed, four deals fell over, and there were around a thousand cold calls. The person who made the thousandth call wasn't the same person who made the first one. That's not a numbers story. That's a who-you-become story.

So which decision in your business have you already made... and are still waiting for permission to act on?

The third piece is how the work gets done.

There's a pattern most owners recognise once it's said out loud. Under about five million, a business is mostly about the product and the marketing. Between five and ten, it's about people and teams. Past ten, it's about systems. What gets you to one level isn't what gets you to the next.

Think about the best bricklayer on a building site. Fast, neat, every course dead level, and the walls he lays are the ones people stop to look at. Down the road, a builder is running five sites at once and won't lay a single brick all week, and five houses go up. He isn't a better bricklayer. He's stopped being the bricklayer.

That's the shift the second business forces on you, whether you plan for it or not. You can't lay bricks on two sites at once. So you build the managers, the meeting, the one-page report. And the moment you do, the first business gets better too, because it was waiting for exactly the same thing.

Put simply, you're either the leverage in your business, or you have leverage. Right now, if you're honest, most of the leverage in your business is you. Your hours, your judgement, your phone. Owning a second business is how you move from being the leverage to having it. Not because you work less, though often you do. Because the work you do starts to multiply through other people instead of stopping at the end of your own day.

Here's a small test. If you were away for a month, starting tomorrow, would the business be bigger or smaller when you got back? Most owners know the answer before I finish asking. That answer is a measure of who you've been so far, not of who you could be.

The fourth piece is what you're looking after.

When you buy a retiring owner's business, you're not only buying a profit line. You're taking over thirty years of somebody's life. Their staff. Their customers. Their name on the sign. And what most of them want isn't the highest bidder. It's someone they trust to look after it.

One of our Owners Academy members, Saul, cold-called the owner of an electrical and air-conditioning business. About three point eight million in revenue, twenty-five staff, and an owner planning to retire in two years. Saul's signed the NDA and he's in early due diligence. It isn't closed, and it may not close. But think about what's sitting on the other side of that table. Twenty-five people whose working lives depend on who takes over.

A friend of mine in the US, Elliot, made around forty offers. Three letters of intent were signed, all at one hundred per cent seller finance, on ninety-nine-year terms set up like a pension plan for the seller. One example is three hundred and fifty thousand a year for life, on a business making five hundred thousand. Results like that aren't typical. But look at what the seller is buying with that structure. Not a price. Being looked after for the rest of his life, out of the thing he built.

That's stewardship. And it's why the person you become matters to the seller as much as it matters to you.

Part of you might be thinking, I'm not a boardroom type. I worry too much about my people to be a dealmaker. But turn that over. A retiring owner isn't looking for a dealmaker. They're looking for the person who'll still be thinking about their staff at nine o'clock at night. The fact that you worry about your people isn't the thing that disqualifies you. It's the thing a good seller is hoping to find.

For a retiring owner, the best change is the one where very little changes. The same faces on Monday. The same customers looked after. Their income still arriving, out of the business they built. Being the person who can promise that, and mean it, is most of what wins the deal.
