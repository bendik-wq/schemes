One Drawing on Your Fridge Decides If You Own the Business or It Owns You

Picture this. Ten past eight at night, parked in your own driveway, engine off, still on the phone. In the rear-view mirror you catch your own face. Grey under the eyes. A dull ache behind them. For a second it does not look like someone who owns a business. It looks like someone the business owns. On illustrative numbers, the business is twice the size it was ten years ago. So are your hours. Let that land. The business grew and you shrank into it.

Now picture your fridge. Tuesday, a bit after seven. You come through the back door with the keys still in your hand and the day still on your clothes. Under a magnet from the pizza place there is a drawing. The class project was called My Parent's Job. It is a stick figure with your hair, standing at the kitchen bench with a phone held to its ear. The speech bubble says one more call. Your throat goes tight. It is not a bad drawing. It is an accurate one. That drawing has nothing to do with your revenue and everything to do with what your business is turning you into.

If there is no drawing on your fridge, there is still a sentence. Listen next time someone asks your kids what you do. Whatever they say, they wrote it by watching you. Not by reading your accounts. They have already noticed the phone. They have already noticed that you are not really there.

Here is the quiet part I suspect a lot of owners do not say out loud. They are not afraid the numbers will stop. They are afraid the numbers will double and the sentence will not change. Or it will get worse. More revenue. More staff. More calls at dinner. The same person underneath, only more tired. They are afraid of being sixty and owning a job that needs them. Of a business that cannot run a month without them, cannot sell, cannot hand over. That is the actual cost. Not the plateau. The decade. Not the missed profit. The missed life.

I will give you the question that decides which drawing your kids make. It has nothing to do with revenue. But before I give it to you, you need to see a different picture.

Walk into an old family business and you often see a photo near the front counter. A person in a work shirt, squinting into the sun, standing in front of the first truck or the first shopfront. Nobody walking past knows what the business turned over the year it was taken. What they know is what that person was like. They paid on time. They kept people on through a bad winter. The apprentice they took on runs the place now. That is the whole distinction. The numbers are how a business is measured. The person is how it is remembered. And the person is also what decides how big the numbers ever get. Keep that photo in your head. We will come back to it.

Who growth asks you to become has four parts. First, what you call yourself. Second, how you decide. Third, how the work gets done. Fourth, what you are looking after.

Start with what you call yourself.

Whatever you call yourself becomes the size of the box you work in. A plumber who owns a business has a ceiling, and it is set by his own hours. An owner of plumbing businesses has a different job, even if he is the same person on the same Monday. The name you give yourself is not a title. It is the height of your ceiling.

Think about rowing and sailing. A rower who wants to go faster pulls harder. The speed comes straight out of his arms, so the limit is how strong he is and how long he lasts. A sailor who wants to go faster does not pull anything. He reads the wind, trims the sail, picks the line. Same water. Completely different person at the helm. Operators row. Owners learn to sail. Neither is a better person. Only one of them is going to cross open water.

Look at your own business card. What is written first, your trade or the word owner. For a lot of owners it is the trade. That is not humility. That is the box.

Now say a couple of weeks after the drawing, your accountant sends a two-line email. The owner of the other business like yours, down the highway, is thinking of retiring. Might be worth a chat. You read it on your phone in a supplier's car park, and your first thought arrives before you can stop it. I do the work. I do not buy businesses. You do not reply for four days. That hesitation is not caution. It is the old name talking.

Part of you is pushing back, and fair enough. You like being on the tools. It is half the reason you started. The question underneath is real. If I become this other person, what do I lose. Think about a hermit crab. It grows until the shell gets tight, then it has to leave that shell and find a bigger one. For a little while in between it is soft and exposed, and that is the bit that feels dangerous. But the crab that refuses to move does not stay safe. It just stays squeezed. The in-between is uncomfortable. Staying put is a slower kind of uncomfortable.

So run it properly. Ask yourself three things. What do I lose if I move forward. What do I gain by losing it. How could I keep both. The first answer is usually something like the hands-on part, or knowing every customer by name. The second is usually time, and range, and a business that does not stop when you do. The third is where most owners surprise themselves. You can keep the part of the work you love. You just stop being the only one who can do it.

Then there is the money fear. Could buying a second business put the first one at risk. Could it cost you the house. That fear deserves a real answer, not a pep talk.

There is one line on a retiring owner's balance sheet that decides whether you could buy them with little or none of your own cash. That line is free cash flow. The rule over it is one and a half times. Let me show you on an illustrative example.

Say a business makes three hundred thousand dollars a year in profit. The price is three and a half times that, about one point zero five million, funded by a lender and by the seller being paid over time out of the business, not out of your savings. The profit has to be at least one and a half times what the business pays in debt each year. Three hundred thousand divided by one and a half is two hundred thousand. That is the most it should ever be asked to repay in a year.

Now the worst case. A bad first year, and profit drops by a third, to two hundred thousand. The repayments are still covered. And the walls. The new business carries its own debt and does not lean on the one you already have. There are three years of financials, matching tax returns and proper due diligence before you sign. Whether a lender wants a personal guarantee is in the terms, in front of you, before anything is signed. If it does not stack up, you walk away.

A word of caution. No money down does not mean no risk. The risk sits in the debt and in the seller's terms, and you read both. This is general information, not financial advice, and every lender and every deal is different. If you would like to see what that looks like on your own numbers, the link is below. But the second piece is the one that changes the most, so stay with me.

The second piece is how you decide.

In my view, a lot of owners make a decision in about two minutes, then take two years to give themselves permission to act on it. Not because they are slow. Because every decision feels like it has to be made fresh, with everything riding on it. Think about how a pilot takes off. There is a speed on the runway called V1. Below it, if something goes wrong, they stop. Above it, they keep going, because stopping would be more dangerous than flying. V1 is not decided on the runway. It is worked out before the plane leaves the gate, from the weight, the weather and the length of the strip.

Deciding like an owner looks like that. You set your criteria before the moment arrives. One and a half times cover. Three years of financials. Tax returns that match the books. If a deal clears them, you go. If it does not, you stop. The decision was already made at the gate. There is never a perfect moment. Owners who wait to be a hundred per cent sure usually wait forever. Owners who decide at about sixty per cent, on criteria they set in advance, are the ones who end up owning more than one business.

So walk it through. On the fifth day you are sitting in the car, and before you have seen a single one of the retiring owner's numbers, you turn over a quote pad and write three lines on the back. One and a half times. Three years. Returns match. Then you ring him. Over the next couple of months the numbers come through, and they clear all three lines. That turns out to be the easy part. The hard part comes in his office on a Wednesday afternoon, a mug of tea going cold in front of you, and on the wall behind him a faded photo of a much younger version of him beside his first ute. He leans back and asks the one thing you have not prepared for. What happens to his people.

And you, who can price a job in your head before the customer finishes describing it, do not have an answer. You say something about keeping things the same, and you hear how thin it sounds. He nods, says he will think about it, and walks you to the car. You drive home with the radio off. That silence is the sound of your current identity costing you a deal. Hold that.

I have not always had the answer ready either. At twenty-five I was unemployed, no savings, zero experience. Before my first deal landed, four deals fell over, and I made around a thousand cold calls. The person who made the thousandth call was not the same person who made the first one. That is not a numbers story. That is a who you become story. My first deal was a physio clinic, one hundred per cent vendor finance, seven years at four per cent, a nine-month payment holiday and no deposit. I pulled a hundred and fifty thousand out in year one and built mid six figures in equity. That was my path. Results like that are not typical, and it is not a promise. But notice what changed before the numbers changed. I changed.

So ask yourself straight. Which decision in your business have you already made, and are still waiting for permission to act on.

The third piece is how the work gets done.

There is a pattern most owners recognise once it is said out loud. Below about five million, a business mostly lives or dies on the product and the marketing. From five to ten, it lives or dies on its people and teams. Beyond ten, on its systems. Each level asks for something the last one never needed. Think about the best bricklayer on a building site. Fast, neat, every course dead level, and the walls he lays are the ones people stop to look at. Down the road, a builder is running five sites at once and will not lay a single brick all week, and five houses go up. He is not a better bricklayer. He has stopped being the bricklayer.

That is the shift the second business forces on you, whether you plan for it or not. You cannot lay bricks on two sites at once. So you build the managers, the meeting, the one-page report. And the moment you do, the first business gets better too, because it was waiting for exactly the same thing. You are either the leverage in your business, or you have leverage. Right now, if you are honest, most of the leverage in your business is you. Your hours. Your judgement. Your phone. Owning a second business is how you move from being the leverage to having it, because the work you do starts to multiply through other people instead of stopping at the end of your own day.

The fourth piece is what you are looking after.

When you buy a retiring owner's business, you are not only buying a profit line. You are taking over thirty years of somebody's life. Their staff. Their customers. Their name on the sign. And what most of them want is not the highest bidder. It is someone they trust to look after it. One of our Owners Academy members, Saul, cold-called the owner of an electrical and air-conditioning business. About three point eight million in revenue, twenty-five staff, and an owner planning to retire in two years. Saul has signed the NDA and is in early due diligence. It is not closed, and it may not close. But think about what is sitting on the other side of that table. Twenty-five people whose working lives depend on who takes over.

Here is what this looks like in real rooms. My good friend Cam closed two deals in twenty-three days, both at one hundred per cent vendor finance, interest-only for ten years with balloons at years three and ten, and the bank put in five hundred thousand in working capital. My friend Jason Nagy's first deal was a five point five million dollar radiology business at zero down, on a sixty-forty structure. Seven years later he exited to private equity for two hundred million. Those are their situations. Results like that are not typical. And a friend of mine in the US, Elliot, made around forty offers and signed three letters of intent, all at one hundred per cent seller finance, on ninety-nine-year terms set up like a pension plan for the seller. One example is three hundred and fifty thousand a year for life, on a business making five hundred thousand. Results like that are not typical. But look at what the seller is really buying with that structure. Not a price. Being looked after for the rest of his life, out of the thing he built.

Think about a beekeeper taking over someone else's hives. The good ones move a hive at night, when every bee is home, and they keep the frames, the comb and the queen exactly as they were. The bees barely notice the hive has changed hands. Tear the comb apart to rebuild it your own way, and they leave. That is stewardship. Staff and customers do not leave a business because it has been sold. They leave a change. For a retiring owner, the best handover is the one where very little changes. The same faces on Monday. The same customers looked after. Their income still arriving, out of the business they built. Being the person who can promise that, and mean it, is most of what wins the deal.

Part of you might be thinking, I am not a boardroom type. I worry too much about my people to be a dealmaker. Turn that over. A retiring owner is not looking for a dealmaker. They are looking for the person who will still be thinking about their staff at nine o'clock at night. The fact that you worry about your people is not the thing that disqualifies you. It is the thing a good seller is hoping to find.

Which is what you work out on that quiet drive home. The next week you go back to his office with a page, not a pitch. His leading hand, by name, running his workshop. Every apprentice carried over. Him in two days a week for six months, if he wants it, so his customers hear it from him. The sign stays. He reads it twice. Then he looks over his shoulder at the photo of himself with the first ute, and back at you, and tells you to send the letter.

So here is the turn. You started this believing growth was about the numbers, and the person would sort itself out along the way. Look at that photo by the front counter again. Most owners think it is the reward at the end, the thing you get remembered for once the growth is done. It is the other way round. The person in that photo is what made the growth. Become the owner the bigger business needs, and the numbers have somewhere to go.

Now let me show you what that person's month looks like.

Picture the first Monday of the month, a couple of years out. Three managers around a table, one page each. Revenue, margin, cash, people. You are not answering questions. You are asking them. Why did the second site's margin slip. Who is ready to step up when the leading hand at the first site retires. Whether the owner up the coast who rang last week is worth a coffee. The meeting is done by eleven. Nobody needed you to fix anything. They needed you to decide a couple of things, and you set the criteria for both months ago. You walk out to the car park and notice your shoulders are sitting lower than they used to.

And that evening you are at the table at home at six. Nobody has to explain to the kids that you are on the phone. Much further out, maybe it is one of your kids in that chair on the first Monday of the month, and you are at the end of the table, mostly listening. Or maybe they choose something else, and the group gives them that choice. Either way, it is something you hand on, not a job they have to inherit.

Now the logic, because you should want to check the feeling.

Say, as an illustration, your business makes three hundred thousand on three million. One acquisition the size of the example we ran, structured at one and a half times, roughly doubles you in a single settlement. A second one, a few years later, can take you to around three times where you are today. That is an illustration, not a promise, and every deal is different. But notice what it took. Not three times the hours. A different kind of owner.

Here is something to try tonight. Think of the last three judgement calls you made in the business that you were proud of. Next to each, write the rule you were really using, the one in your head that nobody else could see. Every rule you can write down is a decision someone else could make. That is the first business you would need to free up before the second one arrives. Then take the profit of one business in your industry you would love to own, and divide it by one and a half. That is the most it should ever repay in a year. Two numbers. One about the deal. One about you.

So what does it cost to keep believing growth is only about the numbers.

Nothing dramatic. You keep pushing the numbers up, a few per cent a year, and they mostly go up. But the business stays built around you. It cannot run for a month without you, so it cannot be sold at a good number, and it cannot be handed to anyone without handing them your job. You end up owning something very valuable that nobody else can hold. Run your own next five years forward, both ways, as an illustration. On the first path, you keep rowing harder. The numbers are up, and so are the hours, and the sentence your kids use has not changed. On the second, you spend the same five years learning to sail. Two businesses, a monthly meeting with managers who run them, and an owner who can be away for a fortnight without the phone ringing. Same you. Same effort. The only difference is which person you decided to become, and when you decided it.

You have made this decision before. Remember your first pair of steel caps. They rubbed for a month. Your heels were raw and you wondered if you would ever get used to them. Then one morning you pulled them on without thinking, and they were just your boots. The day you hired your first person felt the same. You handed over work you used to do yourself, it felt wrong for a while, and the business did not fall over. It grew. This is the same decision, at the next level.

And you are not starting from nothing. You have the profit that proves you can operate, the team that runs things when you are off the floor, and the industry knowledge that lets you see a good business before anyone else does. What you have been short of is time and a map, and we work through this with you, not for you, a few focused hours a week.

Now the second drawing I promised you. Picture it eighteen months after that Tuesday at the fridge. Same project, same class, a new sheet of paper. Same stick figure, same hair. But this time it is sitting at the end of a long table with three other stick figures around it, and the speech bubble says, What do you reckon. And underneath, in careful letters, a line about you owning two businesses, asking people what they think, and being home for tea. That drawing is the sentence I have been talking about.

So come back to that photo by the front counter one last time. Nobody will remember your revenue in the year it was taken. They will remember who you were to the people standing in it. You are the kind of owner who measures growth by who you become, not just by what you bank. You are the kind of owner who decides at the gate, builds people instead of laying every brick yourself, and looks after what someone else spent thirty years building. The numbers tend to follow the person.

And here is the question I promised you, the one that decides the drawing. Not can I afford to grow. But who would I have to become to own three of these, and do I want to be that person.

There are owners who grow the numbers, and owners who grow into the owner the numbers need. Which one are you going to be.
