Start with what is true. Revenue matters. It is the first line anyone reads, and it is how the bank and your competitors size you up. But there is growing the business, and there is growing what it is worth. They are not the same road.

Part of you might already be thinking, fair enough, but buying businesses to chase a valuation sounds like gambling with the one thing I have built. That is the right instinct, so let me kill it before any upside.

Here is what a deal looks like at its worst, in illustrative numbers. Say you buy a business making three hundred thousand a year in profit, at four times. That is a price of one point two million. It is funded by a lender and by the seller being paid over time, and it is sized so that business's own profit covers the yearly repayments at least one and a half times. Three hundred thousand divided by one and a half is two hundred thousand. That is the most it should ever be asked to repay in a year.

Now run the worst case. Profit drops by a third in a bad year, down to two hundred thousand. The repayments still get made. And say the bigger idea never comes off. Nobody ever offers you more than four times for anything. What are you left with.

A business that paid for itself out of its own profit. And once the debt is cleared, three hundred thousand a year that you own outright and did not have before.

Around that there are walls. The new business never leans on the one you already own. The first piece is what is actually being bought.

Think about the stands at a big regional field day. The busiest one is usually giving away free caps and a sausage, and there is a crowd three deep all weekend. Further down the row there is a quieter stand, four people in folding chairs, and the rep is writing orders. On the Sunday afternoon, one stand had the crowd. The other had the business.

Revenue is the crowd. It is what everyone can see. A serious buyer walks straight past it to look at the order book, and the order book is profit.

Here is how revenue and value part ways, purely as an illustration. Say over three years you take revenue from four million to six. But to win the extra work you sharpen your prices, take on a few jobs you would normally pass on, and add the people to deliver it. Your margin slides from ten per cent to seven.

Four million at ten per cent is four hundred thousand of profit. Six million at seven per cent is four hundred and twenty.

Two million more revenue. Twenty thousand more profit. At four times, eighty thousand more value.

Is that the trade you thought you were making.

That is the first distinction. There is revenue, and there is the money that is left. And that is what your accountant shows you the following Monday. A marker, a whiteboard, two lines. The second piece is how a buyer turns profit into a price.

Value is profit times a multiple. You probably know that much. What most owners never get told is what the multiple actually measures.

Come back to the tank and the bore. The tank is full after a good wet season. The water in it is worth what it is, and once it is used, it is gone. The bore is worth something completely different, because you are paying for water that has not come up yet. The only question is how sure you are that it will.

That is what a multiple is. Last year's profit is water in the tank. A buyer is not paying for that. They are paying for the bore. When someone offers four times profit, they are paying four years of water up front, for a bore they believe will keep pumping after you have gone.

So every question a serious buyer asks is really a question about the bore. Does the profit keep coming if the owner steps away. Is it spread across a lot of customers, or sitting on two. Has it held up through a bad year. Are the records good enough to prove any of it.

Ask any joiner about timber. Green timber and kiln-dried timber look identical on the rack. Same species, same size, same grain. Build a cabinet with the green and it will look perfect in the showroom, then twist by the first winter in someone's house. A good joiner pays more for kiln-dried, not because it looks better on the day, but because it will stay true after it leaves the workshop.

A buyer is paying for how true your profit stays after it leaves your hands.

That is the second piece. The multiple is not a fact about your industry. It is a measure of how much a stranger trusts your profit to still be there when you are not. And right now, that trust is probably lower than you think.

The third piece is the kind of growth.

There is growth that adds weight, and there is growth that adds strength.

Picture two blokes who both step on the bathroom scales in spring, six kilos heavier than they were in autumn. One spent the winter in the gym. The other spent it on the couch with the heater on. The scales give them exactly the same reading. Ask either of them to carry something heavy up a hill, and you will find out very quickly the reading was not the point.

Revenue is the bathroom scale. It tells you the business got heavier. It cannot tell you how.

Growth that adds weight looks like a single customer who becomes forty per cent of your revenue. Work that only you can quote. Thinner margins to win volume. More staff reporting straight to you. Every one of those adds revenue, and every one makes the bore look a bit less certain. So the multiple stays put, or slips.

Growth that adds strength looks different. A manager who runs the place when you are away. Customers spread wide, so no single one can hurt you. Work that repeats. Systems that mean the job gets done the same way whoever is doing it. Some of that adds revenue and some of it does not. All of it makes the profit more certain.

Here is a small yes. If two businesses made the same profit, and one kept running while its owner took a month off, which one would you pay more for. You would. So would every buyer.

And you already think this way when you hire. Two tradies, same ticket, same rate. One needs checking on every job. The other you can send anywhere. You would pay the second more without blinking, because you are paying for certainty, not hours.

Part of you might be thinking, that is all very well, but businesses in my industry only ever sell at about three times. Maybe. Some industries do sit lower. But think about a wool sale. Two clips from the same district, same breed of sheep. One has been classed properly, so every bale is exactly what the label says. That clip gets the better price, because the buyer does not have to open every bale to trust it. The district sets the range. The classing decides where you land in it.

Your industry sets the range. Where you sit in it is set by you. And most owners never do the classing.

The fourth piece is the move that changes the number fastest.

There is a well-worn path in private business. Buy smaller businesses at around four times profit. Grow them, combine them, put the right people in to run them. Then sell the group, as one sturdier business, at eight, ten, sometimes fifteen times. Nothing about that is guaranteed. But it is the same profit, valued differently, because of what it now belongs to.

Why would anyone pay more for the same dollar of profit inside a group.

Think about a light plane with one engine and one with two. On the twin, if an engine fails over the hills, the other one gets you home. Same pilot, same fuel, same destination. But the twin is the one people trust over rough country, because one problem does not bring the whole thing down.

That is your first winter as a group. One site loses its biggest customer for two months, and the other keeps flying. The repayments go out on time from the business that took them on. You breathe out. And when the customer comes back in spring, nobody has panicked in between.

That is what a group is to a buyer.
