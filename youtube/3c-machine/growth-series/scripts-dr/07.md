Your Business Ends Where Your Week Ends

There is a knot between your shoulder blades. Hot water cannot touch it.

Stay with that. An eight hundred thousand dollar tender you never got to in April is now being built by a competitor across town. His name is on the sign. Yours is not.

The dishes are done. The house has gone quiet. The footy is on mute. The client has changed the plan again. The foreman needs to know what to tell them at seven in the morning.

The person beside you says nothing. They do not need to. They have watched this exact scene too many times. Your chest goes tight, the same way it does most nights, and you swipe to answer.

Forty minutes later, you are still on the call. Under the whole conversation runs a thought you have had so often it no longer needs saying. Nobody else would handle this the way I would.

Park that call. We are coming back to it. We are coming back to the week it finally breaks.

Underneath that Tuesday sits one belief. Most owners say it out loud every week. Nobody can do it like me.

Here is the strange part. That sentence is usually true. And it is the exact reason the business stopped growing.

The sentence you are proud of is the sentence that owns you.

I am going to show you why. By the end, you will see the exit.

Start with what is true. You are better at it. You earned that. The first job you priced on the back of an envelope. The first person you hired and showed where everything lived. The years spent learning which client pays late, which job blows out, what gets priced in and what gets eaten. That knowledge cost you years. You have every right to be proud of it.

But there is being the best person at the work, and there is being the person the work has to pass through. They feel identical. They are not. The first is a skill. The second is a structure.

And a structure can be changed in a way a skill never has to be.

Think of the old telephone exchange. Every call in town ran through one switchboard. One operator in a headset, plugging cables into holes. She was brilliant. She knew every number by heart. The town could never make more calls in an hour than her two hands could connect.

That is most owner-run businesses. You are the switchboard. Every quote, every problem, every can-you-just-look-at-this routes through you. The business moves no faster than your nervous system.

That is the ceiling. Not the market. Not the marketing. The number of cables one person can plug in before nine o'clock at night.

Now break that sentence apart. There are four pieces.

The first is what it actually is. The second is why one business never forces the change, and the one thing that does. The third is the layer between you and the work, and a number that changes how a manager looks to you. The fourth is the job that is left once that layer is in place.

Along the way I will also show you why the thing you think rules you out is the very thing that makes you good at this. Hold that.

Before the pieces, the fear, because it deserves a straight answer.

It sounds like this. If I take my hands off, standards slip. If I buy another business on top of that, I do both badly and risk the one I spent twenty years building.

So here is the worst case in plain numbers, with walls around it. These numbers are purely illustrative.

Say the business you buy makes four hundred thousand a year in profit. The rule we work to is that its own cash flow covers its yearly debt repayments at least one and a half times. So the repayments are capped at about two hundred and sixty-seven thousand a year. They come out of that business, not yours.

Now the worst happens. The manager does not work out. Profit drops by a third. Four hundred thousand becomes about two hundred and sixty-seven. On that structure, every repayment still gets made. It is a tight year. The bank is not calling. Your first business is not propping anything up.

That is the first wall. The second wall is that the new business never leans on the one you already own. The third wall is that before you sign, you see three years of financials, matching tax returns, and proper due diligence, and you can walk away from any deal that does not stack up.

This is general information, not financial advice, and every lender and every deal is different.

So the downside is a hard stretch, bounded and carried by the business you bought. Let your shoulders come down one notch on that.

The first piece. What it actually is.

When you say nobody can do it like me, it sounds like one thing. It is really two.

There is the work. And there is the standard.

The work is the quoting, the scheduling, the site visits. The standard is the picture in your head of what good looks like. How a job should be left. What a customer should hear when something goes wrong. What gets priced in and what gets eaten.

The work can be done by other people. The standard is the bit that matters. And you have been passing it on by doing the work yourself, in front of everyone.

Think of a head chef in a good kitchen. The head chef is not on the grill. He stands at the pass, the bench where every plate lands before it goes out. He looks at every single plate. He tastes the sauce. He sends back the one that is not right. The cooks do the work. The chef holds the standard, at the one spot every plate has to cross.

Now take the chef who believes nobody can cook like him, so he stays on the grill all night. The food might be brilliant. But nobody is at the pass. The kitchen can only send out as many meals as one pair of hands can make.

You have been on the grill. Your standard is fine. It is just standing in the wrong place.

Ask yourself something hard to un-ask. If you were at the pass instead of the grill, what would you be checking.

The second piece. Why one business never forces the change.

You have tried to step back before. You hired someone good, handed over a chunk of work, and for a few months it went well. Then a rough week. A big client complains, a job runs over, somebody quits. Because you are right there, and because it is quicker, you step back in. Just this once.

Then twice. Then you are back on the grill, and the person you hired is doing a smaller job than you hired them for.

That is not weakness. It is what one business does. There is always enough slack in your week to absorb one more problem, so nothing ever forces the handover to stick.

Think of a garden. One garden, you can water it by hand every evening. Then you take on a second garden across town. You cannot stand at both with a hose. So for the first time, you install drip lines and a timer.

Here is the bit nobody expects. The first garden ends up better watered than it ever was by hand. Because the timer does not get tired and does not take phone calls.

A second business is that second garden. You physically cannot stand in two yards. The handover stops being something you will get round to. It becomes something that has to be built, properly, before settlement.

And the business you already own gets fixed along the way. Whatever works for one site has to be written down for both.

So you came in thinking a second business would make you more necessary. It does the opposite. It is the first thing that has ever made it impossible for you to be the switchboard.

And that is where the numbers start to turn.

The third piece. The layer.

Between you and the work there needs to be a person, or a few, who hold the standard day to day. Most owners know it and still have not hired one. Ask why, and the answer is nearly always the same. Cannot justify it.

So justify it, with illustrative numbers. Say your business makes six hundred thousand a year in profit. A manager who could genuinely run it might cost one hundred and sixty thousand. That is more than a quarter of your profit, spent on someone to do what you are already doing for free. So you keep doing it.

Except it is not free. You are paying for it in evenings.

Now add a business you bought, making four hundred thousand. Combined profit is a million. That same manager, working across both, is sixteen per cent of it. Twenty-seven per cent on one side. Sixteen on the other. Same person, same salary.

Nobody puts up scaffolding to paint one window frame. You get the ladder out and do it yourself. For a whole two-storey, you would be mad not to. The layer is the scaffolding. One business makes it optional. Two make it obvious.

And look at what came with it. Four hundred thousand of profit on top of your six hundred is a business that has grown by two-thirds in a single settlement. The purchase is paid for out of the profit of the business you bought, with seller finance, bank debt, and often none of your own cash, at one and a half times cover, with room left for a bad year. That is the arithmetic of buying instead of building. The numbers are illustrative, not a promise of what you will find.

It is still a real hire. It might not work out first time. And once you have hired, you have to let them do it. That is the harder part.

Think of a parent who does their kid's maths homework every night. The homework comes back perfect. Ticks all the way down. Then the exam comes, and the kid sits there with a pencil and no idea, because they never had to work a single problem out on their own.

That is what stepping back in does to a manager. The week looks perfect, because you fixed it, so the next problem lands on you as well. They will not solve every problem the way you would. Some they will solve better. The only way they ever learn is if you put the pencil down, which a second business makes you do.

Which brings us back to the highway. What you admitted to yourself, an hour into that drive, is this. I am the reason we cannot grow.

The fourth piece. The job that is left once the layer is there.

There is operating a business, and there is chairing one.

Operating is answering. Chairing is asking.

When you operate, the questions come to you all day and you answer them. When you chair, you sit down once a month with the people who run each business and ask the three or four questions only an owner thinks to ask. Where is the margin going on that kind of job. Who is ready for more. What would you do with another crew.

On a paving job, a good leading hand does not eyeball every paver. He sets a string line once, tight, at exactly the right height, and then anyone on the crew can lay to it. The standard lives in the string, not in his eye.

That is what a one-page monthly report does for an owner who chairs. You set the line once, and everyone lays to it. It is the pass, written down.

Here is what that looks like in your world, about a year on.

It is the first Thursday of the month. Ten in the morning. A table, a pot of coffee, two managers, one from each business, each with a single page in front of them. Revenue, margin, cash, jobs won and lost, people. You have read both pages before they have finished pouring. You ask why the second site's margin dipped in the second week, and the manager already knows, because it is her number now, not yours. One hire, one price change, ninety minutes.

And the world around you has quietly rearranged itself.

The bank manager asks for that monthly page. At a mate's fiftieth, two owners you have known for years corner you by the barbecue and want to know how you got off the tools. They corner you because they can smell the difference. They are still on the tools at eight o'clock at night. You are not. Your foreman tells a new apprentice the boss trusts him to run the show, and the apprentice repeats it at smoko like it is the reason he took the job.

Nobody calls you chairman. You would laugh if they did. You just run the room that runs the businesses.

Now the part I promised you.

Nobody tells you this about the head chef. The chef on the grill can only taste the plates they cooked. The chef at the pass tastes every single one.

So nobody can do it like me was never the problem. It is true. It is the reason you should be at the pass. Owners who do not care about standards hand over and walk off. You are the one who will stand there checking every plate, across two kitchens, then three. The sentence you thought kept you on the grill is the qualification for leaving it.

Now the logic, because you should want to check this for yourself.

Get a sheet of paper. Draw a line down the middle. On the left, write every call, text and question you answered last week. On the right, next to each one, write the role of the person who could have handled it, if they had been shown what good looks like.

For most owners, most of the left-hand column has something beside it. Those are not your hours. They belong to a role nobody has been hired for yet, and you have been doing it unpaid, at night.
