**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

One. Time. Not "free time". Hours you can name on a calendar from Monday. The people I see get a deal done are giving this 40 hours a week at the very least. 60 is better. 100 is ideal while you're building. If you can't point to the hours, you haven't committed time.

Two. Money. The least important of the three, and the one people worry about most. A structure replaces most of it. The seller gets paid over time out of the business's profit. What money does change is speed. With cash, an existing business or property behind you, a first company in the next 30 days becomes possible. Without it, it's slower. Not impossible.

Three. Energy. Where your attention actually goes. I've had people pay for advisory and still not take action. Paid, signed up, and then nothing. Where your energy goes decides whether this works. Not your bank balance. Not your CV.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME*

**Time: busy hours vs income hours (3:00)**

If you've been "working on this" for a year and nothing's happened, it has nothing to do with your intelligence, your background or your bank balance. It has everything to do with where the hours went. Nobody ever showed you the difference between busy hours and income hours.

Most people who say they're giving this 20 hours a week are giving it 20 hours of reading. Podcasts. Spreadsheets. Another video. Like this one.

Income hours are the ones that put you in front of a seller, a board member or a lender. Calls. Offers. Lender interviews. Everything else is support.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers."]

Illustrative week. 40 hours. Put 15 of them into calls. Even at 15 dials an hour, that's over 200 dials a week. A thousand in about five weeks. Another 10 hours on board outreach and lenders. 10 on financials, NDAs and offers for whatever the calls turn up. 5 for everything else.

I made about a thousand cold calls, and four deals fell over, before my first one closed. A physio clinic. That thousand calls was time. There's no shortcut through it. You can only get through it faster.

*// BELIEF SHIFT: WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING*

**Money: what it buys and what it doesn't (4:20)**

You were told you need a deposit. You were told you need a track record. You were told the bank decides.

None of that was true for my first deal. 100% vendor finance: seven years at 4%, a nine-month payment holiday, rent in arrears, no deposit.

[SCREEN: "Results not typical; records on file. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

So money doesn't buy you the business. The structure does. And the reason money still matters is that it buys three things. Speed, because a lender takes you more seriously with assets behind you. Room for mistakes, because your first deal will have some. And a runway, so you can live while the business pays its debt first.

Daniel Iles, who built Viral Coach to over $5 million a month, has a test I like. Can you go months without taking money out of the business? If you can't, he says, expect to stay small.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Scared of being broke' · 8:39 · link in description"]

Apply that to buying. A payment holiday gives the business time to breathe after settlement. Pay yourself from day one and you've eaten the breathing room. So the money question isn't "do I have a deposit?" It's "can I live for a while without drawing much from the first business?" That's a planning question. Answer it on paper now.

**Energy: the one nobody measures (5:30)**

Energy kills more deals than money. And nobody tracks it.

Low energy looks like this. Three side projects. A plan to start "after this quarter." A spouse who hasn't been asked. Another book before the first call.

Daniel has two lines on this. The first: "In life, there is no neutral." Standing still is falling behind.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Give me 4 minutes and you will never need motivation again' · 2:38"]

The second is the useful one. You don't need motivation. You need one point of reference that what you want is possible for someone else. A year after he had $400 in the bank, his company was doing more than $200,000 a day. He didn't wait to feel motivated. He had a reference point.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · same video · 4:12"]

So pick yours. Me at 25 with no money. Christian Dawes, who had a $2.4 million first deal funded 100% by the bank while unemployed, with no higher education and a criminal record. Jason with the radiology deal. One reference point. Then put your energy where it goes.

[SCREEN: "Christian Dawes: an example, not a student. Results not typical; records on file."]

And energy has a cost. Daniel again: "You cannot have it all. There is always opportunity cost." Every hour on a side project is an hour not on the phone.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Got fit, started making $3M per month' · 16:41"]

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**The honest audit (7:00)**

Score yourself. One to three on each.

Time: one is "when I can." Two is ten to twenty named hours. Three is 40-plus, on the calendar, starting Monday.

Money: one is nothing. Two is some savings. Three is cash, a business or property you could put to work.

Energy: one is "still deciding." Two is "doing it, alongside three other things." Three is "this is the thing."

A one on money is fine. A one on time or energy isn't. I'd rather work with someone who scores three, one, three than three, three, one. The second person has the money and will still be watching videos next year.

[SCREEN: the three bars. "Money can be a 1. Time and energy can't."]

And the order matters. First you fix time, because hours are the only thing you control this week. Then, once the hours are booked, energy follows, because the calls start giving you real conversations. And once you have real conversations, the money question answers itself, because now you're talking about one business's numbers instead of a fear.

**Do this this week (8:30)**

[SCREEN: checklist]

1. Book your income hours into the calendar for the next four weeks. Name them: "calls", "board outreach", "lenders". If they're not booked, they're not real.
2. Write down every project, course and side thing on the go. Cross off one this week.
3. Work out how many months you could live without drawing much from a business. Write the number down.
4. Pick your one reference point and put it somewhere you'll see it.
5. Make 20 calls. "Have you ever thought about selling?"

## Episode 97 · Walk-away power: why the buyer who can leave gets the best deal

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Walk-away power: why the buyer who can leave gets the best deal
  2. The best negotiation tactic in buying a business is to walk away
  3. How to negotiate with a seller without raising your price
- **Thumbnail text:** READY TO WALK
- **Runtime target:** 12:00 (≈1,850 words)
- **Stage lit:** Closing (Act 20, The long game, episode 2 of 5)
- **ICP:** HNW Capital Deployer (has capital; fears overpaying and can't yet vet deals)
- **Opener:** Chad vs James (contrast)

### Script

*// CHAD VS JAMES OPENER · ICP: HNW Capital Deployer*

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: a negotiating table, one empty chair]

Two buyers look at the same business. James has the cash, the spreadsheet and one deal he's fallen in love with. The other buyer has five conversations, a number written down, and a board who'll tell him "no."

The seller asks both for more. James pays it. The other buyer says "No worries. Good luck." And gets the better deal.

*// 7-MINUTE PROMISE*

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. "Closing" lit. Corner tag: "Act 20 · The long game · 2 of 5"]

This is Closing, from the long-game angle. Episode two of five. Last time: time, money, energy. Today: the one thing that protects all three once you're across the table from a seller.

In the next ten minutes I'll show you how to build walk-away power before you need it, so you never pay a price the business can't carry. All without being rude, bluffing or playing games.

This is for people sending offers, or about to, especially if you've got capital and the fear is overpaying. It's not for anyone who thinks negotiating means being nice until the seller gives in.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Walk-away power isn't a tactic you use on the day. It's a position you build before the day. You either have other deals and a number you won't cross, or you don't. Sellers can tell.

Two. Your walk line is maths, not feelings. Free cash flow has to cover repayments at least 1.5 times. That gives you the most you can pay, and on what terms. Above that, the deal can kill you in a slow year. So you leave.

Three. When the seller pushes, move the terms before you move the number. Longer, later, slower. Hold the line on what the business can carry.

[SCREEN: "1. Build the position before the day · 2. Walk line = 1.5× maths · 3. Move terms, not your line"]

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's the whole lesson. I help people do this: find, structure and negotiate the first business, bought with little to no money down. If you want someone next to you when a seller pushes back, book a call below. It's me, not a setter. Now the detail.

[SCREEN: "Book a call with Josh · link below"]

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME*

**Why the buyer who can walk wins (3:00)**

If you've ever overpaid, or nearly did, it had nothing to do with being soft, or bad with numbers, or not wanting it enough. It had everything to do with having one deal. A buyer with one deal is a buyer who'll say yes to anything.

You've spent three months on it. You've told your partner. You've pictured the first day. The seller asks for more money up front, and you hear yourself say "I'll see what I can do."

A buyer with five deals in the pipeline says "No worries. Good luck." And means it.

And the reason that works is simple. It's not about being tough. It's about having somewhere else to go. That's why we run parallel pipelines and never single-thread a deal. And it's why the thousand calls matter after the first deal too. Volume gives you options. Options give you the ability to leave.

[JOSH: a deal you walked away from: what the seller asked for, what you said, and what happened next (did they come back, or was it the right call to leave?)]

**The "other buyers" line (4:00)**

You will hear it. "I've got other buyers interested." Often it's a bluff. Sometimes it isn't. You don't need to know which.

The answer is the same either way. "That's great, you should talk to them. If it doesn't work out, my offer's here." Calm. No chasing. If they really have a better buyer, you were never getting the deal. If they don't, they'll call back.

And if the offer needs a nudge, that's where the time limit comes in. "This offer's open for 24 hours." Not to bully. To stop a deal drifting for months.

*// BELIEF SHIFT: THAT'S NOT HOW IT WORKS*

**Your walk line is maths (4:50)**

Most people think negotiating is a personality. Hard men win. Nice people get taken.

That's not how it works. The buyer who wins did the maths before the meeting.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Illustrative numbers. A business makes $300,000 profit a year. Divide by 1.5. About $200,000 a year can go to repayments. Over ten years, about $2 million paid to the seller over time. Interest left out to keep it simple.

Now the seller says $2.5 million. Same ten years, that's $250,000 a year. Your cover drops to 1.2. One slow year and you're borrowing to pay the seller.

So $2 million over ten years is your line. You don't need to think in the room.

[SCREEN: "$300K profit ÷ 1.5 = $200K/yr · × 10 yrs = $2M · Seller asks $2.5M → cover 1.2×"]

**Move the terms, not your line (5:50)**

Here's where Daniel Iles comes in. He sells, he doesn't buy, but the principle runs both ways. When a prospect says money's the problem, he changes the terms, not the price. "How much can you put down today?" Same price, different shape.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'I booked 100,000 sales calls' · 1:16:41 · link in description"]

Turn that around for a buyer. When a seller wants more, don't break your line. Change the shape. A longer term. A later start to repayments. Part of the price tied to performance. Each of those can give the seller a bigger headline while the business still carries the debt at 1.5 times.

Illustrative again. $2.5 million, but over 12 and a half years instead of ten. That's back to about $200,000 a year. Your cover's back to 1.5. Seller gets their number. You keep your line.

[SCREEN: "$2.5M ÷ 12.5 yrs = $200K/yr → cover back to 1.5×. Illustrative."]

And if no shape works, you walk. That's the point of having a line.

**Offer your absence (6:55)**

Daniel has another line I love. "If everything you offered was not enough, offer your absence."

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · '$5M/mo objection overcomes' · 0:30"]

He uses it on sales calls. It works with sellers too, because a retiring owner isn't really comparing your offer to another buyer. They're comparing it to doing nothing. Another three years of running the place. Another bank that takes 16 weeks. You remind them, calmly, that the alternative to you is their own Monday morning. Then you leave the room.

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**What gives you the right to walk (7:40)**

Four things build walk-away power, and none of them happen on the day. They happen in order.

First, a pipeline. More than one live deal, always.

Then, once you have deals, a number. Your 1.5 line, done before every meeting.

Then a team. A board and advisers who'll tell you "this one's not it" when you're too close. That's the credibility work from the start of the series doing a second job.

And once you have all that, a runway. If you need this deal to pay your rent next month, you can't walk. That's episode 96 coming back.

**Do this this week (8:30)**

[SCREEN: checklist]

1. Write your walk criteria on one page: the cover you won't go below, the conditions you won't drop, the red flags that end it.
2. Run the 1.5 maths on any live deal or target. Write the most you'd pay over ten years.
3. Write two other shapes that give the seller more without breaking your line.
4. Count your live conversations. If it's one, make 50 calls this week.
5. Practise the line out loud: "No worries. Good luck. My offer's here if it changes."

*// NO CHOICE FRAME*

**The turn (9:00)**

You can keep doing it the way most buyers do. One deal at a time. Falling for it. Moving your line by $100,000, then another, because you're three months in and tired and you're the only person emotionally in the deal. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals, and some of them win the wrong ones.

Or you can have someone outside the deal who's seen the shape before, and who'll say "walk" when you can't.

**Five stages, three seasons, three minutes (0:45–3:45)**

[SCREEN: each stage lights as it's named]

Credibility. You don't have a track record, so you borrow one. A board of credible operators, paid in founders' equity, 2 to 10% each, vesting at the signed purchase agreement. Lawyers and accountants paid at closing. A one-page packet. Now a seller and a bank are dealing with a team, not a person.

Capital. The seller is your first bank. A retiring owner gets paid over time out of the business's profit, often at a better price than a cash buyer would offer, and faster. Add commercial debt where it helps, like a 60/40 split with the seller rolling equity. Two rules, every deal. Free cash flow covers repayments at least 1.5 times. And debt over dilution, so you keep control and the exit.

Closing. Find it off-market with cold calls and one question: "Have you ever thought about selling?" Before any offer, three years of financials, matching tax returns, revenue by year. Offer 3 to 5 times profit in an LOI. Proper due diligence on the quality of the earnings. Good lawyers. And be willing to walk away.

Grow. Once you own it, pull the levers that make it worth more. Price and margin first. A way to buy customers instead of waiting for them. A team, so it runs without you.

Exit. Do it again. Buy several at small-business multiples, combine them, and sell the group at the multiple a bigger buyer pays. Or keep it. Or fix one and sell it.

The speed run, one last time. A business makes $250,000 profit. Divide by 1.5. About $166,000 a year can go to the seller. Over ten years, about $1.66 million. Illustrative. That's above most cash offers, and the $84,000 left over is what keeps you alive in a slow year.

"No money down" is the hook. The 1.5 rule is the safety rail. Never sell one without the other.
