**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Time. Not "free time". Hours you can name on a calendar from Monday. The people I see get a deal done are giving this 40 hours a week at the very least. 60 is better. 100 is ideal while you're building. If you can't point to the hours, you haven't committed time.

Two. Money. The least important of the three, and the one people worry about most. A structure replaces most of it. The seller gets paid over time out of the business's profit. What money does change is speed. If you've got cash, an existing business or property behind you, a first company in the next 30 days becomes possible. Without it, it's slower. Not impossible.

Three. Energy. Where your attention actually goes. I've had people pay for advisory and still not take action. Paid, signed up, and then nothing. Where your energy goes decides whether this works. Not your bank balance. Not your CV.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Time: busy hours vs income hours (3:00)**

Most people who say they're giving this 20 hours a week are giving it 20 hours of reading. Podcasts. Spreadsheets. Another video. Like this one.

Here's the split that matters. Income hours are the ones that put you in front of a seller, a board member or a lender. Calls. Offers. Lender interviews. Everything else is support.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers."]

Illustrative week. 40 hours. Put 15 of them into calls. Even at 15 dials an hour, that's over 200 dials a week. A thousand in about five weeks. Another 10 hours on board outreach and lender conversations. 10 on financials, NDAs and offers for whatever the calls turn up. 5 for everything else.

I made about a thousand cold calls, and four deals fell over, before my first one closed. A physio clinic, at 25, unemployed, no savings. That thousand calls was time. There's no shortcut through it. You can only get through it faster.

**Money: what it buys and what it doesn't (4:15)**

Money doesn't buy you the business. The structure does. My first deal was 100% vendor finance: seven years at 4%, a nine-month payment holiday, rent in arrears, no deposit.

[SCREEN: "Results not typical; records on file. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

What money does buy is three things. Speed, because a lender takes you more seriously with assets behind you. Room for mistakes, because your first deal will have some. And a runway, so you can live while the business pays its debt first.

Daniel Iles, who built Viral Coach to over $5 million a month, has a test I like. Can you go months without taking money out of the business? If you can't, he says, expect to stay small.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Scared of being broke' · 8:39 · link in description"]

Apply that to buying. A payment holiday gives the business time to breathe after settlement. If you need to pay yourself from day one, you've eaten the breathing room. So the money question isn't "do I have a deposit?" It's "can I live for a while without drawing much from the first business?" That's a planning question. Answer it on paper now.

**Energy: the one nobody measures (5:30)**

Energy is the one that kills more deals than money. And nobody tracks it.

Here's what low energy looks like. Three side projects. A plan to start "after this quarter." A spouse who hasn't been asked. Another book before the first call.

Daniel has two lines on this. The first: "In life, there is no neutral." Standing still is falling behind.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Give me 4 minutes and you will never need motivation again' · 2:38"]

The second is the useful one. He says you don't need motivation. You need one point of reference that what you want is possible for someone else. A year after he had $400 in the bank, his company was doing more than $200,000 a day. He didn't wait to feel motivated. He had a reference point.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · same video · 4:12"]

So pick yours. Me at 25 with no money. Christian Dawes, who had a $2.4 million first deal funded 100% by the bank while unemployed, with no higher education and a criminal record. Jason with the radiology deal. One reference point. Then put your energy where it goes.

[SCREEN: "Christian Dawes: an example, not a student. Results not typical; records on file."]

And energy has a cost. Daniel again: "You cannot have it all. There is always opportunity cost." Every hour on a side project is an hour not on the phone.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'I got fit' · 16:41"]

**The honest audit (7:00)**

Score yourself. One to three on each.

Time: one is "when I can." Two is ten to twenty named hours. Three is 40-plus, on the calendar, starting Monday.

Money: one is nothing. Two is some savings. Three is cash, a business or property you could put to work.

Energy: one is "still deciding." Two is "doing it, alongside three other things." Three is "this is the thing."

Here's the part people don't expect. A one on money is fine. A one on time or energy isn't. I'd rather work with someone who scores three, one, three than three, three, one. The second person has the money and will still be watching videos next year.

[SCREEN: the three bars. "Money can be a 1. Time and energy can't."]

## Episode 97 · Walk-away power: why the buyer who can leave gets the best deal

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Walk-away power: why the buyer who can leave gets the best deal
  2. The best negotiation tactic in buying a business is to walk away
  3. How to negotiate with a seller without raising your price
- **Thumbnail text:** READY TO WALK
- **Runtime target:** 11:45 (≈1,750 words)
- **Stage lit:** Closing (Act 20, The long game, episode 2 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: a negotiating table, one empty chair]

The best negotiation tactic in buying a business is to walk away. The second best is a time limit. "You've got 24 hours to accept."

Neither works if you can't actually leave. Today is about building the position where you can.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. "Closing" lit. Corner tag: "Act 20 · The long game · 2 of 5"]

This is Closing, from the long-game angle. Episode two of five. Last time: time, money, energy. Today: the one thing that protects all three once you're across the table from a seller.

This is for people sending offers, or about to. If you haven't made a call yet, it'll still save you a bad deal later. It's not for anyone who thinks negotiating means being nice until the seller gives in.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Walk-away power isn't a tactic you use on the day. It's a position you build before the day. You either have other deals and a number you won't cross, or you don't. Sellers can tell.

Two. Your walk line is maths, not feelings. Free cash flow has to cover repayments at least 1.5 times. That gives you the most you can pay, and on what terms. Above that, the deal can kill you in a slow year. So you leave.

Three. When the seller pushes, move the terms before you move the number. Longer, later, slower. Hold the line on what the business can carry.

[SCREEN: "1. Build the position before the day · 2. Walk line = 1.5× maths · 3. Move terms, not your line"]

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's the whole lesson. I help people do this: find, structure and negotiate the first business, bought with little to no money down. If you want someone next to you when a seller pushes back, book a call below. It's me, not a setter. Now the detail.

[SCREEN: "Book a call with Josh · link below"]

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why the buyer who can walk wins (3:00)**

A buyer with one deal is a buyer who'll say yes to anything. You've spent three months on it. You've told your partner. You've pictured the first day. The seller asks for more money up front, and you hear yourself say "I'll see what I can do."

A buyer with five deals in the pipeline says "No worries. Good luck." And means it.

That's the whole difference. It's not about being tough. It's about having somewhere else to go. That's why we run parallel pipelines and never single-thread a deal. And it's why the thousand calls matter after the first deal too. Volume gives you options. Options give you the ability to leave.

[JOSH: a deal you walked away from: what the seller asked for, what you said, and what happened next (did they come back, or was it the right call to leave?)]

**The "other buyers" line (4:00)**

You will hear it. "I've got other buyers interested." In my experience, most sellers who say that are bluffing. Some aren't. You don't need to know which.

The answer is the same either way. "That's great, you should talk to them. If it doesn't work out, my offer's here." Calm. No chasing. If they really have a better buyer, you were never getting the deal. If they don't, they'll call back.

And if the offer needs a nudge, that's where the time limit comes in. "This offer's open for 24 hours." Not to bully. To stop a deal drifting for months.

**Your walk line is maths (4:50)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Illustrative numbers. A business makes $300,000 profit a year. Divide by 1.5. About $200,000 a year can go to repayments. Over ten years, about $2 million paid to the seller over time. Interest left out to keep it simple.

Now the seller says $2.5 million. Same ten years, that's $250,000 a year. Your cover drops to 1.2. One slow year and you're borrowing to pay the seller.

So $2 million over ten years is your line. You've done the maths before the meeting. You don't need to think in the room.

[SCREEN: "$300K profit ÷ 1.5 = $200K/yr · × 10 yrs = $2M · Seller asks $2.5M → cover 1.2×"]

**Move the terms, not your line (5:50)**

Here's where Daniel Iles comes in. He sells, he doesn't buy, but the principle runs both ways. When a prospect says money's the problem, he changes the terms, not the price. "How much can you put down today?" Same price, different shape.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · '100,000 sales calls' · 1:16:41 · link in description"]

Turn that around for a buyer. When a seller wants more, don't break your line. Change the shape. A longer term. A later start to repayments. Part of the price tied to performance. Each of those can give the seller a bigger headline while the business still carries the debt at 1.5 times.

Illustrative again. $2.5 million, but over 12 and a half years instead of ten. That's back to about $200,000 a year. Your cover's back to 1.5. Seller gets their number. You keep your line.

[SCREEN: "$2.5M ÷ 12.5 yrs = $200K/yr → cover back to 1.5×. Illustrative."]

And if no shape works? You walk. That's the point of having a line.

**Offer your absence (6:55)**

Daniel has another line I love. "If everything you offered was not enough, offer your absence."

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · '$5M/mo objection overcomes' · 0:30"]

He uses it on sales calls. It works with sellers too, because a retiring owner isn't really comparing your offer to another buyer. They're comparing it to doing nothing. Another three years of running the place. Another bank that takes 16 weeks. You remind them, calmly, that the alternative to you is their own Monday morning. Then you leave the room.

**What gives you the right to walk (7:40)**

Four things build walk-away power, and none of them happen on the day.

A pipeline. More than one live deal, always.

A number. Your 1.5 line, done before the meeting.

A team. A board and advisers who'll tell you "this one's not it" when you're too close. That's the credibility work from the start of the series doing a second job.

And a runway. If you need this deal to pay your rent next month, you can't walk. That's episode 96 coming back.

**Do this this week (8:30)**

[SCREEN: checklist]

1. Write your walk criteria on one page: the cover you won't go below, the conditions you won't drop, the red flags that end it.
2. Run the 1.5 maths on any live deal or target. Write the most you'd pay over ten years.
3. Write two other shapes that give the seller more without breaking your line.
4. Count your live conversations. If it's one, make 50 calls this week.
5. Practise the line out loud: "No worries. Good luck. My offer's here if it changes."

**The turn (9:00)**

You can do all of this alone. The maths is in this video. The words are in this video.

Here's what goes wrong alone. You're the only person who's emotionally in the deal, so there's no one to say "walk." Three months in, you can't see it clearly anymore. You move your line by $100,000, then another. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals, and some of them win the wrong ones. Or you do it with me, with someone outside the deal who's seen the shape before.

Or 1:1 advisory through G&L M&A Advisory. I work your strategy, structures and lender presentations with you, and I'm in your corner when the negotiation gets tight. Equity or advisory roles considered case by case.

**For skeptics (10:30)**

"If I walk, I lose the deal." Sometimes. A deal you lose by holding the line at 1.5 times is usually a deal that would have hurt you.

"I don't have a pipeline yet." Then you don't have walk-away power yet. That's fine. Build it before you need it. Calls first.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. The plan is in stages, not years. Roughly: get ready, buy one, buy more, build the group, sell or hold. The years are a guide. The order isn't.

Two. Your job changes at every stage. You start as a caller. Then you're an owner. Then a manager of managers. Then someone who allocates capital. Most people get stuck because they keep doing the last stage's job.

Three. Every stage has one constraint. Find it, fix it, move on. Don't fix three things at once.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative plan. Timelines vary. Not a forecast or a promise."]

**Year 0: get ready (3:00)**

Six months, give or take. You build a board with founders' equity, 2 to 10% each. You line up lawyers and accountants paid at closing. You interview lenders before you need one. And you start the calls.

Your job here is caller. Every hour is calls, board outreach or lenders. The constraint is volume. Daniel Iles says the risk early on is moving too slowly. Later it flips.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Spending $10K vs $1M on ads' · 11:47 · link in description"]

**Year 1: buy one (3:50)**

The first deal. Usually the hardest. Mine took about a thousand calls and four deals that fell over. Pick a business that can carry its debt 1.5 times and that you could build on. Asset-rich, boring, bankable.

Your job switches to owner, overnight. Keep the staff. Keep the customers. Keep the seller close for the handover, anywhere from six months to three years. Then fix price and margin, then a way to bring in customers on purpose.
