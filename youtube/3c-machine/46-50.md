## Episode 46 · Find the margin leaks in your first 30 days

### Metadata

- **Title options:**

- **Thumbnail text:** PRICE EVERY JOB
- **Runtime target:** 11:30

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: a one-page P&L. The line "Total revenue" circled in red.]

The number new owners stare at is revenue. It's the wrong number.

In a lot of businesses you buy, the biggest customer pays the least for every hour of work. It's not on the P&L. You only see it when you price every job.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Grow" lights up. Corner tag: "Act 10 · Grow: profit · 1 of 5"]

This is the 3C Machine. Credibility, Capital and Closing got you the business. Act 9 was the deals that died, and it finished with the dead-deal checklist. This act assumes you got past it. You own one. Now the job is profit. Grow, episode 1 of 5.

It's for people who've bought, or are close, and want to know exactly where the money goes. It's not for anyone planning to put a new business on autopilot from day one. That isn't how year one works.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Price every job, not just the year. The P&L says the business made $300,000. It doesn't say which work made it and which work ate it. You want margin by service line, by customer and by job. That's your unit economics.

Two. Look for leaks before cuts. A leak is money the business earned and didn't collect, or work it did at a loss without noticing. Extra work never billed. Return visits done free. A big account on a discount someone agreed to years ago. Fixing a leak doesn't hurt anyone.

Three. Change nothing visible for 30 days. Measure first. Staff and customers are watching the new owner. Your first month is a spreadsheet, not a speech.

Why this matters in a 3C deal: you're paying the seller out of profit. Every leak you plug goes straight to headroom on that payment. And at the exit, every dollar of profit gets multiplied.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

This is part of what I do with people. Buy the business with little to no money down, then make it worth more. If you want help on your deal, book a call below. Short application, then you talk to me. Now the detail.

[SCREEN: "Book a call with Josh · link below"]

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Six words first (3:00)**

Daniel Iles built Viral Coach to around $5 million a month. He's an accountant by trade, and he has a five-minute lesson called "The math behind crazy profit margins." It starts with six definitions.

Revenue. Cost of goods sold, which is the direct cost of delivering the work. Gross profit. Overhead, the costs you can't pin on any one customer. Net profit. And gross margin: gross profit divided by revenue.

[SCREEN: the six definitions as a list. "Daniel Iles · The math behind crazy profit margins · 0:31 · link in description"]

His point is that gross margin is the lever. You move it two ways: charge more, or deliver for less. We did the price side in episode 27. Today is the step before. Find out where the margin goes.

**The illustrative business (3:45)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Round numbers, and illustrative. This is the business we'll use all act.

A plumbing business. $2 million revenue, $300,000 profit after paying a manager to run it. Bought on vendor finance, paying the seller $200,000 a year. That's $300,000 divided by 1.5. It clears the 1.5 rule, just.

It does three kinds of work.

Emergency callouts. $500,000 of revenue at a 45% gross margin. About $225,000 of gross profit.

Maintenance for local homes and shops. $700,000 at 40%. $280,000.

New-build work for one builder. $800,000 at 18%. About $144,000.

[SCREEN: table. Emergency $500K / 45% / $225K · Maintenance $700K / 40% / $280K · Builder $800K / 18% / $144K · Total GP ~$650K − overhead $350K = profit $300K]

Add the gross profit and you get about $650,000. Take off $350,000 of overhead and there's your $300,000.

Now look at what the table shows. The biggest line by revenue is the weakest. The builder is 40% of revenue and less than a quarter of the gross profit. In diligence, one customer over 30% of revenue is a red flag. Once you own it, it isn't a red flag any more. It's your problem to manage.

[B-ROLL: a tech's van at a building site; invoices on a clipboard]

**Where the margin goes (5:00)**

Now go down a level, job by job.

Pull twelve months of jobs out of the job system. For every job, write down what you invoiced, the hours on it times what an hour of labour really costs you with super, vehicle and phone, the materials, and any return visit.

Then sort from worst to best. In trades and service businesses the same handful of leaks often turn up.

Variations. Extra work done on the day that never made it onto the invoice.

Return visits. The tech goes back to fix the same job, free, and nobody records it.

Travel. A job an hour away charged the same as a job around the corner.

Old deals. A customer on a rate the previous owner agreed to years ago, as a favour.

Slow payers. Profit on paper, cash sixty days later. That one gets its own episode, number 50.

[SCREEN: the five leaks as a checklist]

**What fixing one leak is worth (6:15)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers."]

Back to the builder. Say you find their margin is low because variations go unbilled and the rates haven't moved in years. You don't ring them this month. But at the next contract review, every variation gets billed and the rates move. Their margin goes from 18% to 25%.

That's seven points on $800,000. About $56,000 more gross profit. Profit goes from $300,000 to roughly $356,000.

Your payment to the seller hasn't changed. It's still $200,000. So your cover goes from 1.5 times to about 1.78. That's the difference between a bad quarter being uncomfortable and a bad quarter being dangerous.

And at the exit. If a buyer pays, say, four times profit, that $56,000 adds about $224,000 of value. Illustrative. The multiple depends on the market and the buyer.

[SCREEN: "$56K × 4 = ~$224K of value · cover 1.5× → ~1.78×" · "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**Revenue by source (7:15)**

One more cut, and this one's Daniel's. At one stage, he says, organic content brought him 5 to 10% of his leads and up to 60% of his revenue. Paid ads brought most of the leads and closed poorly. His blended numbers hid it.

[SCREEN: "Organic: 5–10% of leads, up to 60% of revenue." — Daniel Iles · Focus on revenue · 0:30]

The same trap sits in a boring business. Tag every job with where the customer came from. Referral. Google. The builder. The phone number on the side of the van. You might find your best-margin work comes from the channel you spend least on.

**In a clinic (7:50)**

I run a healthcare group, and my first deal was a physio clinic. In a clinic, the job is the appointment. You cut it by practitioner and by service instead of by job. Same method.

[JOSH: one margin leak you found in the first months of owning the physio clinic or the healthcare group (a service line, a billing pattern, an old arrangement), and roughly what fixing it was worth in round numbers. If there isn't one you can share, cut this paragraph.]

**Do this this week (8:30)**

Do this this week.

One. Export twelve months of jobs or invoices.

Two. Split revenue into service lines and work out gross margin for each.

Three. List your top ten customers by revenue, with gross margin next to each.

Four. Ask your techs one question: "What do we do that we never charge for?" Write down every answer.

Five. Change nothing yet. Pick the three biggest leaks to fix in month two.

[SCREEN: the five actions as a checklist]

## Episode 47 · Cut the costs your customers never see

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Cut the Costs Your Customers Never See
  2. The Costs to Cut After You Buy a Business (and the Ones You Never Touch)
  3. Two Lists Every New Owner Should Write in Month Two
- **Thumbnail text:** CUT THE INVISIBLE
- **Runtime target:** 11:30

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: two columns. Left: "Customers feel it". Right: "Customers never see it".]

Cut the wrong cost and you lose a customer. Cut the right one and nobody notices.

Daniel Iles, who's an accountant by trade, puts the right cuts at about 10% added to monthly take-home. Worth having. And it has a floor.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map, "Grow" lit. Corner tag: "Act 10 · Grow: profit · 2 of 5"]

Grow, episode 2 of 5. The first three Cs got you the business. Last episode we found the margin leaks. Today, the overhead.

This one's for owners who'd rather fix the boring stuff than hire a consultant to tell them about it. It's not for the slash-and-burn crowd. If your plan is to cut staff in week two to make the numbers look good, you'll lose the business you just bought.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Write two lists. Costs your customers feel: the tech who turns up, how fast you answer, the quality of the work. And costs they never see: software, insurance, suppliers, vehicles, bank fees. You only cut from the second list.

Two. Watch labour cost per dollar of revenue, not the wage bill. Daniel's rule is that it should fall as you get better, because systems make every job easier. You get there by cutting wasted hours, not pay.

Three. Cut once, cut fast, then move on. Daniel's line: "There's only a limit to how much expense you can control… there's no limit to how much income you can make." Costs are a one-month project. Income is the rest of the act.

[SCREEN: "There's only a limit to how much expense you can control… there's no limit to how much income you can make." — Daniel Iles]

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why the floor matters (3:00)**

In a video called "5 Frameworks That Will Make You Too Much Money", Daniel ranks the ways to raise profit. Cutting costs isn't his favourite. Cut employees who produce nothing and software nobody uses, he says, and it can add about 10% to take-home each month. But costs only go to zero. Income has no ceiling.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 5 Frameworks · 2:33 · link in description"]

For a new owner that's the right attitude. Do the cuts. Do them properly. Don't make them your whole strategy.

**The overhead, line by line (3:45)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Same illustrative plumbing business as last episode. $2 million revenue, $300,000 profit, $200,000 a year to the seller. Overhead is $350,000.

[SCREEN: overhead table. Admin wages $90K · Vehicles $90K · Rent $60K · Insurance $40K · Software and subscriptions $30K · Accounting, bank and card fees $25K · Phones and IT $15K = $350K]

Admin wages, $90,000. Vehicles, $90,000. Rent, $60,000. Insurance, $40,000. Software and subscriptions, $30,000. Accounting, bank and card fees, $25,000. Phones and IT, $15,000.

Now walk the second list, one line at a time.

Software. Businesses that have run for twenty years often pay for three tools that do one job, and seats for people who left. Say you cut $8,000.

Insurance. Re-quote it through a broker at renewal. It often comes in a bit cheaper for the same cover. Say $5,000. Check the cover is the same, line by line.

Vehicles. Is there a ute nobody drives? Selling one idle vehicle saves the lease or loan, the rego and the insurance. Say $7,000.

Bank and card fees. Ask. Say $2,000.

And one cost that sits in delivery but customers still never see: materials. Ask suppliers for better terms if you put more volume through fewer of them. On $400,000 of materials, a couple of points is about $10,000.

Add it up. About $32,000. On $300,000 of profit, that's roughly 10%, right where Daniel says it lands.

[SCREEN: "~$32K saved ≈ 10% more profit · cover 1.5× → ~1.66×" · "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Your seller payment is still $200,000. Profit is now around $332,000. Cover goes from 1.5 times to about 1.66.

**The add-backs (5:45)**

One more line hides here. In diligence you'll have adjusted the profit for the seller's personal costs run through the business. The car. The phone. Sometimes a family member on the payroll. That adjustment assumed those costs stop.

Make sure they actually stop. If the seller's still on the fuel card six months after settlement, the profit you paid for isn't the profit you're getting.

**The list you never touch (6:15)**

Now the first list. Costs your customers feel.

Daniel has a strong view here. In "How to build a 100-person team without killing profit," he says don't save money on delivery with cheap offshore assistants. Pay properly for client-facing people. Retention pays back more than the savings. And he says bad fulfilment kills a growing business faster than slow sales.

[SCREEN: "Don't save money on delivery. Retention pays back more." — Daniel Iles · 100-person team · 10:49]

In a trades business that's your best techs, the person who answers the phone, and parts on the van. In a clinic it's your practitioners and your front desk. Touch those and customers feel it in a week.

**Labour cost per dollar (7:00)**

So how do you get the wage bill under control without cutting pay? You measure it differently.

Daniel's principle, from "Every revenue level explained": "As you scale, you should be paying your people less, not more." He means per dollar of revenue, not per person. Systems, SOPs and tools make each job easier, so the same team produces more.

[SCREEN: "As you scale, you should be paying your people less, not more." — Daniel Iles · Every revenue level · 12:55]

In the plumbing business, say tech wages are $800,000 on $2 million of revenue. Forty cents in every dollar. Now look at where hours go that nobody pays for. Driving across town between jobs. A second trip for a part. Going back to fix a job twice. Fix the scheduling, stock the vans, get the first-time fix rate up, and the same team bills more hours.

Every cent off labour per dollar is worth about $20,000 on $2 million of revenue. Nobody took a pay cut.

**Don't add overhead ahead of income (7:45)**

One warning. New owners love to add overhead. A general manager they don't need yet. A new system. A nicer office.

Daniel learnt this the hard way. At one point his business was doing around $700,000 a month with about $200,000 a month of payroll, built for growth that hadn't arrived. Razor-thin margins. And cutting it later cost him people he respected.

[SCREEN: "~$700K/month topline · ~$200K/month payroll" — Daniel Iles · Went broke · 12:56]

In a 3C deal, every new fixed cost comes out of the same cash that pays the seller. Add it when the income is there, not before.

**Do this this week (8:30)**

Do this this week.

One. Write the two lists. Customers feel it. Customers never see it.

Two. Print twelve months of bank and card statements and highlight every subscription. Cancel what nobody uses.

Three. Book an insurance re-quote for your next renewal.

Four. Check the add-backs. Is any personal cost of the seller still being paid?

Five. Work out labour cost per dollar of revenue for each of the last twelve months.

Here's what goes wrong at this step. People cut from the wrong list because it's the biggest number. The tech wages are the biggest line, so that's where they go. ## Episode 48 · Turn one-off customers into contracts

### Metadata

- **Title options:**

- **Thumbnail text:** REVENUE THAT REPEATS
- **Runtime target:** 12:00

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: a calendar. January blank. Every month after it blank.]

Most boring businesses start every year at zero. Every customer has to find them again.

Daniel Iles makes the point with a security system. People pay thousands for the alarm, then sign up for a small monthly monitoring fee to protect what they already bought.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map, "Grow" lit. Corner tag: "Act 10 · Grow: profit · 3 of 5"]

This is the 3C Machine, Grow, episode 3 of 5. We've found the leaks and cut the invisible costs. Now we start adding income, and the best kind is income that comes back on its own.

For owners willing to build something slowly that pays for years. Not for anyone looking for a trick that doubles revenue next month. This one compounds.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Sell the plan at the moment of the big job. Right after someone pays for a new hot water system, a small yearly plan to look after it is an easy yes. That's Daniel's security-system idea, moved into a trade.

Two. Set the term just past how long happy customers stay, and get paid upfront. Daniel's rule: if clients usually stay four to five months, sign six-month contracts. In a trade, the natural unit is often a year. Make paying upfront the obvious choice, and make monthly cost more.

Three. Recurring revenue is worth more twice. It's predictable cash for your seller payment every month. And when you sell, it's the kind of revenue a buyer checks first in diligence.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and then build them up like this. If that's what you're working towards, book a call below. Short application, then me. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**What Daniel says, and where he disagrees with himself (3:00)**

Daniel is careful here, so let's be careful too.

He's famous for moving away from monthly billing. His business went from about $1.3 million a month to $3 million in around 30 days when they stopped charging weekly and sold a fixed six-month programme paid upfront. His line: "Maximum value at the start, maximum payment at the start."

[SCREEN: "Maximum value at the start, maximum payment at the start." — Daniel Iles · I paid 20k to learn this · 1:01]

But in "5 Frameworks" he also lists retainers and recurring fees as one of the five ways to make more profit, for services only you provide.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 5 Frameworks · 3:04 · link in description"]

The two fit together. Charging monthly for something that's already delivered is a mistake. Nobody keeps paying for a book they've finished. Charging for an ongoing relationship, where you keep turning up and keep doing work, is fine. A maintenance plan is the second kind. You're there every service, every year.

**What to sell (4:00)**

In trades and services, it often looks like one of three things.

Maintenance plans for homes. A plumber checks the hot water system and clears the drains twice a year, with priority booking and no callout fee. Aircon servicing works the same way.

Service agreements for businesses. Property managers, strata, schools, shops. A fixed monthly fee for a set scope, plus repairs at agreed rates.

Packages and memberships in clinics. A block of sessions paid upfront, or a membership. In healthcare, check what the rules in your sector allow before you design one.

[SCREEN: three icons. Home plan · Business agreement · Clinic package. "Check your industry's rules."]

**The illustrative maths (4:45)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Same illustrative plumbing business. $2 million revenue, $300,000 profit, $200,000 a year to the seller.

Say it does work for about 2,500 different households a year. Almost all one-off.

You offer a home plan. Two visits a year, priority booking, no callout fee. $360 a year paid upfront, or $36 a month.

Look at those two prices. Monthly adds up to $432. That's 20% more for paying monthly, which is the same shape as Daniel's worked example: an exit-planning consultant at $6,000 a month moved to a six-month minimum, $36,000 paid monthly or $30,000 paid in full.

[SCREEN: "$360 upfront vs $36 × 12 = $432 · Daniel's example: $36K monthly vs $30K paid in full" — Raw $5M convo · 37:13]

Say 8% of those households take it. That's 200 plans. About $72,000 a year, mostly paid in advance. The visits are booked, so you can put them on quiet days. And when the hot water goes at 9pm, those 200 households ring you first, not Google.

At an illustrative 50% gross margin, that's about $36,000 of gross profit before any repair work it brings.

**What it does to the deal (5:45)**

Two things.

Your cover. Profit up around $36,000 means the $200,000 seller payment is covered more comfortably. And it arrives every year in advance, not when someone's pipes happen to burst.

Your exit. When a buyer looks at your business, one of the things they check is how much revenue is one-off and how much repeats. Daniel makes a related point in "Why $10K per month is a dangerous place to be." Multiples rise with size and stability. He gives around four times on $1 million of profit and six to eight times on $10 million, because there are more problems already solved.

[SCREEN: "~4× on $1M profit · 6–8× on $10M profit" — Daniel Iles · $10K/mo dangerous · 3:35 · "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Revenue that repeats is part of what makes profit stable. That's the roll-up logic from Act 7. Buy at small-business multiples, make the group bigger and steadier, sell it at a bigger one.

**Don't keep people past the value (6:45)**

One warning from Daniel. Don't jump to long terms just because you can. He says twelve-month contracts were harder to sell, and keeping people past the value created review, chargeback and complaint problems.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · Every revenue level explained · 30:34"]

So: a yearly plan that does real work, with a reminder before it renews and an easy way to cancel. If the plan isn't worth it, people will tell their neighbours. That's the opposite of what you're building.

**Ask it in diligence (7:20)**

If you haven't bought yet, this is a question for diligence.

Saul is an Owners Academy member. He's in early due diligence on an electrical and aircon business: 25 staff, around $3.8 million in revenue, owner retiring in two years. He found it by cold call, direct to the owner.

[SCREEN: "Saul, Owners Academy member. Deal in due diligence."]

A question I'd want answered on any business like that: how much of the revenue repeats every year, and how much has to be won again? The answer changes what the business is worth today, and what it could be worth once you've built plans into it.

Do this this week.
