There are only two types of people who put money into businesses.

The ones who get paid the day they buy. And the ones who get paid the day they sell.

And it has nothing to do with how much money you start with. It has everything to do with one idea: multiple arbitrage.

**The recipe, fast (1:00–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Small businesses sell cheap. Three to five times profit is normal. And the reason is that, from the buyer's side, they're risky. The owner is the business. There's one key customer, or one key tech, or one key relationship. And there aren't many buyers who can fund them.

Two. Groups sell expensive. Bigger profit, more stable profit, a management team, and a whole new class of buyer: private equity and larger companies who pay more for size. Daniel Iles makes the same point from the other side of the table. A business doing about a million in profit might sell around four times. One doing ten million might get six to eight times. His reason: more profit means more stability and more problems already solved.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Why $10k per month is a dangerous place to be", 3:35. Link in description.]

Three. A roll-up is buying the cheap thing and building the expensive thing. Same industry. Bought one at a time with the seller paid over time and the bank where it makes sense. Combined, professionalised, sold together. The multiple does a lot of the lifting. But only if what you've built is a real group: one set of numbers, one team, and it runs without you.

*// LESSON · WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING · NOT-YOUR-FAULT · TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[B-ROLL: a row of small shopfronts in one suburb; a trades van; a clinic reception.]

Most of what you've been told about where the money is in business points the wrong way.

You were told you make it by buying cheap, or by working harder inside the business, or by having a huge pile of capital.

None of that is where the big number comes from. People who build groups make most of it at the exit, from the gap between what small businesses sell for and what groups sell for.

And if nobody showed you that, it's not your fault. It has nothing to do with how smart you are or how much you've saved. It has everything to do with the fact that most people only ever see one business at a time.

So start with why small is cheap.

A buyer looking at a business making four hundred thousand a year asks one question: what happens to that profit when the owner walks out? Usually the honest answer is, nobody knows. The owner does the quoting. The owner has the relationships. The books are kept for the tax return, not for a buyer. So the buyer protects themselves with a low multiple. They pay you for a few years of profit and take the risk on the rest.

Now flip it. A group with two and a half million in profit, five sites, a general manager in each, one finance team, monthly numbers that tie to the bank. That buyer is asking a different question: how much more can we bolt on to this? It's a platform. That's worth more.

So here's what a buyer checks when they value a group. Size and stability of profit. No single customer carrying the place. A management layer that isn't you. Numbers they can trust, which is your Monday pack from episode 30. And room to keep going: more businesses in the same vertical they can add.

Now the worked example. Round numbers, illustrative. Not a promise, not a forecast.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical. Education, not financial advice.]

You buy five businesses in the same industry, in the same region. Each makes four hundred thousand a year in profit. You pay four times for each. That's one point six million each, eight million all up. The money comes from the sellers, paid over time, and lenders where it fits. Every single deal still has to pass the rule from episode 12: profit covers the repayments at least one and a half times.

[SCREEN: 5 × $400K profit = $2M profit. Paid: 5 × $1.6M = $8M.]

Combined, that's two million in profit. Now imagine you do nothing else. No growth at all. A buyer pays six times for the group. That's twelve million for something you paid eight for. Four million of value came from nothing but putting them together properly.

Now you do the work from Act 6. Prices up. A customer machine installed. Overheads shared. Say profit rises a quarter, to two and a half million. At six times, that's fifteen million. At eight, it's twenty.

[SCREEN: $2.5M profit × 6 = $15M · × 8 = $20M. Illustrative.]

And there's a second engine running the whole time. Every year, the businesses pay down the debt that bought them. If you started with eight million owing and you've paid off half by the time you sell, you owe four. So at a fifteen million sale, roughly eleven is equity. At twenty, roughly sixteen. That's before fees and tax, and before the hundred things that will go differently in real life.

You'll notice I didn't use ten times. Ten is possible. Fifteen happens. I built the example at six and eight on purpose. If your deal only works at ten, it doesn't work.

Three ways roll-ups go wrong.

First, overpaying. People fall in love with the exit multiple and start paying seven times for business number three because "we'll sell at twelve anyway". No. You buy at three to five, and every deal stands on its own cash flow. The exit is a bonus on a deal that already works.

Second, building a collection instead of a group. Five businesses, five sets of books, five owners' habits, five ways of doing everything. That's not a platform. A buyer sees five small businesses and pays small-business multiples. The arbitrage only happens if you integrate. That's episode 33.

Third, the wrong vertical. You want something fragmented: lots of small operators, nobody dominant. Boring. Lots of owners near retirement. Healthcare, trades, commercial services. If three big players already own the market, there's nothing to roll up.

The method itself is plain. First you pick one fragmented industry in one region. Then you build your board. Once you have the board, you source off-market. Then you structure with the seller paid over time, rollover and bank debt, diligence with your team, and close. And once the first one's closed, you go back to sourcing and do it again, with the exit written down before the first deal. From first deal to exit, plan on somewhere between three and ten years.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

One. Write your vertical in one sentence. Industry plus region. "Allied health clinics in south-east Queensland."

Two. Count it. List a hundred businesses in that vertical. If you can't find a hundred, it's not fragmented enough.

Three. Take one target, or the business you already own. Write profit times four, then profit times eight. Look at the gap.

Four. One page: who buys your group in year five to seven, and what will they check? That page is your exit thesis.

### Script

*// FROM-TO OPENER · ICP: One-Business Ceiling*

**Cold open (0:00–0:30)**

Owning one good business is a phenomenal thing. You get the cash flow. You get the control. You get to build something that's yours.

Until it stops growing past you.

Then you go from building to maintaining. You go from "what's next" to "is this it". And that's the point that decides whether you stay at one business, or go to two.

The second business is easier than the first. A good friend of mine, Cam, did two deals in 23 days.

This is Scale and exit, episode two of five. Last time: the roll-up, buying at around four and selling a group for more. A group needs a second business. So today is how to buy number two, and why it's easier.

In the next few minutes I'll show you how to use what deal one already gave you, the credibility, the lender, the cash flow, to buy number two faster, so you never have to start from zero again. All without draining business one to pay for it, and without a big deposit out of your own pocket.

This is for people who already own one business, or are close to their first and want to see where it goes. And if one business is plenty for you, that's fine. Skip this one.

**The recipe, fast (1:00–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Your credibility is real now. On deal one you borrowed it from a board. On deal two you're an owner with a track record. Sellers, lenders and lawyers treat you differently, and they should.

Two. You've got assets you didn't have last time. A lender who has watched your numbers for a year. A business throwing off cash after its repayments. A team, templates and a process you've run once already.

Three. The hard part moves. On deal one, the hard part was getting a yes. On deal two, it's your attention. Business one still needs running. So before you buy number two, business one has to pass three gates. I'll give you all three.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's the shape of it. I help people do this: the first deal with little to no money down, and then the next one, faster. If you want your number two planned around your number one, book a call below. You'll talk to me, not a setter. Now the detail.

*// LESSON · BEFORE I KNEW / AFTER I KNEW · NOT-YOUR-FAULT · TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Before deal one, you didn't know how any of this worked. You called owners who'd never heard of you. You borrowed a board's credibility. You walked into lenders as a stranger. Every step was slow because every step was new.

After deal one, you know. You know what a seller needs to hear. You know what a lender needs to see. You know which lawyer to call.

And if number one felt like a grind, that's not your fault, and it isn't a sign you can't do it again. It has nothing to do with your talent or your stamina. It has everything to do with it being the first time. The first time is always the hard path.

So let's take what's easier, one by one.

Credibility first. When I was 25 I had no track record, so I built a board and borrowed theirs. You did the same, or you will. But once you've closed and run a business for a year, you have your own. You can say, "We own a clinic in this region, here's how it's performed since we bought it." That one sentence does more on a seller call than any board bio.

Second, the lender. In episode 13 I said interview fifty banks before you need one. If one of them funded deal one, or even just watched it, they now have twelve months of your reporting. They've seen you hit your numbers. They've seen the cover ratio hold. You're not a hypothetical anymore. You're a customer.

Third, the cash flow from business one. This is the big one, and it's also where people get hurt, so slow down here.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Education, not financial advice.]

Round numbers, illustrative. Business one makes three hundred thousand a year in profit. Repayments to the seller and the bank are two hundred thousand. That's one and a half times cover.

You spend a year on Act 6. Prices up, customer machine in, owner's jobs handed over. Profit goes to three hundred and sixty thousand. Repayments are still two hundred. Cover's now one point eight. And there's a hundred and sixty thousand left over after repayments.

[SCREEN: Biz #1: $360K profit − $200K repayments = $160K left. Keep $60K reserve. $100K free.]

Keep sixty of that as a reserve. Never touch it. That leaves a hundred thousand you can point at deal two. It can cover your legal and accounting fees, so you're not waiting on a settlement draw. It can sit as a buffer the lender can see. Or, if a seller wants a small amount up front, it can be that.

Now business two. It makes two hundred and fifty thousand in profit. Same speed run as episode 11: divide by one and a half, and about a hundred and sixty-six thousand a year can go to the seller. Over ten years, that's one point six six million. A strong offer.

[SCREEN: Biz #2: $250K ÷ 1.5 ≈ $166K/yr → ~$1.66M over 10 years.]

Put them side by side. Six hundred and ten thousand in profit across both. About three hundred and sixty-six thousand in repayments. Group cover, about one point six seven. Still above the line.

And the reason "I don't have the capital to buy more" stops being true is right there. Business two pays for itself, out of its own profit, the same way business one did.

What you don't do is strip business one to buy business two. Don't drain its reserve. Don't let a lender tie the two together without your lawyer reading every word. Each acquisition sits in its own company, under your holding company, so if number two goes wrong, it can't take number one down with it. Ask your lawyer about cross-guarantees before you sign anything.

Fourth, your process. You've got the NBIO template, the diligence checklist, the lawyers and accountants who know how you work. Diligence goes faster when everyone's done it with you before.

Fifth, your pipeline. Remember the owners who said "not yet" during your first thousand calls? Some of them are ready now. Call them back.

That's how Cam moved. Two deals in 23 days, both 100% vendor finance, interest only for ten years with balloon payments in years three and ten, and five hundred thousand in working capital from the bank. That speed came from having the machine ready before the deals showed up.

[SCREEN: Cam (Josh's friend). Results not typical; records on file.]

Now the part that's harder. Your job changes.

Daniel Iles has a whole breakdown of every revenue level in his business, and the lesson that runs through it is that each level changes what the founder does. Early on, he sold every call himself, because without that he couldn't set the numbers for anyone else. Later, at five million a month, he says the CEO's job is small, high-leverage adjustments. Don't touch the steering wheel at a hundred miles an hour. He also says bloat is natural the first time you hit a new level. You over-expand, then you get lean again.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Every revenue level from $10k to $5M / month explained", 12:55 and 48:29. "How to build a 100-person team without killing profit", 3:06. Links in description.]

For you, that means: on business one you were the operator. On business two you're the owner of two operators. You stop doing the work and start checking it.

And Daniel would push back on this whole episode. He's said the extra income from buying another business is small next to the compounding effect of focus. He's right, for most people. That's why number two should be in the same industry, ideally the same region. Then it isn't a distraction. It's the same thing done twice, and that's still focus.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "How being 'boring' makes me $1.5M profit every month", 2:34.]

So, the three gates before business two. First, business one runs without you: the test from episode 29, a real stretch where you're not there and nothing breaks. Then, once that's true, the cover ratio has to hold at one and a half or better, every month, for at least six months of Monday numbers. And once you have both, number two has to be in the same vertical. So it can share a back office later.

Pass all three, and go.

Mistake one. Waiting for perfect.
