**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Follow the billings, not the brass plate. The clinic's name is on the door, but the revenue usually sits with individual practitioners. Ask for billings by practitioner for three years. If one person, often the owner, does a big slice of it, you're not buying a clinic. You're buying that person's hands, and they're about to leave.

Two. Price the clinic without the owner's hands. Work out what it would cost to replace the owner's clinical work with someone you pay a fair rate. Take that off the profit. That's the profit you're buying. Offer 3 to 5 times that, not the headline.

Three. Lock in the people before you lock in the price. Practitioners, the practice manager, the front desk. Talk to the key ones before settlement, with the seller's agreement. And check the rules. Healthcare is regulated, and in some places the rules affect who can own a clinic and how it bills.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why clinic owners sell (3:00)**

There's a generation of owners in traditional industries ready to retire, and healthcare is full of them. A lot of clinic owners are practitioners who started the place, treated patients all day for twenty years, and did the admin at night. The burden isn't the patients. It's the payroll, the compliance, the lease, the rosters.

They don't want to wait 16 weeks for a bank. They want out, and they want their patients and staff looked after. That's why getting paid over time lands well with them. Never call it vendor finance to their face. Say "getting paid over time."

[B-ROLL: an empty treatment room after hours; a practitioner doing paperwork at reception]

**What banks look for (3:50)**

Lenders often like healthcare. Demand tends to hold up when the economy doesn't. Typically. But what they'll want to see is fairly predictable.

Three years of financials that match the tax returns. Revenue that doesn't sit with one person. The practitioners staying, ideally on agreements. A lease that's assignable, with enough term left. And registrations and billing compliance in order.

That's also why the board matters here. A chair, a finance lead, and ideally someone who's actually run clinics. A bank looks at who's behind you before it looks at the spreadsheet.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**The three numbers (4:45)**

Number one. Share of billings by practitioner. Take each person's billings as a share of the total. If one or two names carry a big share, say more than 30%, treat it like customer concentration. Because that's what it is. Those patients may follow that person.

Number two. Utilisation. Booked appointments against available appointments, per practitioner. A clinic at 60% has room to grow without a single new hire. A clinic at 95% is capped until you recruit.

Number three. Profit after a market wage for the owner. A lot of owner-practitioners pay themselves through the profit, not a salary. So the profit looks bigger than it is. Pay the owner's clinical work at a fair rate in your model and see what's left.

**Worked example (5:30)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Round numbers. Illustrative. Not a real deal.

A clinic turns over $2 million and reports $500,000 profit. The owner treats patients four days a week and doesn't take a wage. Their billings are $600,000. That's 30% of the clinic.

Step one. Replace the owner's clinical work with a practitioner at a fair rate. Say that costs $200,000 a year. True profit: $300,000.

Step two. Price it. Four times $500,000 is $2 million. Four times $300,000 is $1.2 million. That $800,000 gap is the price of the owner's hands. Don't pay it.

Step three. Can it carry the debt? $300,000 divided by 1.5 is $200,000 a year for the seller. Over seven years, that's $1.4 million. More than your $1.2 million offer, so it fits with headroom.

[SCREEN: "$500K reported − $200K owner's work = $300K · ÷ 1.5 = $200K a year"]

Step four. Protect the 30%. The owner stays on for a handover period, introduces patients to the new practitioner, and part of what they're paid can depend on those billings holding.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Daniel Iles has a video called "You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers." One of the businesses he describes was capped because he was the bottleneck. The money depended on him being on screen. A clinic where one practitioner does a third of the work has the same cap. Your job is to remove it.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers' · 2:02 · link in description"]

**What usually goes wrong (6:45)**

The owner is the top biller and leaves early. The handover was a handshake, not a contract.

A practitioner walks across the road and takes their patients. Whether a restraint clause would hold depends on where you are and how it's written. Ask your lawyer. Don't assume.

The billing has problems. If claims to a government scheme or an insurer were done wrong, that can come back after you own it. That's what diligence is for. Have someone who knows healthcare billing look at it.

And the staff hear about the sale from the car park. Interview the key people before close, with the seller's blessing, so nobody resigns in week one.

**How to grow it after (7:40)**

Start with utilisation. Fill the empty appointments before you hire anyone. Answer every enquiry fast. Episode 51 covers speed to lead.

Then recruit. Every practitioner you add who builds a full book makes the clinic less dependent on any one person, and worth more.

Then add a service that fits the patients you already have.

Then do it again. A second clinic nearby can share a practice manager, the marketing, the bookkeeping. Buy at small-business multiples, combine, and a group can sell at a bigger buyer's multiple.

**What banks look for (3:55)**

Trade businesses often have something clinics don't: hard assets. Vehicles, tools, stock, sometimes a yard. That can give a lender some security. Typically. Every lender is different.

They'll also look at the debtor book, the maintenance contracts, insurance, licensing, and whether the senior techs are staying. And they'll look at you. A board with someone who's run a trade or construction business carries a lot of weight here.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**The three numbers (4:40)**

Number one. Recurring share. What percentage of revenue comes from maintenance contracts, service agreements and repeat commercial clients, against one-off installs and new builds. The higher it is, the steadier the profit.

Number two. Gross margin by job type. Labour plus materials against the price, for service calls, installs and contract work separately. Owners often know the business is profitable. They don't always know which work makes the money.

Number three. Debtor days. How long, on average, customers take to pay. This one is where the cash hides.

**Worked example (5:20)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Illustrative numbers. Not Saul's deal. Not a real deal.

A trade business turns over $3 million a year. That's about $8,200 of revenue a day.

Customers take 75 days to pay. So there's about $615,000 sitting in unpaid invoices at any time.

Get that down to 45 days with deposits, progress billing and someone who chases invoices every week. Now there's about $370,000 outstanding.

That's roughly a quarter of a million dollars of cash freed up. Same customers, same work. And you can do it in your first few months.

[SCREEN: "$3M ÷ 365 ≈ $8,200 a day · 75 days ≈ $615K · 45 days ≈ $370K · ≈ $245K freed"]

Now flip it. If you'd bought that business and the debtor days went the other way, to 105, you'd need another quarter of a million just to stand still. That's why the working capital line at settlement matters. It's often around 10% of annual revenue. Here, that's about $300,000.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Recurring fees are the asset. Annual compliance work, tax returns, bookkeeping, payroll. Work that comes back every year without a sales call. That's what you're buying. One-off projects are nice. Don't pay for them like they repeat.

Two. Retention is the risk. The partner selling may have looked after some clients for twenty years. Some will stay with the firm. Some will leave when that partner does. So you structure the deal so the seller is paid for the clients who stay, not the ones on the list.

Three. Pay over time, tied to what's retained. That's what makes this industry a natural fit for vendor finance. The seller gets paid out of the fees. If clients leave, the payments adjust. Both sides want the same thing: a smooth handover.

And this is a regulated profession. Who can sign returns, hold a practising certificate or call themselves an accountant depends on where you are. Check your local licensing rules.

[SCREEN: "Check your local licensing rules."]

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why partners sell (3:00)**

A lot of accounting firms are owned by partners in their sixties. They've done forty tax seasons. There's often no obvious successor inside the firm. Young accountants may not want to buy in. Technology is changing the work, and some partners don't want to learn a new system at 64.

So the burden of ownership is real here too. They want out. They want their clients looked after. And a lot of them want their staff kept on.

[B-ROLL: a partner's office with archive boxes; a small team at desks during tax season]
