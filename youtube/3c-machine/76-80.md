### Script

A clinic is a phenomenal business. People need it in good years and bad. The work matters. The patients come back.

Until the owner is the clinic. Then it goes from a business to a job with a lease. From one good practitioner to one bottleneck. From something you could sell to something that leaves when you do.

My first deal was a physio clinic. Seven years of vendor finance at 4%. No deposit. And in any clinic deal now, the report I'd want before the profit and loss is billings by practitioner.

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Closing" lights up. Corner tag: "Act 16 · Industry playbooks I · 1 of 5"]

Healthcare first, because it's my world. I run a healthcare group full time.

In the next few minutes I'll show you how to tell whether a clinic is a business or one person's hands, so you never pay a business price for a job. All without a healthcare degree, and without a deposit.

This is for people who can give it 40-plus hours a week and want a group. Not for anyone after a lifestyle clinic they visit on Fridays.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Follow the billings, not the brass plate. The clinic's name is on the door, but the revenue usually sits with individual practitioners. Ask for billings by practitioner for three years. If one person, often the owner, does a big slice of it, you're not buying a clinic. You're buying that person's hands, and they're about to leave.

Two. Price the clinic without the owner's hands. Work out what it would cost to replace the owner's clinical work with someone you pay a fair rate. Take that off the profit. That's the profit you're buying. Offer 3 to 5 times that, not the headline.

Three. Lock in the people before you lock in the price. Practitioners, the practice manager, the front desk. Talk to the key ones before settlement, with the seller's agreement. And check the rules. Healthcare is regulated, and in some places the rules affect who can own a clinic and how it bills.

[SCREEN: "Check your local licensing rules."]

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy businesses like this with little to no money down. If you're looking at a clinic, or any business, book a call with me below. Not a setter. Short application first.

Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why clinic owners sell (3:00)**

There's a generation of owners in traditional industries ready to retire, and healthcare is full of them. A lot of clinic owners are practitioners who started the place, treated patients all day for twenty years, and did the admin at night. The burden isn't the patients. It's the payroll, the compliance, the lease, the rosters.

They don't want to wait 16 weeks for a bank. They want out, and they want their patients and staff looked after. And that's why getting paid over time lands well with them. Never call it vendor finance to their face. Say "getting paid over time."

[B-ROLL: an empty treatment room after hours; a practitioner doing paperwork at reception]

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME*

And if a clinic owner has hit a ceiling, it has nothing to do with how good a clinician they are, or how hard they work, or how many hours they put in. It has everything to do with how the business was built. Around one person. That's not a character flaw. It's a design problem, and design problems can be fixed by the next owner.

**What banks look for (3:50)**

Lenders often like healthcare. Demand tends to hold up when the economy doesn't. Typically. And what they want to see is fairly predictable.

Three years of financials that match the tax returns. Revenue that doesn't sit with one person. The practitioners staying, ideally on agreements. A lease that's assignable, with enough term left. Registrations and billing compliance in order.

And that's why the board matters here. A chair, a finance lead, ideally someone who's run clinics. A bank looks at who's behind you before it looks at the spreadsheet.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**The three numbers (4:45)**

Number one. Share of billings by practitioner. If one or two names carry a big share, say more than 30%, treat it like customer concentration. Because that's what it is. Those patients may follow that person.

Number two. Utilisation. Booked appointments against available appointments, per practitioner. A clinic at 60% has room to grow without a new hire. A clinic at 95% is capped until you recruit.

Number three. Profit after a market wage for the owner. A lot of owner-practitioners pay themselves through the profit, not a salary. So the profit looks bigger than it is.

**Worked example (5:30)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Round numbers. Illustrative. Not a real deal.

A clinic turns over $2 million and reports $500,000 profit. The owner treats patients four days a week and doesn't take a wage. Their billings are $600,000. That's 30% of the clinic.

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

First, replace the owner's clinical work with a practitioner at a fair rate. Say that costs $200,000 a year. True profit: $300,000.

Then, once you have the true profit, price it. Four times $500,000 is $2 million. Four times $300,000 is $1.2 million. That $800,000 gap is the price of the owner's hands. Don't pay it.

Then check the debt. $300,000 divided by 1.5 is $200,000 a year for the seller. Over seven years, $1.4 million. More than your $1.2 million offer, so it fits with headroom.

[SCREEN: "$500K reported − $200K owner's work = $300K · ÷ 1.5 = $200K a year"]

And once that's in place, protect the 30%. The owner stays on for a handover, introduces patients to the new practitioner, and part of what they're paid can depend on those billings holding.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

*// BELIEF SHIFT · THAT'S NOT HOW IT WORKS*

Most buyers think a clinic's value is its profit. Its name. Its location. That's not how it works. A clinic's value is the profit that stays after the owner goes. Daniel Iles has a video called "You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers." One of the businesses he describes was capped because he was the bottleneck. The money depended on him being on screen. A clinic where one practitioner does a third of the work has the same cap. Your job is to remove it.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers' · 2:02 · link in description"]

**What usually goes wrong (6:45)**

The owner is the top biller and leaves early. The handover was a handshake, not a contract.

A practitioner walks across the road and takes their patients. Whether a restraint clause would hold depends on where you are and how it's written. Ask your lawyer.

The billing has problems. If claims to a government scheme or an insurer were done wrong, that can come back after you own it. Have someone who knows healthcare billing look at it.

And the staff hear about the sale from the car park. Interview the key people before close, with the seller's blessing.

[JOSH: one thing you checked in the physio clinic's numbers or staff before settlement that you'd tell every clinic buyer to check]

**How to grow it after (7:40)**

First, fill the empty appointments before you hire anyone. Answer every enquiry fast. Episode 51 covers speed to lead.

Then recruit. Every practitioner who builds a full book makes the clinic less dependent on any one person, and worth more.

Once you have that, add a service that fits the patients you already have. Then do it again. A second clinic nearby can share a practice manager, the marketing, the bookkeeping. Buy at small-business multiples, combine, and a group can sell at a bigger buyer's multiple.

[JOSH: one change you made after taking over the physio clinic that lifted revenue or profit, in a sentence]

**Do this this week (8:30)**

Do this this week.

One. Pick three clinics in your area. Count the practitioners on each website.

Two. Write down the five reports you'll ask for after an NDA: three years of financials, matching tax returns, revenue by year, billings by practitioner, and appointment utilisation.

Three. Look up the ownership and licensing rules for clinics where you live. Check your local licensing rules.

Four. Ring one clinic owner. "Have you ever thought about selling?"

[SCREEN: the four actions as a checklist]

There are two paths from here.

Or you learn it after settlement, when the owner retires and a third of the patients go too. The numbers were real. They just weren't the clinic's. Most people on this path take years and lose deals.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Find out who holds the licence. In a lot of places, an electrical or refrigeration business has to have a licensed person nominated on the business licence. If that's the retiring owner, your deal needs a plan for the day they leave. Check your local licensing rules. Before you offer, not after.

[SCREEN: "Check your local licensing rules."]

Two. Split the revenue into recurring and one-off. Maintenance contracts and service agreements are worth more than a busy year of installs. Banks like them. Buyers later pay more for them.

Three. Follow the cash, not the jobs. Trade businesses can look profitable and still be short of cash, because customers pay late and materials get paid up front.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why trade owners sell (3:00)**

A lot of trade business owners started on the tools. Thirty years later they're running a team of twenty, quoting at night, and their knees have had enough. The kids often don't want it. Finding licensed staff is hard. The burden of ownership is real.

Saul's owner is retiring in two years. That's useful. There's time for a proper handover, and the owner has a reason to care who takes over.

*// BELIEF SHIFT · WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING*

You were told the good businesses are the new ones. Tech. Apps. Something you'd brag about at a barbecue. You were told you need to know the trade to own a trade business. Neither was ever true. Daniel Iles has a line for it. "New is fun, but fun will keep you broke… boring is where real wealth gets built." He says it about running his own business. It's just as true about buying one. Nobody's making a documentary about an aircon company. And that's exactly why the price can be sensible.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'I just made 3 million dollars in one month' · 20:10 · link in description"]

[B-ROLL: a service van fleet in a yard at dawn; a technician on a rooftop condenser]

**What banks look for (3:55)**

Trade businesses often have something clinics don't: hard assets. Vehicles, tools, stock, sometimes a yard. That can give a lender some security. Typically. Every lender is different.

They'll also look at the debtor book, the maintenance contracts, insurance, licensing, and whether the senior techs are staying. And they'll look at you. A board with someone who's run a trade or construction business carries a lot of weight.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**The three numbers (4:40)**

Number one. Recurring share. What percentage of revenue comes from maintenance contracts, service agreements and repeat commercial clients, against one-off installs. The higher it is, the steadier the profit.

Number two. Gross margin by job type. Labour plus materials against the price, for service calls, installs and contract work separately. Owners often know the business is profitable. They don't always know which work makes the money.

Number three. Debtor days. How long, on average, customers take to pay. This is where the cash hides.

**Worked example (5:20)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Illustrative numbers. Not Saul's deal. Not a real deal.

A trade business turns over $3 million a year. That's about $8,200 of revenue a day.

Customers take 75 days to pay. So there's about $615,000 sitting in unpaid invoices at any time.

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

First, you bring in deposits and progress billing. Then you put someone on chasing invoices every week. Once you have both, debtor days can come down to 45. Now there's about $370,000 outstanding.

That's roughly a quarter of a million dollars of cash freed up. Same customers, same work.

[SCREEN: "$3M ÷ 365 ≈ $8,200 a day · 75 days ≈ $615K · 45 days ≈ $370K · ≈ $245K freed"]

Now flip it. If the debtor days went the other way, to 105, you'd need another quarter of a million just to stand still. And that's why the working capital line at settlement matters. It's often around 10% of annual revenue. Here, about $300,000.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

And if a business like this ever looked out of reach, it had nothing to do with your trade skills, your savings or your contacts. It had everything to do with nobody showing you where the money in a trade business actually sits.

The licence walks out with the owner. Nobody checked who the nominee was until the week before settlement.

And the warranty work. Jobs from last year come back. Find out who pays for that in the contract.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Count the concentration. List customers by revenue. If the top one or two are a big share, the business is only as safe as those relationships. You price that in, and you structure around it.

Two. Read the contracts, not the customer list. Signed contracts or month-to-month? How long is left? Can the contract be assigned to a new owner? Some let the customer walk on a change of control. Your lawyer reads every major one.

Three. Find out who owns each relationship. If the owner personally looks after the top clients, the clients may be loyal to the owner, not the firm. That's key-person risk. The fix is a handover plan and a manager who takes over those relationships before the owner leaves.

**Why B2B service owners sell (3:00)**

A lot of these firms were built by one person who sold every contract themselves. Twenty years later they're still the one who takes the client's call at 9pm. The firm can't grow past them, and they're tired.

**What banks look for (3:40)**

Service firms are often asset-light. A few vehicles, some equipment. So lenders tend to look hard at the contracts and the cash flow. Typically.

They'll want three years of financials matching the tax returns, revenue by customer, the major contracts and their terms, and comfort that the big customers will stay. Concentration makes lenders nervous. And that's another reason the seller ends up carrying more of the price here.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**The three numbers (4:20)**

Number one. Top-two customer share. Above about 30%, it's a risk you deal with in the structure.

Number two. Contracted revenue and term left. What share is under signed contracts, and on average how many months remain?

Number three. Owner-managed revenue. What share comes from customers whose main contact is the owner? That's how much walks out when the owner does.

And if the service is licensed, say security or electrical work, check your local licensing rules too.

[SCREEN: "Check your local licensing rules."]

**Worked example (5:05)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Round numbers. Illustrative. Not a real deal.

A firm turns over $4 million. Profit is $600,000. The top customer is $1.2 million of that revenue. 30%.

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

First, debt capacity. $600,000 divided by 1.5 is $400,000 a year.

Then stress it. That top customer leaves. Say each dollar of their revenue carried 25 cents of profit. You lose $1.2 million of revenue and $300,000 of profit. Now there's $300,000 left and $400,000 of repayments. You can't pay.

[SCREEN: "$600K − $300K = $300K profit vs $400K repayments"]

Once you've seen that, structure around it. Size the fixed debt off the profit without that customer. $300,000 divided by 1.5 is $200,000 a year. Then make part of the seller's payments depend on that customer renewing. If they stay, the seller gets paid more. If they go, you're not broke. Episode 74 covers payments tied to performance.

**What usually goes wrong (7:05)**

The top customer has a change-of-control clause and uses the sale to retender.

The owner's handover is a single lunch with each client.

The contracts are mostly month-to-month and nobody checked.

**How to grow it after (7:40)**

First, put a manager on every big relationship and introduce them while the owner is still there.

Then renew contracts on longer terms. Customers who have stayed for years may sign for longer if it comes with something they value.

Once that's done, spread the revenue. Every new mid-sized customer makes the top one a smaller share. And track where new work comes from. If outbound or ads can win contracts, you've got a volume knob, not a referral habit.
