This is the 3C Machine. We're at Grow, the scale end of it, with Exit in sight. Last episode was financing deal three and beyond. The lenders now trust the group. This act is about the person running it. You.

It's for people who give this 40-plus hours a week and want the big outcome: a group, a roll-up, an exit. It's not for anyone who bought a business to work four hours a week. Five businesses won't give you that either.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Five unrelated businesses are five distractions. Five businesses in one industry, under one holding company, with one playbook, are one business with five sites. That's the only version of "five" I'd build. It's the version that passes Daniel's test.

Two. Your hours stop moving the numbers. Once each business has a general manager, the week isn't about how many hours you put in. It's about a small number of decisions, made on time, from good numbers.

Three. The week runs on a rhythm, not on you showing up. Numbers on Monday. One meeting day. The rest goes to what only the owner of a group does: capital, the next deal, and the people who run the businesses.

Get those three right and owning five is a calmer job than owning one. Get them wrong and you've bought five jobs with debt attached.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why Daniel's warning is right (3:00)**

Daniel built Viral Coach to around $5 million a month on one business, one product, one customer type, one price. He sold half of a side business to a partner so it wouldn't pull his focus. His point is about attention. You can buy time and money. Attention in the moment is finite. Give 20% to something else and you're at 80% on the thing that matters.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Why do you turn the music down…' · 3:37"]

So be honest about which five you're buying. A clinic, a car wash, a café, a software idea and a property flip is five games with five rulebooks. You'll be bad at four of them.

Five physio clinics, or five electrical contractors, under one holdco is different. Same staff types. Same suppliers. Same numbers. What you learn in one, you use in all five. The finance, HR and marketing run once for the group, which was episode 62. That's focus at a bigger size, not distraction.

**Your hours stop counting (3:50)**

Here's the part that surprises people after the second or third deal. Working harder stops working.

Daniel says it plainly. With about 100 staff, his own hours and IQ don't move outcomes. His job is to make the right decisions while specialist teams do their parts better than he could. "Success has nothing to do with how hard you work."

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'I just made 5 million dollars in one month' · 4:05"]

He makes the same point about leverage in a video called "Hustle culture is broken". 70 to 80% of sales calls don't buy, and that hour is gone. A video speaks to thousands and keeps working.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Hustle culture is broken' · 2:04"]

Here's the owner's version. An hour spent fixing one problem in one clinic is one-to-one. An hour spent fixing the system all five clinics use is one-to-many. A new booking script, a pricing change, a better hiring scorecard. Same hour. Five times the effect.

So the question for every hour of your week is: does this fix one site, or the system?

**Don't touch the steering wheel (4:45)**

The biggest mistake new group owners make is driving each business themselves. You walk into site three, see something you'd do differently, and change it on the spot. The GM learns you'll override them. Next time they wait for you. Now you're the bottleneck in five places.

Daniel's rule at scale: the CEO adds value through small, high-leverage adjustments, and doesn't step into sales or delivery. "Don't touch the steering wheel at 100 miles an hour."

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Every revenue level explained' · 48:29"]

He also runs changes past the person who owns that function first. He promoted one of his own account managers to run client experience, and he keeps weekly visibility through that one person.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'How I built my $3M/month team' · 5:37"]

That's your model. One GM per business. You see each business through its GM and its numbers. If you want something changed, you change it through them.

**The rhythm (5:30)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative week. Not Josh's actual calendar."]

Here's an illustrative week for one owner running five businesses in one industry, each with a GM. Round numbers. A guide, not a rule.

Monday morning. Five one-page KPI packs land by 9am, the Monday numbers from episode 30. You read them for red flags only. Two hours.

Tuesday. Your meeting day. Each GM gets 45 minutes: last week's numbers, the one problem, the one decision they need from you. Call it four hours, plus your finance lead.

Wednesday and Thursday. The owner's real job. Lenders. The pipeline for business six. Calls to owners who might sell. Due diligence on whatever's live. This is where a group gets bigger, and nobody else in the business will do it for you.

Friday. People and one site. Rotate the visit so each business sees you about once a month. Hiring for the roles that report to you. Then an hour with no meetings to think about the one constraint holding the group back.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative 50-hour week: ~10 reporting and GMs · ~15 deals and capital · ~10 people · ~5 site visits · ~10 the one constraint"]

Call it 50 hours. Roughly ten on reporting and GMs, fifteen on deals and capital, ten on people, five on site visits, and ten on the single most important problem. Illustrative.

Daniel does something close to this. He batches his essential meetings into one day a week and spends the rest on the work that only he can do.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Why I disappeared…' · 13:54"]

**My week (6:50)**

[JOSH: what a normal week running your healthcare group looks like: which days go to which jobs, roughly how many hours go to reporting, deals, people and sites, and the one meeting or report you'd never drop.]

I run a healthcare group full time, so this isn't theory for me. What I've learned is that the calendar only holds if the numbers hold. If a GM's pack is late or wrong, the whole week turns into chasing.

[JOSH: one thing you changed in your own week after the group grew, and why.]

**Boring is the goal (7:30)**

One more from Daniel. His routine is deliberately dull. An eight-minute drive to the office, eight or nine hours of focused work with headphones on, review the numbers, home to his son. He says the exciting part of life is about 5%.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'How being boring makes me $1.5M profit every month' · 9:14"]

That's the target. If your week as the owner of five is exciting, something's broken. Exciting means a GM left, a lender's nervous or a site is losing money. A boring week means the machine is working, and you're free to go and buy the next one.

**Do this this week (8:30)**

[SCREEN: checklist builds]

Do this this week. Even if you own one business, or none yet.

One. Write down last week's hours in four buckets: running one site, building the system, deals and capital, people. Be honest.

Two. Draft the one-page Monday pack you'd want from every business. Nine numbers or fewer.

Three. Pick one meeting day and move every recurring meeting into it.

Four. Write the list of decisions only you make. Everything else belongs to a GM, now or later.

Five. For each business you own or want to own, write the name of the person who could run it. If there's no name, that's your first hire.

**The turn (9:00)**

You can do all of this alone. Plenty of people work out a rhythm eventually.

Here's what usually happens. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. Business two arrives and they run both by hand. They drive between sites, override their managers, and the pipeline for business three dies because there's no Wednesday. Lenders notice an owner who's stretched thin. Nobody told them the week has to change before the second settlement, not after.

Or you can do it with me, and set the rhythm up while it's still easy.

**For skeptics (10:30)**

"I don't own one business yet." Then build the rhythm into business one. A Monday pack and a meeting day cost nothing and make business two easier to finance.

"Running five sounds like 100 hours a week." Building is hard work, and I won't pretend it isn't. But at five, the hours go to deals and decisions, not to doing the work in each site. If you're doing the work in each site, you don't have a group yet. You have five jobs.

"My industry is different." Maybe. Healthcare has practitioners and billing. Trades have crews and quotes. The rhythm is the same: numbers, GMs, one meeting day, the owner on capital and deals.

"Isn't one great business better?" Daniel would say yes, and for an internet business he's probably right. In a roll-up, the group is the one great business. That's how the exit works.

Three points.

Two. The KPI pack goes out before the meeting, not during it. Same pack, same order, every month. The board reads it beforehand and arrives with questions.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Who's in the room (3:00)**

Quick reminder of who sits on the board in the 3C model. A chair, someone senior with credibility. A finance lead who understands deal structures and lenders. A legal lead. An operator from your industry. Paid in founders' equity, typically 2 to 10% each, vesting from the signed purchase agreement.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

The usual commitment I'd ask for is a few hours a month for a board meeting, plus being available on deals. That's not a lot of their time. So you can't spend it reading them slides.

I found my board through LinkedIn and cold outreach. The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder in my company. When people like that give you two hours, the meeting has to be worth it.

**The KPI pack (3:50)**

The pack is one page per business and one page for the group. Same numbers every month, so trends jump out.

Daniel's whole management system runs on tracked numbers. He calls close rate, total revenue and revenue per call "kindergarten" metrics, and tracks 10 to 20 data points per sales rep.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'I just made 3 million dollars in one month' · 5:40"]

You don't need 20 for a board. You need the right handful, and the same handful every time. For a group of service businesses, something like this:

[SCREEN: KPI pack builds, labelled "Illustrative KPI pack"]

Per business: revenue against budget, gross margin, EBITDA, cash in the bank, debtor days, the one sales number that drives that business, staff turnover, and the GM's one problem.

For the group: total EBITDA, debt service cover against the 1.5 rule, cash against your reserve floor, the deal pipeline, and lender covenants.

Two rules. The pack goes out at least two days before the meeting. And every number has an owner, a named person who explains it.

Daniel's line on the rules is useful here. "If you make the rules available to everyone, they can influence them. They can play this game with you."

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'My $5 million per month sales system' · 16:00"]

Publish the pack to your GMs too. If they can see what the board sees, they'll fix things before the board asks.

**The agenda (4:50)**

[SCREEN: agenda card]

Here's an agenda that fits in two hours.

Ten minutes: last meeting's decisions. Done, not done, why.

Twenty minutes: the numbers. No walk-through. The board has read the pack. Questions only, and only on what's off budget or trending the wrong way.

Forty minutes: the one or two decisions. A price change across the group. A GM who isn't working. Whether to sign an LOI on business six. Each decision comes with a one-page paper: the options, the numbers, your recommendation.

Thirty minutes: capital and deals. Pipeline, lenders, covenant headroom. This is where your finance lead earns their equity.

Ten minutes: risks. What could hurt the group in the next 90 days.

Ten minutes: write the decision list. Decision, owner, date. Read it out before anyone leaves.

**Forecast before you decide (5:50)**

The best boards ask one question more than any other: "What do you expect to happen, and how do you know?"

Daniel calls quantitative forecasting one of the strongest signs of a good operator. His method: take marketing spend per rep, divide by cost per lead to get leads, multiply leads by that rep's revenue per lead. That's the forecast. Then check it against other models.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Every number I tracked to hit $3m/mo' · 11:48"]

And the failure that taught him to plan. He set a $5 million goal two weeks before a month started. Ad spend went from $800,000 to $1.1 million, sales stayed flat, and about $300,000 was gone.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'I failed…' · 2:35"]

For your board, that means no decision on a goal without the sub-goals underneath it. "Grow the group 20%" isn't a decision.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers."]

An illustrative version that is. Five clinics, each booking 400 appointments a month at $100 average. That's $200,000 a month for the group. You want $220,000. The paper says: price up 5% at all five, that's $10,000. Fill two empty practitioner days at the two busiest clinics, that's the other $10,000. Who owns each, by what date, and what number shows it's working. Now the board can decide.

**Decisions, owners, dates (6:50)**

Daniel has a rule for disagreements between leaders: whoever has more information decides, and the other defers completely.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'The reality of scaling a $5M/mo business with my wife' · 3:36"]

That works well on a board. The operator decides on operations. The finance lead decides how the debt is structured. You break ties as the owner, and you write down who decided.

He also learned to act fast on a known constraint. He named the fix for his refund problem in February, didn't act on it, and estimates the delay cost him about $6 million.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'I just made 5 million dollars in one month' · 0:30"]

So every decision gets a date. If it slips past two meetings, the board talks about why.

**Set the KPIs before the hire (7:40)**

One more. The board sets the KPIs for every senior role before that person is hired. Daniel's rule is never to let a senior hire define their own KPIs. He sets them per role, and measures cost centres too.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · '5 business lessons I wish I knew earlier' · 1:32"]

When you hire a GM for business six, the board agrees the scorecard first. Then the search. Then the hire. That's episode 56, applied at group level.

Do this this week.

One. Write the one-page KPI pack for your business, or the one you plan to buy. Ten numbers or fewer, each with an owner.

Two. Copy the agenda: decisions review, numbers, decisions, capital, risks, decision list.

Three. Turn the last thing you "discussed" with a partner or adviser into a decision: owner and date.

Four. Write down how you'd forecast next month. One method, then a second one to check it.

Five. If you don't have a board yet, write the names of four people you'd want around that table. That's Credibility, episode 6.

You can do all of this alone. You can build a pack, write an agenda and run a meeting.

Here's what usually happens. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. Their board meets once and drifts. Or it never forms, because they didn't know how to ask. The meetings turn into slides and nobody leaves with a decision. Covenant headroom gets thin and nobody notices until the bank does.

**The four clauses (4:50)**

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice. Get your own lawyer."]

Here are the four things I'd want written into any shareholders' agreement in a group. Your lawyer drafts them. You decide what goes in.

One. Roles and contribution. Who does what, how many hours, and what happens if someone stops. Tie equity to contribution with vesting. For board members, that's founders' equity vesting from the signed purchase agreement.

Two. A deadlock rule. When two owners disagree and neither will move, what happens? Daniel's tie-break between leaders is useful as a first step: whoever has more information decides, and the other defers completely.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'The reality of scaling a $5M/mo business with my wife' · 3:36"]

Agree in advance who has more information on what. The deal person on structures. The operator on operations. Then add a formal last resort in the agreement, which your lawyer can set out.

Three. A buy-sell clause. If one partner wants out, dies, gets divorced or stops contributing, how is their share valued, who can buy it, and how is it paid for? Agree the formula now, while nobody knows which side of it they'll be on.

Four. Minority holders on exit. Drag-along, so a minority can't block a sale of the group. No veto or voting control for small holders. Set up from the start. When a private equity buyer wants 100%, one person with 2% can't hold the deal hostage.

**Make the terms move with the conditions (6:10)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Here's the piece most agreements miss. Daniel's whole point is that conditions change. So build in the moment you'll revisit the terms.

Illustrative. Two co-buyers, 50/50, buy a business making $400,000. One runs it full time. The other finds the next deal. In year three the seller note's paid down, and the deal-finder takes a job elsewhere. Under a frozen 50/50, the operator is now doing all the work for half the fruit.

A better agreement has a trigger: when the seller note is paid off, or when either partner's hours drop below an agreed level, the terms reopen. Maybe the operator gets a salary first, then profit splits. Maybe the deal-finder sells some shares under the buy-sell formula. The point is the conversation is already in the contract, not left to resentment.

**Sellers are partners too (7:10)**

In a blended structure, the seller often rolls part of their equity into the new company. My friend Jason Nagy's first deal was a $5.5 million radiology business with nothing down, on a 60/40 structure. Seven years later he exited to private equity for $200 million.

Any time a seller stays in as a shareholder, you have a partner who used to be the boss. Same rules. Their role after settlement. What they can and can't vote on. And drag-along, so they come with you when the group sells.

And it's why I care about control. I keep 73% of my company. Equity can be shared. Control should sit where the decisions get made.
