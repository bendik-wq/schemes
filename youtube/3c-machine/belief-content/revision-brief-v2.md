# Revision brief v2: analogies and whole-brain belief shifting

The 12 scripts in `scripts/` are good. This pass makes the belief shift land harder. You're **revising, not rewriting**: keep each script's title, central distinction, facts, proof, structure, the closing invitation sentence and every hard rule from `script-brief.md`. Keep Josh's voice and the Alen-style flow: one subject decompiled, each line pulling into the next.

## 1. Analogies that create an "aha"

Add **4–5 analogies per script**. An "aha" happens when the viewer suddenly sees their own situation through something they already understand completely. So:

- **Draw from an owner's world:** trucks and trailers, property, farms and orchards, tools, hiring, the footy club, the local pub, renting vs owning a house, buying a ute, a franchise, a shop on the main street, fishing, building a house, sport.
- **One analogy per idea, set exactly where the belief turns.** Place it right after the old view is named, or right before the flip. Never stack two analogies in a row.
- **Make it concrete and visual, two to four sentences, then land it.** Link it back in one plain line: "That's exactly what a seller note is." Then move on.
- **Each script gets one "hero" analogy** that carries the central distinction and returns once near the end as a callback.
- **Keep them precise.** An analogy that bends the facts is worse than none. For example, DSCR is like the towing capacity on a ute: the ute doesn't care what the trailer cost, only what it weighs, and you never tow at the limit.
- **Ban clichés:** no "rocket fuel", no "game changer", no "fish where the fish are".

## 2. Belief shifting that reaches the whole brain

A belief only shifts when all three parts of the brain stop resisting. Work through each layer deliberately, in roughly this order inside the script, and weave them in rather than labelling them:

**Safety: the old, survival brain.** It asks, "Will this hurt me or my family? Can I lose the house?" Before any upside, make the downside small, bounded and visible:

- name the worst case in plain numbers;
- show the walls around it: the 1.5 times buffer, no personal guarantee where it applies, the deal never leaning on the first business, the ability to walk before signing;
- use calm, slow sentences here;
- make retiring sellers feel safe too ("change back to the same": their staff, their name, their income looked after).

**Feeling: the emotional brain.** It asks, "What does this mean for me, my status, the people I care about? What will I regret?" Make it felt:

- **Sensory scenes.** Use one future-pace per script: a specific moment, such as Monday morning at the second site, the call from the accountant, driving past the competitor's sign that's now yours, or telling your partner.
- **Status and belonging.** Use "the owners who buy" as a group the viewer wants to belong to.
- **Pride in what they've already built,** and legacy, and the kids.
- **Loss aversion.** Show the cost of standing still, made vivid but never exaggerated.
- **Relief and certainty** as feelings, alongside the outcomes.

**Logic: the thinking brain.** It asks, "Do the numbers actually work? What's the mechanism?" Give it something it can check:

- the worked numbers, the mechanism step by step, and a test the viewer can run on their own business tonight;
- logic comes after safety and feeling, and it's what lets them justify the decision they already feel.

**Identity.** Close every script so the new belief reads as who they already are: "You're the kind of owner who..." Present it as an extension of a decision they've already made.

## 3. Extra belief-shift moves to use

Use at least three per script, varied across your three scripts:

- **Pattern interrupt.** A short, surprising line that breaks the expected thought, e.g. "The bank isn't the hard part. You are."
- **The question they can't un-ask**, posed and left hanging a beat before the answer.
- **The reframe of an existing fear into evidence for the new belief,** e.g. "The fact that you're cautious is exactly why lenders like you."
- **"You already do this"** points to something they do in their own business that is the same mechanism, so the new belief feels familiar, not foreign. For example: they already use supplier terms, which is seller finance in miniature.
- **The contrast pair:** two owners, same starting point, one belief apart, five years later.
- **The small yes.** A self-test with an obvious answer that walks them toward the conclusion.
- **Naming the inner voice:** "Part of you is thinking..." answered calmly, not argued with.

## Rules

- **Length:** the final script is 2,900–3,400 words. Cut weaker lines to make room. Don't pad.
- **Facts:** no new facts about Josh, his deals or named people. Analogies and scenes are clearly illustrative.
- **Voice:** no exclamation marks, no hype, no fake urgency.
- **Flow:** keep "See how that works?" to at most twice and ellipses in bursts. Don't let it become a list of analogies.
- **Format:** the exact same file format as before: `# N. Title`, then plain paragraphs, no markdown in the script. Overwrite your scripts in place in the same file.
