# 7. Debt Isn't What Sinks a Business (This One Number Is)

Debt has never sunk a business on its own. What sinks a business is debt that nobody measured before they signed for it.

I know that's not how most owners hear the word. You hear "debt" and you think of someone you knew who borrowed against the house, had one bad year, and spent the next five climbing out of it.

So you made yourself a rule. No debt, or as little as you can get away with. And for a long time that rule has probably kept you safe.

By the end of this, you'll know the one number that separates debt that sinks an owner from debt that buys them a second business. You'll know how to work it out on the back of an envelope, and the rule that keeps it from ever touching the business you already own.

Because there's debt, and then there's serviceable debt. They look the same on a loan document. They behave completely differently in a bad year.

Let's take that apart, one piece at a time.

Start with the fear itself, because it isn't wrong. It's just looking at the wrong part of the picture.

When a business goes under carrying debt, the debt is the thing you see at the end. It's on the letter from the bank. It's the reason everyone gives at the pub afterwards.

But go back a few years before that letter and look at what actually happened. In the stories owners tell me, the owner nearly always borrowed an amount. They asked the bank how much they could get, the bank told them, and they took it.

What they never asked was how much the business could repay in a normal year, with room left over for a bad one.

That's the whole difference. One owner borrowed an amount. The other borrowed a payment the business could carry. Same word on the paperwork, a completely different kind of risk.

So when you say debt is dangerous, you're right, up to a point. Debt without a ratio is dangerous. Which leaves the obvious question of what the ratio is.

It's called debt service coverage. Banks shorten it to DSCR, and it's simpler than it sounds.

You take the profit the business makes in a year, the free cash it throws off after it's paid its people and its bills, and you divide it by the total loan repayments for that same year. Principal and interest, all of it.

If the business makes one dollar fifty for every dollar it owes in repayments, that's one and a half times. And one and a half times is the line I work to on every deal. Not one point one. Not one point two because the seller's forecast looks lovely. One and a half, on real numbers.

Let me show you what that looks like with an illustrative business. Say it makes five hundred thousand dollars a year in profit, and it's done that for three years running, on its tax returns, not on a spreadsheet the seller put together last week.

Divide five hundred thousand by one and a half and you get roughly three hundred and thirty three thousand. That's the most the business can put toward debt in a year and still sit inside the rule.

Not the most a bank will lend you. The most this business can repay. That's the ceiling, and the whole deal gets built underneath it.

A word of caution here, because this is where the number gets fudged. There's the profit a seller tells you about, and there's the profit the business actually makes. Before any offer, you want three years of financials, the tax returns that match them, and revenue broken down by financial year.

If the returns don't line up with the pitch, the ratio is built on sand, and so is the debt. One and a half times on a hopeful number is worth nothing. One and a half times on three years of returns is something you and a lender can both lean on.

And here's where the picture turns over. Most owners walk into an acquisition asking, how much can I borrow? ...

...The owner who knows the number asks something else entirely. How much can this business repay? ...

...Because the first question is about you. Your savings, your house, your history. The second question is only about the business you're buying, and that's the only thing the debt should ever depend on.

So which of those two questions have you been asking?

There's a second piece to this, and it's the one that catches good operators out.

There's debt the business you buy carries, and there's debt you end up carrying for it. On day one they look identical.

The rule I hold every deal to is plain. The business you buy pays its own debt, out of its own cash flow. You never prop it up with the first business.

The moment a deal only works if your existing business tops up the repayments, it's stopped being serviceable debt. It's become a bet, and the stake is the thing you've spent years building.

That's where most of the stories you've heard actually come from. Not from the debt on the new business. From the owner quietly moving money across from the old one, month after month, hoping the new one comes good.

At one and a half times, hope doesn't come into it. The target's own profit covers the repayments and leaves fifty cents of cushion on every dollar. Your first business stays exactly as it is, beside it, untouched.

At one and a half times, that's exactly where you land. One times. The business still pays every cent of its debt. There's nothing left over for you that year, and it isn't a fun year, but nobody's ringing the bank and nobody's touching your first business.
