# 10. Why Waiting for the Right Time to Buy a Business Is the Riskiest Move You Can Make

Think back to the biggest decision you've made in your business. Maybe it was your first hire. Maybe it was signing the lease on a bigger site, or walking out of a job that paid you well so you could back yourself.

Now be honest. How much did you actually know when you made it?

Not all of it. Probably not even most of it. You had a few numbers on a page, a feel for the work, and a sense that if you waited much longer, the chance would go to someone else.

And it worked. That's why you've got a business turning over seven figures, and that's why you're watching this.

So here's the odd part. The same owner who backed themselves at the start, on half the information, now has a team, a track record and real profit... and says they'll buy a business when the timing is right.

By the end of this, you'll know why that timing never shows up, what waiting actually costs in plain numbers, and the one shift in how you look at risk that lets owners move before they feel ready.

Let's start with the belief itself, because it sounds sensible. "I'll do it when the timing is right." Nobody argues with that sentence. It sounds patient. It sounds like the sort of thing a careful owner says over a beer.

And there's truth in it. Timing does matter. Buying the wrong business, at the wrong price, in the wrong year, can hurt you, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

But when you take "the right timing" apart, it isn't one thing. It's three, and most owners have never pulled them apart. The first is the market. The second is your own business. The third is you.

Most owners think they're waiting on the first one. Almost all of them are actually waiting on the third.

Take the market first. This is the one you hear most. Wait till rates come down. Wait till the economy settles. Wait till the election's out of the way.

Here's what's actually happening when a deal gets funded. Nobody lends against the mood of the economy. The money is lent against the cash flow of the business you're buying, and the rule we work to is simple. The free cash flow has to cover the debt repayments at least one and a half times.

Let me show you what that means with an illustrative number. Say a business makes two hundred and fifty thousand a year in profit. Divide that by one and a half and you get about a hundred and sixty-six thousand. That's the most that business should ever be asked to repay in a year. Not what the seller wants. Not what a lender might stretch to. What the business can carry with a cushion left over.

And I know what some of you are thinking, because I'd think it too. "No money down" sounds like a late-night infomercial. So let's be clear about where the money actually comes from. Part of it is a bank. Part of it is the seller, getting paid over time instead of all on day one. Sometimes it's a sixty-forty split, sometimes seventy, twenty, ten, sometimes the seller carries the whole thing. Either way, the repayments come out of the business you bought, and they have to clear that ratio first.

Now, if rates go up, what changes? The structure changes. More of the price gets paid to the seller over time, the term gets longer, the split between the bank and the seller moves. What doesn't change is the ratio. One and a half times is one and a half times in any year.

So there's a good time to buy, and then there's a good deal to buy. A deal that clears one and a half times is a good deal in a rough market. A deal that doesn't clear it is a bad deal in the best market you'll ever see.

The ratio protects you. The calendar doesn't.

Now let's go underneath all three, because there's a reason this belief feels so safe. It comes down to two kinds of risk.

Passive risk is the risk you take by doing nothing. It never shows up on a statement. Nobody sends you an invoice for it. And because nothing gets signed, it feels like no risk at all.

That's the trick of it. Waiting feels like zero. It isn't zero. It's just unpriced.
