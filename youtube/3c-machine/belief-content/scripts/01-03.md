For most owners I speak with, the honest answer is years. Years of marketing, hiring, quoting and chasing, one customer at a time. And that's the belief I want to take apart with you today... the idea that growth means winning more customers.

Now, the common view isn't wrong. Customers are the lifeblood of a business. Every dollar you've ever banked came from someone who decided to pay you. So when you want more dollars, the obvious move is more someones.

And that's true, as far as it goes.

But here's what's actually going on underneath it. There's growing, and then there's compounding. On a chart they look alike for the first year or two. Underneath, they're two completely different machines.

Think about two ways to end up with an orchard. The first is to buy a paddock of seedlings. You plant them, water them, stake them against the wind, and wait years before most of them bear anything worth picking. The second is to buy the orchard over the fence, where the trees are twenty years old, the fruit is already on them this season, and the harvest helps pay for the land.

That's the whole difference. Growing is planting seedlings. Compounding starts when you buy trees that already bear.

Growing is adding. You win a customer, you serve them, you win the next one. Each one costs you something to get, something to keep, and a slice of your week to look after. The line goes up, and it goes up at exactly the speed of your effort.

Compounding is when what you already have starts paying for what comes next. The profit from one thing funds the next thing, the next thing adds its own profit to the pile, and the bigger pile pays for the one after that.

Most owners spend twenty years growing and assume they've been compounding. That isn't a criticism. Nobody ever sat them down and showed them the difference.

So let's take it apart. There are three pieces to this, and most owners only ever look at the first. The first piece is where the growth comes from. The second is who pays for it. And the third is what it builds once it's in place.

# 2. You Don't Need Money to Buy a Business (You Need a Structure It Can Carry)

At twenty-five, I was unemployed. No savings, no experience, and nothing in the bank a lender would have looked at twice.

That's the year I bought my first business, a physio clinic, without putting down a deposit.

Not because someone handed me money, and not because I found a loophole. It happened because I stopped asking the question nearly every owner asks first, which is "how much money do I need?"... and started asking a better one.

That better question is what we're going to work through. Because the belief that you need money to buy a business is quietly costing owners who have far more going for them than I had at twenty-five. By the end, you'll know where the money in a deal like mine actually comes from, what the terms looked like, and how to tell whether a business can carry its own purchase.

Here's the common view. You save up a deposit, you take it to the bank, the bank lends you the rest, and you spend the next decade paying it off. That's how most people buy a house, so it's natural to assume it's how a business gets bought too.

And for some deals, that is how it goes.

But look at what that path really asks of you. Saving a deposit big enough for a serious business takes years. Then, when you finally walk into the bank, there's a fair chance they say no, or a private equity group outbids you before your paperwork's done.

The other path people reach for is investors. It looks faster on paper, but each cheque tends to take months to land, and every investor you bring in takes a piece of your equity, a piece of your control, and a say in how and when you exit.

So most owners look at those two paths, decide neither suits them, and conclude that buying just isn't for them yet.

Here's what's actually happening. There's paying for a business, and there's funding one.

Think about an owner-driver buying a prime mover. Very few of them save up and pay cash for the truck. They look at the freight it's going to haul, work out whether those loads cover the repayments with enough left for fuel, tyres and a slow month, and they finance it against the work. The truck pays for itself on the road.

That's funding. Paying would be emptying your savings for the truck and then hoping the freight turns up. Funding is knowing the freight covers it before you sign.

A business works the same way. Paying for one means your money goes in first, and then you hope the business earns it back. Funding one means you arrange where the money comes from, so the business carries the cost of its own purchase. Your savings aren't the engine. The business's profit is.

For a lot of owners, savings were never the engine of a deal at all. They were the handbrake.

Let me ask you something, and sit with it for a second before you answer. If you had the full price of a good business sitting in your account tomorrow, would you hand all of it over on day one... or would you rather the business paid for itself?

Most owners need a second with that one.

And almost every owner I put that to picks the second. Which means the money was never really what was stopping you. The structure was.

So let's take it apart. There are four pieces to funding a business, and your savings account isn't one of them.

The first piece is where the money comes from.

In most deals, it comes from some mix of three places: a lender, the seller, and the business's own cash flow. How you blend them is the structure. Sometimes it's sixty per cent from the bank and forty from the seller. Sometimes it's seventy, twenty, ten. Sometimes the seller carries the whole thing, which is what's called one hundred per cent vendor finance.

A well-built deal is often over-financed as well. That means the funding arranged covers more than the price alone, so there's working capital in the business from the first week. The point of that isn't to look clever. It's so the business you've bought doesn't have to lean on the business you already own to make payroll.

Which leads to the first smaller distinction. There's the price, and there are the terms.

Two utes sit on the same lot with the same sticker on the windscreen. One is cash only, today. The other comes with years to pay at a low rate. Same price. A completely different deal for whoever drives it away.

That's the gap between price and terms. Most buyers fixate on the price, because it's the big number at the top of the page. But the price only tells you what the business costs. The terms tell you how it gets paid, when, by whom, and out of what.

So when you've looked at businesses before, were you looking at the price, or reading the terms?

The second piece is what the business can carry.

This is the rule that keeps the whole thing honest, and it's the one I'd want you to hold onto if you forget everything else. The business's free cash flow has to cover its debt repayments at least one and a half times over. It's called the debt service cover ratio, and one and a half times is the floor.

Walk into any warehouse and every bay of racking has a load rating on it. That rating isn't set by how much stock you'd like to put up there. It's set by what the frame can carry. And nobody sensible loads it right to the number, because one day a forklift clips an upright.

One and a half times is the load rating on a deal. It's set by the business's profit, not by how big a deal you'd like, and it leaves a margin for the day something clips you.

Here's how it plays out, as a simple illustration. Say a business makes two hundred and fifty thousand a year in profit. Divide that by one and a half and you get roughly a hundred and sixty-six thousand. That's the most the business should pay out in debt each year. Over ten years, that comes to about one point six six million paid to the seller.

Now sit with what that means. A business making two hundred and fifty thousand can carry a purchase that, paid over time, adds up to about one point six six million. It does it out of its own profit, and it keeps a third of that profit back as a buffer the whole way through.

See how that works? You didn't start by asking how much money you had. You started by asking how much the business could carry, and the answer set the size of the deal.

That's the next distinction. There's the deposit, and there's the debt service. Savers think about the deposit, the lump they need up front.
