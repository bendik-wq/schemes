# Hypnotic devices for B2B belief content

Source: Joe Vitale and Larry Dotson, *Hypnotic Writing Swipe File* (2002–08). Lines in quotation marks are verbatim, original typos included. Reader: an owner of a $1M–$20M business weighing up buying a competitor or a retiring owner's business with Josh Li / G&L M&A Advisory.

**The one rule.** A device can carry the reader to a point. It can't make the point true. If you can't check the point, cut the device. Anything in [brackets] has to be swapped for a real, consented detail. If there isn't one, drop the line.

---

## 1. Devices worth using

### 1.1 Openings: set the deal for their time, start with something they already accept
**What it does:** tells the reader what they'll get for their attention, or starts with something they already know is true, so they trust the next sentence.

From the file:
- "By the time you finish reading this document, you'll will have learned..."
- "In the next 2 minutes you are going to learn..."
- "You probably already know this..."
- "I'll get straight to the point..."

For Josh:
- "By the end of this you'll know how a deal for a $4M business gets funded without your cash going in first, and the two places that structure usually breaks."
- "You've probably noticed a few owners in your patch heading for 65 with nobody lined up to take over. What's less obvious is what that does to the price and the terms."

### 1.2 Future pacing
**What it does:** puts the reader at a specific point in the future and has them judge the decision from there. For operators, make it a dated, operational picture with the work and the risk included. No beach.

From the file:
- "Imagine, for a moment, that it's a month from today..."
- "Picture yourself five years from now..."
- "Just suppose..."

For Josh:
- "Imagine it's eighteen months from now. You run two sites, the vendor note gets paid out of combined cash flow, and the hard question on your desk is which of two ops managers runs the merged crew."
- "Just suppose the owner down the road sells to a roll-up instead. Picture your next tender against a competitor with twice your balance sheet."

### 1.3 Story methods
**What it does:** a story gets past "that won't work for me" better than an argument does. The file names three types: first person, endorsement and future-paced.

From the file:
- "First person story: Where you talk about how you used a product or service to get the result."
- "Endorsement story: Someone like your reader used your product or service and got the result."
- "John was skeptical but used the Hypnotic Swipe file and he was amazed at how easily he could write ads and letters that got results."

For Josh (real deals only, with consent, numbers stated, the problems left in):
- "[On one deal I worked on], the bank wanted another 10% equity in week six that nobody had. Here's how the vendor terms were reworked to close the gap, and what it cost the buyer."
- "[Client], who runs a [$6M] electrical contractor, was sceptical too. He asked the same thing you're asking: why would a seller wait to be paid? Here's what his vendor told him."

### 1.4 Copy connectors
**What it does:** names the reader's objection before they raise it and keeps them reading into the next section.

From the file:
- "You're probably wondering..."
- "Now, let me ask you this..."
- "In a moment, I'll tell you..."
- "If you're thinking..."

For Josh:
- "You're probably wondering why a retiring owner would take 40% at settlement and the rest over four years. In a moment I'll go through the reasons sellers give us most often."
- "If you're thinking this only works when the books are clean, hold that thought. It's the right question, and it's usually where the price gets renegotiated."

### 1.5 Presuppositions and soft embedded suggestions
**What it does:** writes "when", not "if", so the next step reads as already under way. Use it only for small steps like reading on, running the numbers or making a list. Never use it to presume the buying decision.

From the file:
- "And as you absorb this information, you'll..."
- "Now that you know what has been happening..."
- "Since you're still here, I'm going to assume..."
- "think about this" (from the embedded-command list)

For Josh:
- "When you run your own numbers on a target, start from the last three years of actual EBITDA, not the seller's forecast."
- "Since you've read this far, I'll assume the idea doesn't strike you as mad. So let's talk about terms."

### 1.6 Agreement framing
**What it does:** builds momentum out of things the reader already agrees with. The file does this with tag questions to create a "yes trance". The B2B version states the obvious plainly and lets the reader nod. Leave the tag off.

From the file:
- "Use statements at the beginning of the ad copy that your prospects already know to be true. This creates trust right away."
- "I think you'll agree..."
- "Would you agree"
- "You know what I mean, don't you?" (as an example of what not to do. Drop the tag.)

For Josh:
- "Organic growth in a mature trade business is slow. Five to ten per cent is a good year. Buying a competitor can add half your revenue in one settlement. Neither is free, and the second carries more risk."
- "Would you agree the hard part of getting past $5M isn't the work, it's the people? An acquisition buys a team that already turns up on Monday."

### 1.7 Sensory language
**What it does:** makes abstract finance feel real. Use details from the operator's world: the site, the P&L, the phone call.

From the file:
- "Let me show you"
- "Look at this"
- "Get a feel for this"
- "It rings true"

For Josh:
- "Let me show you what this looks like on one page: price, what's paid at settlement, what's paid out of the target's own cash flow, and what's left for you."
- "Get a feel for it before you sign anything. Walk the site, sit in on a Monday toolbox meeting, and listen to how the staff talk about the owner."

### 1.8 P.S. closers
**What it does:** the P.S. is often what gets read last, or first. Use it to restate one point and offer one low-pressure next step. The file's "but/however" move works when what follows is true and useful.

From the file:
- "It isn't necessary to order now, however I have one more thing to tell you..."
- "You don't have to order now, but consider you'll receive the product instantly after your order."
- "Before you make your decision, ask yourself this critical question...."
- "If you're not totally convinced yet..."

For Josh:
- "P.S. You don't need to do anything with this today. But ask yourself one question: if a retiring owner rang tomorrow and offered you the business on terms, would you know what to check first?"
- "P.S. If you're not convinced yet, good. Send me the three questions you'd ask a seller and I'll tell you which one matters most."

### 1.9 Bullets
**What it does:** lets skimmers see what's in the piece. Keep each one specific, numbered and practical. Leave out every modifier in the file ("Just incredible!" and the like).

From the file:
- "(No.) mistakes that..."
- "How to tell if..."
- "The difference between..."
- "What to do when..."

For Josh:
- "The 3 numbers a bank checks before it funds senior debt on an acquisition"
- "How to tell if a seller's add-backs are real or wishful"
- "What to do when the seller wants all cash at settlement"

### 1.10 Guarantee framing
**What it does:** lowers the risk the reader sees. Never guarantee an outcome. You can't promise a deal will close, a price or a return. What you can be honest about is process: what you'll do, what it costs, and what happens if a deal doesn't go ahead. Only say it if it's in the engagement terms.

From the file:
- "What would be the best way for us to prove this (product or service) really does what we say? THAT'S what we'll do for you..."
- "You be the judge"
- "Here is the proof:"

For Josh:
- "What's the best way to show you this works for your business? Test it on a real target. On the first call we'll take one business you know and sketch the structure on paper. If the numbers don't stack up, we'll say so."
- "You be the judge. Here's the deal summary from [a completed transaction, de-identified, with the client's consent]: price, terms, and the two things that nearly killed it."

### 1.11 Sentence starters
**What it does:** plain transitions that show your reasoning. This is the calmest, most usable part of the file.

From the file: "To be blunt", "Put simply", "A word of caution", "That doesn't mean", "Granted", "A case in point", "In other words"

For Josh:
- "Put simply, a good price won't save a bad handover."
- "A word of caution: no money down doesn't mean no risk. The risk sits in the debt and the vendor terms, and you need to read both."

## 2. Translate the hype

| From the file (verbatim) | B2B-grade equivalent |
|---|---|
| "You don't realize it yet, but in the next 2 minutes you're going to learn..." | "This takes about four minutes to read. It covers how the deal is funded, what the seller gets, and where it goes wrong." |
| "Here's a secret:" | "Here's something sellers rarely say upfront:" |
| "Top Secret Information:" | "What the information memorandum won't show you:" |
| "Warning!" | "One risk to check before anything else:" |
| "STOP and ask yourself this key question..." | "Before you go further, one question:" |
| "A Breakthrough System For…" | "A long-standing deal structure most owners have never had explained to them" |
| "Instant Results…" | "From signed heads of agreement, settlement usually takes [x] months." |
| "Zero Risk…" | "Here's where the risk sits, and who carries each piece." |
| "Make Money…" | "Buy EBITDA rather than build it." |
| "Hurry! Quantities are limited!" | "We run [x] mandates at a time because each deal takes [y] hours of work." (Only if it's true.) |
| "Beyond Your Wildest Dreams (Fantasies, Expectations, etc.)…" | "Unremarkable, if the numbers are right. That's the point." |
| "Absolute Fact!" | "According to [named source, year]," |
| "Little-Known Secrets…" / "Closely-Guarded Secrets…" | "What experienced buyers check first" |
| "Dear Future Millionaire," | "Dear Fellow Business Owner," (also in the file) |
| "Do it now, while you're thinking about it!" | "If this is relevant in the next 12 months, book a 30-minute call. If not, keep this for later." |

## 3. Do not use

| Device (verbatim) | Why not |
|---|---|
| **Confusers (Ch 9):** "Pumpkins fly. You'll order Joe's books on "Hypnotic Writing" sooner or later, anyway." The file's stated aim is "intentionally making your reader dizzy so they will not question your next command." | It exists to get around the reader's judgement. That's manipulative by design, and an operator will read it as either a scam or nonsense. |
| **Fake proof:** "If you have no proof to support your facts you can always use words and phrases that people naturally assume contain factual information." Also "Everyone knows...", "Any expert will agree that...", "Studies will soon prove that...", "The future will prove this fact:...". Fact labels with no source, such as "Proven Fact:". | Presenting something as fact without a basis is misleading conduct under the Australian Consumer Law (s18, s29). It also breaks Josh's no-invented-proof rule. Every claim needs a source or has to be framed as opinion. |
| **Fake urgency and scarcity:** "Hurry! Quantities are limited!", "We reserve the right to withdraw this extremely rare offer at any time.", "After today I am seriously considering raising this price to ($) So act now!", "If you order in the next (No.) minutes I'll throw in these (No.) bonuses:", "There is nothing more I can do for you, you either have to try out our product or order in the next 15 minutes." | False deadlines and limits are misleading. Nobody signs a multi-million-dollar acquisition on a 15-minute clock, and owners who see one stop trusting anything else you say. |
| **Guarantees you don't mean:** "Relax. Most people never enforce a guarantee, anyway." | This means offering a promise you're betting won't be called. That's deceptive. Don't offer any guarantee you aren't happy to have enforced in full. |
| **Outcome guarantees:** "I'll personally guarantee that you'll (results) or you pay me nothing!" | You can't guarantee deal flow, closings, prices or returns. Promising them is an income or outcome promise. |
| **False majority:** "Just think, you can't go wrong because 2 out of every 3 visitors buy our product." The file's instruction: "explain how the majority of people have purchased your product." | Made-up social proof. Only use real, countable figures. |
| **Command-questions, hidden and stop commands:** "You will order now, will you not?", "I'm wondering if....(you will buy today)", "STOP and listen to your own thoughts. You want to buy all of Joe Vitale's books and tapes now..." | Transparent pressure that puts words in the reader's head. Sophisticated buyers read it as desperation. |
| **Inflating the problem:** "Make their problems seem bigger than they actually are by using "what if" questions." | Exaggerating risk to sell is deceptive. Describe the problem at its real size and with real numbers. |
| **Trance and unconscious-mind lines:** "Now I want your unconscious mind to listen very carefully...", "And you will sink deeper and deeper...", "When you're lying in bed tonight, you will think of all the benefits of buying our product." | Creepy and obviously manipulative. It would wreck Josh's credibility. |
| **Telling them how they feel:** "Your feeling of...is slowly vanishing away as you come closer to ordering..." | Presumptuous. "You might be wondering" is fine. Telling them what they feel isn't. |
| **Ads dressed as articles:** "A key to getting your ad read is making your ad's headline not look like a headline and your ad not look like an ad." | Teaching content is fine. Presenting a pitch as independent editorial isn't. Always make the call to action and who's behind the piece clear. |
| **Wealth and payback claims:** "Dear Future Millionaire,", "Make Money…", "Just one of these (benefits) will pay for your purchase many times over." | These imply income outcomes. Show real deal maths and let the reader draw their own conclusion. |
| **Tax claims:** "Remember, this purchase could be tax detectable." | Tax treatment is for the client's accountant, not marketing copy. |
| **Assertion and shouting:** "Obviously you can see how using this Hypnotic Swipe File will clearly make you more money...", "I'm ready to EXPLODE!" | Asserting instead of showing makes a sceptic look for the hole. Wrong voice, and no exclamation marks. |
| **Testimonial padding:** "We have over ( ) pages of testimonials, here is just small sample of them:" | Only if it's literally true. Testimonials must be real, consented and representative. |

If a piece talks about funding structures or returns in any detail, get it checked against the relevant financial-services and advertising rules before it's published. This guide is not legal advice.

## 4. Checklist: 4–6 devices in a 700–1,000-word post

**Before writing**
- [ ] One belief to shift, in one sentence (e.g. "You don't need your own cash to buy a $3M business, but you do need a seller who'll carry some of the price").
- [ ] At least one real number, one real example and one honest risk. If you're missing any of the three, don't write the post yet.

**Pick 4–6 devices, no more than one per section**
- [ ] **Opening** (1.1): what they'll know by the end, or something they already accept as true. First 50 words.
- [ ] **Connector** (1.4): name the main objection around the 25% mark.
- [ ] **Story** (1.3): one real deal or a clearly labelled hypothetical, with the numbers and the problem left in. Middle of the post.
- [ ] **Future pace** (1.2): a dated operational picture, including the work. Around 70%.
- [ ] **Sentence starter** (1.11): "A word of caution" or "To be blunt" before the risk paragraph.
- [ ] **P.S.** (1.8): one point restated and one small, optional next step.

**Keep it from reading as hypnotic**
- [ ] No device in two paragraphs in a row. Plain explanation goes in between.
- [ ] At most one "imagine/picture" per post.
- [ ] No tag questions ("...don't you?"), no "STOP", no "-ly" assertions.
- [ ] Zero exclamation marks. Count them.
- [ ] More numbers than adjectives in any paragraph that makes a claim.
- [ ] Read it aloud as Josh talking to an owner over coffee. Cut anything he wouldn't say.

**Compliance pass**
- [ ] Every factual claim has a source, or is framed as experience ("in deals we've worked on") or as opinion.
- [ ] Every example is real and consented, or labelled hypothetical. No [brackets] left in.
- [ ] No promised outcomes, returns, timelines, prices or tax results.
- [ ] Risk is stated in the same tone as the upside.
- [ ] The CTA is low-pressure, with no deadline, scarcity or bonus unless it's literally true.
- [ ] It's clear who wrote the piece and that G&L offers a paid service.

**Final test:** would a sceptical owner who's already bought a business forward this to their accountant without feeling embarrassed? If not, take out the last device you added.
