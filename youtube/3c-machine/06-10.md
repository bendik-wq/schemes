**The lesson (3:15–8:45)**

If you've been knocked back, or never even tried, it has nothing to do with your age, your bank balance or the job title on your LinkedIn. It has everything to do with who's standing next to you when you ask.

You were told you need a track record before anyone will back you. You were told you need your own money in the deal. You were told you need to know the right people.

None of that is how this stage works. The buyers who get through didn't wait for a track record. They borrowed one.

And the reason it works is simple. A retiring owner has spent 30 years building something. They're not going to hand it to a 25-year-old with nothing. But they will talk to a company with a board that looks like it knows what it's doing. Banks are the same. A credit team doesn't lend to enthusiasm. It lends to people it thinks will run the business and pay the money back. Your board is the evidence.

So, the seats. You want four.

[SCREEN: four boxes. Chair · Finance lead · Legal lead · Sector operator.]

A chair. Someone with weight. A former executive or a seasoned board member. This is the name that makes people sit up.

A finance lead. A former CFO or a credit person who can talk to a bank in the bank's language.

A legal lead. An M&A lawyer or a former general counsel.

And a sector operator. Someone who's actually run businesses in the industry you're buying in.

The people who say yes are mostly people who've recently retired. They've got the experience and the time, and plenty of them miss being involved. You're offering something meaningful plus a share of the upside.

What doesn't work is friends and family. If they haven't done deals or run what you're buying, they don't go on the board. A weak board can be worse than no board, because it tells the bank you don't know what good looks like.

Now the outreach, in order. First you pick the seat you need most. Then you build a list for it. Once you have the list, you send a short message. Then you follow up, twice.

[SCREEN: "I'm building [company] to buy and grow [industry] businesses. I've looked at your background and think you'd be a strong [chair / finance lead]. I'm offering founders' equity, vesting on our first acquisition. Open to a 30-minute call?"]

That's the whole message. No essay. No begging. You're offering a seat, not asking for a favour.

And the profile you're looking for has three things. A title a bank manager or an owner would recognise. Real time in the role. And a sign they're winding down: "former", "non-executive", "adviser", a few board roles.

Then a 30-minute call. The industry, why owners are selling, what you're building. Then what you need from them: a few hours a month, their name on the summary, and help on lender and seller calls. Founders' equity that vests on the first deal. Don't haggle over percentages on the first call.

Busy people miss messages. Send a second one a week later, and a third a week after that. A lot of yeses come from the third message, not the first.

### Script

*// TWO-TYPES OPENER · ICP: One-Business Ceiling*

**Cold open (0:00–0:45)**

[SCREEN: a pie chart. One slice labelled "73%".]

There are only two types of business owners when it comes to building a group.

The ones who use equity as currency. And the ones who sit on 100% of one business for twenty years.

That's it.

The owners on the second side usually aren't lazy. They've hit a ceiling because there's only one of them, and they've been told the only way to buy a second business is with cash they don't have.

And when they do try equity, they get it wrong one of two ways. They give away so much there's nothing left for the bank, the seller or themselves. Or they give so little nobody good says yes.

I pay my board in equity and I still keep 73% of my company. The number matters less than what the equity is tied to.

*// 7-MINUTE PROMISE*

**The map (0:45–1:15)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Credibility" lit. Caption: "Credibility · episode 2 of 5".]

Last episode we found the board. Now we pay them. Credibility, episode two of five.

In the next few minutes I'll show you how to pay a serious board without cash, so you never have to choose between giving away the company and doing everything yourself. All without a dollar of salary, and without handing anyone a veto over your exit.

Quick filter, same as always. This is for people building something to scale or sell. If you want one small business to run on your own, you probably don't need a board, and this episode isn't for you.

**The recipe, fast (1:15–2:45)**

Three rules.

One. Founders' equity of 2 to 10% per person, scaled to the seat. The chair carries the most weight, so the chair gets the top of the range. Specialists sit lower. Four seats at 10% each is 40% gone before you've bought anything. Don't do that.

Two. Nothing vests until a deal is signed. The vesting ties to a signed sale agreement on your first acquisition, the SPA, and then to 12 months of support after closing. If they don't help you get a deal done, they don't own anything. That protects you and it filters out people who just want a title.

Three. Write the job before you hand over the equity. Every seat gets clear jobs and numbers before anyone says yes. And keep control from day one: board members are minority holders, they get no veto, and there's a drag-along so nobody can block your exit.

[SCREEN: "2–10% per seat · vests on signed SPA + 12 months · KPIs first · no minority veto."]

Small slices, earned on a deal, with the job written down.

**Soft pitch (2:45–3:15)**

Equity is the one thing you can't easily take back, so this is a step I like to do with people properly. If you want me to look at your split before you offer it to anyone, book a call. Link below. Now the detail.

*// LESSON · NOT-YOUR-FAULT · BELIEF SHIFT: THAT'S NOT HOW IT WORKS · TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**The lesson (3:15–8:45)**

[SCREEN: education, not financial or legal advice. Get your own lawyer and accountant.]

Quick note first. This is education, not legal or tax advice. Get a lawyer to paper it. But you should walk into that lawyer's office knowing what you want.

If you've been stuck at one business, it has nothing to do with your work ethic, your industry or how good the business is. It has everything to do with never being shown how to pay people when you don't have cash.

Most owners think giving away equity means losing. They think the bigger your percentage, the safer you are. They think equity is what you hand over when you've run out of options.

That's not how it works. Equity is the currency you use before you have cash. Spent small, tied to a deal and written down, it buys you the people who get the next business funded.

Start with where the shares sit. You set up a holding company. That's where the board owns shares and sits as a board. Every business you buy goes into its own company underneath it. One deal, one company.

[SCREEN: diagram. HoldCo (you + board) → SPV 1 · SPV 2 · SPV 3.]

And the reason is ring-fencing. Each business's debt and problems stay in its own box. And your board gets a share of the whole group, not of one clinic.

When you set up the holding company, issue a lot of shares. A million, say. It's much easier to hand someone 70,000 shares out of a million than to restructure later because you started with 100.

Now a worked example. Illustrative numbers, round, and not my actual cap table.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only." 1,000,000 shares. You 730,000 (73%). Chair 80,000 (8%). Finance 70,000 (7%). Legal 60,000 (6%). Sector operator 60,000 (6%).]

You keep 730,000 shares. 73%. The chair gets 8%. Finance lead, 7%. Legal lead, 6%. Sector operator, 6%. That's 27% to a board that makes a bank and a seller take you seriously.

The way you decide who sits where in the range is three questions about each seat. How much their name moves a bank or a seller. How many hours they'll actually give you. And how hard they'd be to replace. The chair usually wins on the first, so the chair goes higher. A sector operator on seller calls every week might earn more than a lawyer who reviews two documents a quarter. Weigh the work, not the title.

And leave headroom. You'll want to add people later. A group CEO one day, maybe another board seat when you move into a second industry. Start at 60% and you've got nowhere to go. Keep yourself at 70% or above after the board.

One more thing that protects your number. When a seller keeps a stake in the business they sell you, that stake usually sits in the company that owns that one business, not in your holding company. So a seller's rollover doesn't dilute your board or you at the top.

And the reason 7% is enough for a former CFO is clear from their side. Again, illustrative, not a forecast. Say over a few years the group buys five businesses, each making $500,000 profit. That's $2.5 million of profit. You buy small businesses at around four times profit. Bigger groups sell at eight, ten, sometimes more. At eight times, that group is worth $20 million. 7% of that is $1.4 million, for a few hours a month and some phone calls.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only. 5 × $500K profit = $2.5M. At 8× = $20M. 7% = $1.4M. Not a forecast. Results not typical."]

That's the pitch. Not "help me out". It's "come in at the ground floor of something that's worth a lot if we build it".

Now vesting, in order. First, nothing at the handshake. Then, once you sign the SPA on your first deal, a portion vests. Then the rest vests over the 12 months after closing, while they help you settle in. And if someone walks away before that, the unvested shares come back to you. Your lawyer will call these leaver terms. Have them.

[SCREEN: "Handshake: 0 · First SPA signed: part vests · 12 months post-close: rest vests · Leaves early: unvested shares return."]

Next, the job. Daniel Iles has a lesson here that cost him real money. He says never let a senior hire define their own KPIs. He did it twice, with roles paying $24,000 and $30,000 a month, held by proven people. Both failed. His fix: set the KPIs before you hire.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles: 'I lost millions learning these harsh business lessons' 1:31. Link below."]

A board seat is a senior hire paid in equity. Same rule. Write it before they say yes.

Put all of it in one short letter for each person. Their role. Their percentage. How it vests. What they've agreed to do each month. What happens if they leave early. And confidentiality, because they'll see seller financials. One or two pages, signed, before their name goes on your executive summary. If someone won't sign a page that fair, you've learned something cheaply.

[SCREEN: four cards.]

Chair: two introductions a month to lenders or owners, and attends every lender meeting. Finance lead: reviews every offer within 48 hours, signs off that the model clears the 1.5 debt cover rule. Legal lead: reviews the NDA, the offer letter and the scope of the sale agreement. Sector operator: on seller calls and staff interviews before you close. Plus a board meeting a month, two to four hours. Equity only. No cash.

Last, control. Set this up from the start, not when there's a problem. Minority holders get no veto and no voting control. There's a drag-along, so if you sell the group, they sell with you on the same terms. And transfer restrictions, so nobody sells their shares to a stranger.

[SCREEN: "Drag-along · no minority veto · transfer restrictions. Set up from day one."]

And get directors' and officers' insurance. Good people ask about it, and it protects them.

**Do this this week (8:45–9:15)**

Four things.

One. Write the job and two or three numbers for each seat.

Two. Sketch your split on a million shares. Keep yourself at 70% or more.

Three. Write the vesting in one line: signed SPA, then 12 months.

Four. Book an hour with a lawyer to quote a holding company, a shareholders' agreement and board equity terms.

**The recipe, fast (1:15–2:45)**

Three rules.

Do those three and a partner can make you faster. Skip them and the partner is the biggest risk in the deal.

**The lesson (3:15–8:45)**

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

There are three good reasons to buy with a partner. More hours, because this needs 40-plus a week. Assets, because cash, an existing business or property make it easier and faster. And skills you don't have. If a partner brings none of those, Daniel's advice is to go solo, work twice as hard and keep 100%. And remember, you can borrow credibility from a board without giving anyone a full partner's share.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles: 'The truth about running a business with your bros' 6:44 and 7:16."]

When partnerships blow up, it usually has nothing to do with one of you being greedy, or lazy, or a bad person. It has everything to do with terms written for year one still being used in year five.

Before you know that, a partnership feels like a question of trust. You trust them, they trust you, so why write anything down.

After you know it, you see it's a question of conditions. Daniel's line: "The reason most partnerships suck is because the conditions change over time and the terms don't." He uses a tree. Years one and two, it needs a lot of watering. By years five to ten it fruits with almost no work. That's great if it's yours. In a partnership, one person taking fruit without watering feels wrong to the other.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles, 'The truth about running a business with your bros' 0:00 and 1:33. Links below."]

For a buyer, the tree is very real. In years one to three you're paying the seller and the bank, settling staff and customers in, and working long hours. Later the debt's paid down and the business throws off cash. That's exactly when an unwritten 50/50 starts to hurt.

So, roles. Daniel says, "No good company has two CEOs."

[SCREEN: three columns. Get: sourcing, seller calls, offers · Keep: running what you buy, staff, customers · Jail: capital, lenders, legals, numbers.]

Here's the buyer's version. Get: one of you makes the calls, talks to sellers, writes the offers. Keep: one of you runs the business after closing, keeps the staff and customers. Jail: capital, lenders, lawyers, the numbers. Two of you can split three buckets. Two of you can't both own "Get".

Then decisions. Daniel runs a business with his wife and they use a simple tie-break: "The person with more information takes over." Write that in. The operator decides on staff. The deal-finder decides which seller to chase. Nobody relitigates the other's area.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles, 'The reality of scaling a $5M/mo business with my wife' 4:06."]

Now the paperwork. A company, not a partnership. A partnership gives you no protection between the partners and the business's liabilities. Then a shareholders' agreement with five things: transfer restrictions, so nobody sells to a stranger. Pre-emptive rights, so new shares are offered to you first. Drag-along and tag-along. A deadlock rule. And exit terms.

[SCREEN: "Transfer restrictions · pre-emptive rights · drag/tag-along · deadlock rule · exit terms."]

The buy-sell clause sits inside that. It answers: if one of us leaves, who buys the shares, at what price, worked out how, and paid over how long. Agree the formula now, not the number.

Then the issue nobody raises until a bank does. Personal guarantees. If a lender wants one, someone has to sign. Larger deals, typically over about $5 million, often don't need one, because the business's assets give the bank its security. But every lender and every deal is different. So agree now who signs if it comes up, and how you share that risk if it does.

And the reason this all goes before the LOI is timing. First the seller signs. Then you're usually in 60 to 90 days of exclusivity. Then the seller wants to know who'll run the business, and the bank wants to know who owns it and who signs. You don't want to be negotiating with your partner and the seller in the same week.

Here's a worked example. Illustrative, round numbers.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only. Partner A: deal-finder, 50 hrs/week, no cash. Partner B: operator, 40 hrs/week, owns property. Board: 25%. A and B split 75%."]

Partner A finds deals, 50 hours a week, no cash. Partner B will run the business, 40 hours a week, and owns property. The board holds 25% of the holding company. A and B split the other 75%.

Version one: 37.5% each, nothing written. Version two: same split, but each person's shares vest on their role. A's on signing the first SPA, B's over the first 12 months of running it. A buy-sell formula based on last year's profit and an agreed multiple. A deadlock rule. And a date each year to review the terms, because conditions will change. Version two costs a few hours with a lawyer. Version one can cost you the business.

The board is still there in that example. A partner doesn't replace the board. A partner is a co-owner with a full-time job in the business. A board member is a few hours a month, a name a bank trusts, and a small slice that vests on a deal. Two co-buyers with no credible board are still two people with no track record.

One more from Daniel. His COO is paid a salary with a guaranteed floor, and terms that are always open to renegotiation, not equity. If your "partner" is really an operator you need, that can be the better deal for both of you.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles, 'The truth about running a business with your bros' 4:41."]

And the way to have this conversation without it getting weird is on paper. Each of you writes your own version on one page, separately. Your role, your hours, what you bring, what you expect to own, what happens if you leave. Then swap. Where the pages match, you're done. Where they don't, that's the conversation you'd otherwise have in a lawyer's office during due diligence, with a seller waiting.

Five. Draft the buy-sell in plain English and book a lawyer to paper it.
