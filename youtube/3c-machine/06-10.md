## Episode 06 · Steal Credibility: Build a Board in 30 Days

### Metadata

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: black. White text: "25. Unemployed. No savings. Zero experience."]

At 25 I was unemployed. No savings. No experience running a business. A bank had no reason to lend me a dollar, and a retiring owner had no reason to take my call. So I stopped selling them me. I sold them my board. And I found every person on it through LinkedIn and cold messages.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Credibility" lit. Caption: "Credibility · episode 1 of 5".]

Last episode was about deciding at 60%. This is the first thing you do once you've decided. We're in Credibility now. Episode one of five.

This is for people who'll put 40-plus hours a week into buying a company and want something big at the end. A group, a legacy, an exit. If you're after a four-hour work week, this isn't it, and that's fine. Click away now.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Here's the whole lesson in three points.

One. Nobody checks your track record if your board has one. A seller asks, "Who are these people?" A bank asks, "Who's running this?" If the answer is a former CFO, an M&A lawyer and someone who's run businesses in that industry for 20 years, the conversation changes. You stop being the risk. You become the person who put a serious team together.

Two. You pay them in equity, not cash. Founders' equity, usually somewhere between 2 and 10% each. That's the trade. They get a piece of something at the ground floor. You get their names, their judgement and their networks. You've got no cash, so equity is the only currency you have. Use it.

Three. You find them cold. LinkedIn and direct outreach. Not your mates. Not your uncle. I had no network at 25. I built one in messages. The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder in my company today, and I still keep 73% of it.

[SCREEN: "Board found entirely via LinkedIn and cold outreach. Josh keeps 73%."]

That's it. Borrow the credibility you haven't had time to earn yet. Then go and use it.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people do exactly this. Pick the seats, write the messages, set the equity so it doesn't bite you later. If you want to work it out with me, book a call. The link's below. It's me on the call, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[B-ROLL: an older owner behind a clinic front desk. Then a bank meeting room.]

Start with why this works. A retiring owner has spent 30 years building something. They're not going to hand it to a 25-year-old with nothing. But they will talk to a company with a board that looks like it knows what it's doing. Banks are the same. A credit team doesn't lend to enthusiasm. It lends to people it thinks will run the business and pay the money back. Your board is the evidence.

So, the seats. You want four.

[SCREEN: four boxes. Chair · Finance lead · Legal lead · Sector operator.]

A chair. Someone with weight. A former executive, someone who's run a large company or sat on boards. This is the name that makes people sit up.

A finance lead. A former CFO or a credit person. Someone who understands deal structures and can talk to a bank in the bank's language.

A legal lead. An M&A lawyer or a former general counsel.

And a sector operator. Someone who's actually run businesses in the industry you're buying in. Buying clinics? Someone who's run clinics. Buying a trade business? Someone who's run one.

Who says yes? Mostly people who've recently retired. They've got the experience and the time, and plenty of them miss being involved. You're offering something meaningful plus a share of the upside. That's a better pitch than most people think.

What doesn't work is friends and family. If they haven't done deals or run what you're buying, they don't go on the board. A weak board can be worse than no board, because it tells the bank you don't know what good looks like.

Now the outreach. Keep it short.

[SCREEN: "I'm building [company] to buy and grow [industry] businesses. I've looked at your background and think you'd be a strong [chair / finance lead]. I'm offering founders' equity, vesting on our first acquisition. Open to a 30-minute call?"]

That's the whole message. No essay. No begging. You're offering a seat, not asking for a favour.

What do you look for in a profile? Three things. A title that a bank manager or an owner would recognise. Time in the role, not six months somewhere big. And a sign they're winding down: "former", "non-executive", "adviser", a few board roles. Those are the people with time and a reason to say yes.

Then the call. Thirty minutes. You talk about the industry, why owners are selling, what you're building and how big it could get. Then what you need from them: a few hours a month, their name on the summary, and help on lender and seller calls. You mention founders' equity and that it vests on the first deal. Don't haggle over percentages on the first call. Let them see the plan first.

And follow up. Busy people miss messages. Send a second one a week later, and a third a week after that. A lot of yeses come from the third message, not the first.

Here's a worked example. These numbers are illustrative and round. They're not a promise.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only." 100 messages → 10 replies → 4 calls → 1–2 yes.]

Say you message 100 people who fit one seat. Maybe ten reply. Maybe four take a call. Maybe one or two say yes. Do that for four seats and you're sending a few hundred messages. That's a few weeks of work, not a few years. Most people never send the first 20.

And it can move fast. Jy Kingston is one of my mentees. IT background, no finance experience. He built his board in under 30 days through LinkedIn, and had accountants and lawyers within about two weeks. He's got 12 deals active and he's closing his first.

[SCREEN: "Jy Kingston, mentee. Results not typical; records on file."]

Another of my students got the former CEO of IBM to join their company. Same method. They asked.

[SCREEN: "Student result. Results not typical; records on file."]

This is where Daniel Iles comes in. He built a business doing $5 million a month, and he says each of his big jumps came from one outside insight, not from more hours. He paid people further ahead of him for their perspective. His line is: "What do you do when you come across a challenge? You just go and buy more experience."

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles: 'Every revenue level…' 23:20 and 'Buying XP in real life' 6:14. Links below."]

He buys experience with money. You don't have money yet, so you buy it with equity. Same idea. You won't learn 30 years of deal-making in one year. So you put someone who already has it on your board.

Last point. Board first or deal first? Both work. I recommend board first. A one-page executive summary with your board on it, and a handshake on the equity. Then you go to sellers. You walk in with a team, not a story.

[SCREEN: "Board first = executive summary PDF + handshake equity."]

The handshake is not the paperwork, though. How much you give, how it vests and how you keep control is the next episode.

## Episode 07 · How Much Equity to Give Your Board

### Metadata

- **Title options**
  1. How Much Equity to Give Your Board (and Keep Control)
  2. I Keep 73% of My Company. Here's How the Rest Works.
  3. Founders' Equity: Pay Your Board Without Cash
- **Thumbnail text:** KEEP 73%
- **Runtime target:** 10–11 minutes (≈1,500–1,600 spoken words; about 11 at a natural pace)
- **Stage lit:** C1 Credibility, episode 2 of 5

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: a pie chart. One slice labelled "73%".]

Most first-time buyers get equity wrong one of two ways. They give away so much there's nothing left for the bank, the seller or themselves. Or they give so little nobody good says yes. I pay my board in equity and I still keep 73% of my company. The number matters less than what the equity is tied to.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Credibility" lit. Caption: "Credibility · episode 2 of 5".]

Last episode we found the board. Now we pay them. Credibility, episode two of five.

Quick filter, same as always. This is for people building something to scale or sell. If you want one small business to run on your own, you probably don't need a board, and this episode isn't for you.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three rules.

One. Founders' equity of 2 to 10% per person, scaled to the seat. The chair carries the most weight, so the chair gets the top of the range. Specialists sit lower. Four seats at 10% each is 40% gone before you've bought anything. Don't do that.

Two. Nothing vests until a deal is signed. The vesting ties to a signed sale agreement on your first acquisition, the SPA, and then to 12 months of support after closing. If they don't help you get a deal done, they don't own anything. That protects you and it filters out people who just want a title.

Three. Write the job before you hand over the equity. Every seat gets clear jobs and numbers before anyone says yes. And keep control from day one: board members are minority holders, they get no veto, and there's a drag-along so nobody can block your exit.

[SCREEN: "2–10% per seat · vests on signed SPA + 12 months · KPIs first · no minority veto."]

That's it. Small slices, earned on a deal, with the job written down.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

Equity is the one thing you can't easily take back, so this is a step I like to do with people properly. If you want me to look at your split before you offer it to anyone, book a call. Link below. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[SCREEN: education, not financial or legal advice. Get your own lawyer and accountant.]

Quick note first. This is education, not legal or tax advice. Get a lawyer to paper it. But you should walk into that lawyer's office knowing what you want.

Start with where the shares sit. You set up a holding company. That's where the board owns shares and sits as a board. Every business you buy goes into its own company underneath it. One deal, one company.

[SCREEN: diagram. HoldCo (you + board) → SPV 1 · SPV 2 · SPV 3.]

Why? Each business is ring-fenced. Its debt and its problems stay in its own box. And your board gets a share of the whole group, not of one clinic.

When you set up the holding company, issue a lot of shares. A million, say. It's much easier to hand someone 70,000 shares out of a million than to restructure later because you started with 100.

Now a worked example. Illustrative numbers, round, and not my actual cap table.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only." 1,000,000 shares. You 730,000 (73%). Chair 80,000 (8%). Finance 70,000 (7%). Legal 60,000 (6%). Sector operator 60,000 (6%).]

You keep 730,000 shares. 73%. The chair gets 8%. Finance lead, 7%. Legal lead, 6%. Sector operator, 6%. That's 27% to a board that makes a bank and a seller take you seriously.

How do you decide who sits where in the range? Ask three questions about each seat. How much does their name move a bank or a seller? How many hours will they actually give you? And how hard would they be to replace? The chair usually wins on the first question, so the chair goes higher. A sector operator who'll be on seller calls every week might earn more than a lawyer who reviews two documents a quarter. Weigh the work, not the title.

And leave headroom. You'll want to add people later. A group CEO one day, maybe another board seat when you move into a second industry. If you start at 60%, you've got nowhere to go. Keep yourself at 70% or above after the board.

One more thing that protects your number. When a seller keeps a stake in the business they sell you, that stake usually sits in the company that owns that one business, not in your holding company. So a seller's rollover doesn't dilute your board or you at the top.

Is 7% enough for a former CFO to say yes? Look at it from their side. Again, illustrative, not a forecast. Say over a few years the group buys five businesses, each making $500,000 profit. That's $2.5 million of profit. You buy small businesses at around four times profit. Bigger groups sell at eight, ten, sometimes more. At eight times, that group is worth $20 million. 7% of that is $1.4 million, for a few hours a month and some phone calls.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only. 5 × $500K profit = $2.5M. At 8× = $20M. 7% = $1.4M. Not a forecast. Results not typical."]

That's the pitch. Not "help me out". It's "come in at the ground floor of something that's worth a lot if we build it".

Now vesting. Here's one way to set it up. Nothing at the handshake. A portion vests when you sign the SPA on your first deal. The rest vests over the 12 months after closing, while they help you settle in. If someone walks away before that, the unvested shares come back to you. Your lawyer will call these leaver terms. Have them.

[SCREEN: "Handshake: 0 · First SPA signed: part vests · 12 months post-close: rest vests · Leaves early: unvested shares return."]

Next, the job. This is where Daniel Iles has a lesson that cost him real money. He says never let a senior hire define their own KPIs. He did it twice, with roles paying $24,000 and $30,000 a month, held by proven people. Both failed. His fix: set the KPIs before you hire.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles: 'I lost millions learning these harsh business lessons' 1:31. Link below."]

A board seat is a senior hire paid in equity. Same rule. Write it before they say yes.

Put all of it in one short letter for each person. Their role. Their percentage. How it vests. What they've agreed to do each month. What happens if they leave early. And confidentiality, because they'll see seller financials. One or two pages, signed, before their name goes on your executive summary. If someone won't sign a page that fair, you've learned something cheaply.

[SCREEN: four cards.]

Chair: two introductions a month to lenders or owners, and attends every lender meeting. Finance lead: reviews every offer within 48 hours, signs off that the model clears the 1.5 debt cover rule. Legal lead: reviews the NDA, the offer letter and the scope of the sale agreement. Sector operator: on seller calls and staff interviews before you close.

Plus a board meeting a month, two to four hours. Equity only. No cash.

Last, control. Set this up from the start, not when there's a problem. Minority holders get no veto and no voting control. There's a drag-along, which means if you sell the group, they sell with you on the same terms. And transfer restrictions, so nobody sells their shares to a stranger.

[SCREEN: "Drag-along · no minority veto · transfer restrictions. Set up from day one."]

And get directors' and officers' insurance. Good people ask about it, and it protects them.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

Four things.

One. Write the job and two or three numbers for each seat.

Two. Sketch your split on a million shares. Keep yourself at 70% or more.

Three. Write the vesting in one line: signed SPA, then 12 months.

Four. Book an hour with a lawyer to quote a holding company, a shareholders' agreement and board equity terms.

## Episode 08 · Lawyers and Accountants Who Get Paid at Closing

### Metadata

- **Title options**
  1. Lawyers and Accountants Who Get Paid at Closing
  2. How to Pay for Due Diligence With No Money
  3. Let the Deal Pay Its Own Legal Bill
- **Thumbnail text:** PAID AT CLOSING
- **Runtime target:** 10–11 minutes (≈1,500–1,600 spoken words; about 11 at a natural pace)
- **Stage lit:** C1 Credibility, episode 3 of 5

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: "How do I pay the lawyers?"]

The question right after "how do I buy with no money" is always "then how do I pay the lawyers?" Here's the answer. You pay them at closing, with money the bank lends the business on settlement day. The deal pays its own costs.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Credibility" lit. Caption: "Credibility · episode 3 of 5".]

Credibility, episode three of five. Your board is the inside team. This is the outside team: the lawyers and accountants who do the work on every deal.

This is for people buying real businesses, usually a million dollars or more in value. If you're buying a small online store off a marketplace, you won't need any of this.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three moves.

One. Build an outside bench before you need it. An M&A lawyer. An accountant who can do a quality of earnings review, which is a deep check that the profit is real. Big Four, or a strong regional firm. And an industry adviser if the sector is specialised.

Two. Ask for delayed or rolled fees. That means some or all of their bill is paid at settlement instead of as they go. Not every firm will agree. Plenty will, for the right client.

Three. At settlement, draw a working capital line from the bank. As a rough guide, that line is often around 10% of the business's annual revenue, drawn on the day. It pays the lawyers and accountants, and the rest is runway.

[SCREEN: "Bench first · fees deferred to settlement · working capital line pays them on the day."]

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: two coffee cups. Caption: "50/50. Nothing in writing."]

Two mates. 50/50. Nothing in writing. It's how a lot of people start out together, and it's one of the most dangerous ways to do it. Daniel Iles says a split like that between two founders with no plan for the money is a time bomb, and it tends to go off six to twelve months after real money starts.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Credibility" lit. Caption: "Credibility · episode 5 of 5".]

Credibility, episode five of five. The last piece of your team: the person you might buy with.

If you're buying alone, stay anyway. Most of this applies to your board, and to anyone who'll ever want a piece of what you build. If you're looking for a mate to share a small lifestyle business with, this will feel like too much paperwork. It isn't.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three rules.

One. Only partner on non-overlap. Each of you owns a different job. Daniel splits a business into three buckets: someone to get the clients, someone to keep the clients, and someone to keep the other two out of jail. For a buyer, that's the deal-finder, the operator and the person on capital, lenders and legals. Two people doing the same job is one too many.

Two. Use a company, not a partnership, and a proper shareholders' agreement. Who owns what, who decides what, how shares can be sold, and what happens if you get stuck.

Three. Sign the buy-sell clause before the offer letter. What happens if one of you wants out, stops working, gets sick or dies. You agree it while you still like each other.

[SCREEN: "Non-overlapping jobs · a company + shareholders' agreement · buy-sell before the LOI."]

Do those three and a partner can make you faster. Skip them and the partner is the biggest risk in the deal.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help co-buyers set this up before they're under exclusivity with a seller and a bank asking who's in charge. If you're buying with someone, book a call and bring them. It's me on the call. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Why buy with a partner at all? Three good reasons. More hours, because this needs 40-plus a week. Assets, because cash, an existing business or property make it easier and faster. And skills you don't have. If a partner brings none of those, Daniel's advice is to go solo, work twice as hard and keep 100%. And remember, you can borrow credibility from a board without giving anyone a full partner's share.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles: 'The truth about running a business with your bros' 6:44 and 7:16."]

Now why partnerships break. Daniel's line: "The reason most partnerships suck is because the conditions change over time and the terms don't." He uses a tree. Years one and two, it needs a lot of watering. By years five to ten it fruits with almost no work. That's great if it's yours. In a partnership, one person taking fruit without watering feels wrong to the other.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles, 'The truth about running a business with your bros' 0:00 and 1:33. Links below."]

For a buyer, the tree is very real. In years one to three you're paying the seller and the bank, settling staff and customers in, and working long hours. Later the debt's paid down and the business throws off cash. That's exactly when an unwritten 50/50 starts to hurt.

So, roles. Daniel says, "No good company has two CEOs."

[SCREEN: three columns. Get: sourcing, seller calls, offers · Keep: running what you buy, staff, customers · Jail: capital, lenders, legals, numbers.]

Here's the buyer's version. Get: one of you makes the calls, talks to sellers, writes the offers. Keep: one of you runs the business after closing, keeps the staff and customers. Jail: capital, lenders, lawyers, the numbers. Two of you can split three buckets. Two of you can't both own "Get".

Then decisions. Daniel runs a business with his wife and they use a simple tie-break: "The person with more information takes over." Write that in. The operator decides on staff. The deal-finder decides which seller to chase. Nobody relitigates the other's area.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles, 'The reality of scaling a $5M/mo business with my wife' 4:06."]

Now the paperwork. A company, not a partnership. A partnership gives you no protection between the partners and the business's liabilities. Then a shareholders' agreement with five things: transfer restrictions, so nobody sells to a stranger. Pre-emptive rights, so new shares are offered to you first. Drag-along and tag-along. A deadlock rule. And exit terms.

[SCREEN: "Transfer restrictions · pre-emptive rights · drag/tag-along · deadlock rule · exit terms."]

The buy-sell clause sits inside that. It answers: if one of us leaves, who buys the shares, at what price, worked out how, and paid over how long. Agree the formula now, not the number.

Then the question nobody asks until a bank does. Personal guarantees. If a lender wants one, who signs? Both of you? The one with the property? Larger deals, typically over about $5 million, often don't need one, because the business's assets give the bank its security. But every lender and every deal is different. So agree now who signs if it comes up, and how you share that risk if it does.

Why before the LOI? Because once a seller signs, you're usually in 60 to 90 days of exclusivity. The seller wants to know who'll run the business. The bank wants to know who owns it and who signs. You don't want to be negotiating with your partner and the seller in the same week.

Here's a worked example. Illustrative, round numbers.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only. Partner A: deal-finder, 50 hrs/week, no cash. Partner B: operator, 40 hrs/week, owns property. Board: 25%. A and B split 75%."]

Partner A finds deals, 50 hours a week, no cash. Partner B will run the business, 40 hours a week, and owns property. The board holds 25% of the holding company. A and B split the other 75%.

Version one: 37.5% each, nothing written. Version two: same split, but each person's shares vest on their role. A's on signing the first SPA, B's over the first 12 months of running it. A buy-sell formula based on last year's profit and an agreed multiple. A deadlock rule. And a date each year to review the terms, because conditions will change. Version two costs a few hours with a lawyer. Version one can cost you the business.

Notice the board is still there in that example. A partner doesn't replace the board. A partner is a co-owner with a full-time job in the business. A board member is a few hours a month, a name a bank trusts, and a small slice that vests on a deal. You'll usually want both. Two co-buyers with no credible board are still two people with no track record.

One more from Daniel. His COO is paid a salary with a guaranteed floor, and terms that are always open to renegotiation, not equity. If your "partner" is really an operator you need, that can be the better deal for both of you.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles, 'The truth about running a business with your bros' 4:41."]

How do you have this conversation without it getting weird? Each of you writes your own version on one page, separately. Your role, your hours, what you bring, what you expect to own, what happens if you leave. Then swap. Where the pages match, you're done. Where they don't, that's the conversation you'd otherwise have in a lawyer's office during due diligence, with a seller waiting.
