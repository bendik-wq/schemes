**The recipe, fast (1:20–2:40)**

One. Find the real profit. Not revenue. The profit the business makes after paying someone to run it. Say that's $250,000.

That's the deal a retiring owner hears. More money, faster close, and someone who'll look after the business they built. And the business's own cash flow pays for it.

*// LESSON · NOT-YOUR-FAULT · BELIEF SHIFT: WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING · TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**The lesson (3:05–8:50)**

First, something worth saying. If you've spent years thinking business ownership wasn't for you, it has nothing to do with your salary, your savings or your credit score. It has everything to do with a question nobody taught you to ask. Not "what's it worth?" but "what can it pay?"

You were told you need a deposit. You were told you need a track record. You were told the bank decides. That's not how most retiring owners sell. I was 25, unemployed, with no savings and no experience, when I bought my first business. No deposit. The seller was paid out of the business. So the old rules weren't rules. They were one way of doing it.

[SCREEN: "Three traps" · 1. The profit number · 2. Interest · 3. How the payments land]

Trap one. The profit number.

Every seller's number is a story. Some tell it high, some tell it low, most don't know it at all. So before you do any maths, you get three things: three years of financials, the matching tax returns, and revenue broken down by financial year. If the financials and the tax returns don't match, that's not a negotiation. That's a walk. We go deep on this in episode 21.

Then you normalise it. Add back the things the owner runs through the business that you won't. The personal car. The family member on payroll who never turns up. Then take out a proper wage for whoever runs the place after you buy it. If the owner works sixty hours a week and pays himself nothing, that profit isn't real. Someone has to do those sixty hours, and it costs money.

[SCREEN: Worked example, illustrative · Profit on the books $280,000 + owner's personal costs $20,000 − manager's wage $50,000 = $250,000 real profit]

And the reason this matters is simple. Skip the manager's wage and you'd use $300,000. Divide by 1.5, times ten, and you'd offer $2 million for a business that can only carry about $1.66 million. That's how people buy a job with a debt attached.

Trap two. Interest.

The $166,000 a year is everything you pay the seller. Principal and interest together. So the napkin $1.66 million is a ceiling, not a price.

[SCREEN: Illustrative · $166,000 a year for 10 years at 4% repays about $1.35M of principal · Education, not financial or legal advice]

At something like 4% over ten years, $166,000 a year pays off roughly $1.35 million. Still above the $750,000 to $1.25 million a cash buyer offers. So you're still the best offer on the table. You just know your real number before you open your mouth.

And the other $84,000 a year, the buffer, isn't spare money. It pays for the broken machine, the staff member who leaves, the slow month. Once the business proves itself, more of it becomes yours.

That's the point of the speed run. It's not your offer. It's your sanity check.

Trap three. How the payments actually land.

[B-ROLL: simple flow diagram, business → costs → manager → seller payment → buffer]

You set up a company to do the buying. That company signs an agreement with the seller: price, term, interest rate, how often you pay, and when the first payment is due. Then the business trades. Revenue comes in. It pays its costs. It pays the manager. It pays the seller. What's left is the buffer, and over time, yours.

You have four levers in that agreement. The term. The rate. A payment holiday before the first instalment. And sometimes a balloon.

My first deal was a physio clinic. 100% vendor finance, seven years at 4% principal and interest, a nine-month payment holiday, rent in arrears, and no deposit. Nine months of trading before the first payment. That's breathing room built into the paperwork, not borrowed. I pulled $150,000 out in year one.

[SCREEN: Results not typical; records on file]

My good friend Cam did two deals in 23 days, both 100% vendor finance. Interest-only for ten years, with balloons in years three and ten. Interest-only keeps the regular payments small. The balloon is the principal, due in a lump. That can work beautifully, but only if you've planned how you'll pay it. Cash you've built up, or a bank refinance once you've got a track record. Never sign a balloon you haven't planned for.

[SCREEN: Results not typical; records on file]

Now, how you say this to a seller. Never say "vendor finance". To a retiring owner it sounds like risk. Say "getting paid over time". Something like: "I can get you more than a cash buyer would, paid over time out of the business you built, and we can close in weeks, not months."

And the speed run tells you when to say no. When the profit's too thin to support you and the debt. Under about a million in value, it's rarely worth the effort. When the numbers don't match the tax returns. And when the seller needs every dollar on day one. That last one isn't always a dead end. You blend it with a bank, and that's episode 14.

So here's the order. First you find the real profit. Then, once you have it, you divide by 1.5 and set your ceiling. Once you know the ceiling, you pick your levers. And then you've got one sentence a seller can say yes to.

[SCREEN: 3C map. Credibility ✓ · CAPITAL lit · Closing · Grow · Exit · "Capital, episode 2 of 5"]

Back on the map. Capital, episode two of five. Last time you learned the speed run: profit, divide by 1.5, times the term. Today is the 1.5.

In the next few minutes I'll show you how to test any deal against its worst year before you sign. So you never have to fund a business from your own pocket, or explain a breach to a bank. All without a finance degree or a fifty-tab model.

This is for people who'll do the work and want to own businesses for the long run. If you're hoping to squeeze every last dollar of debt out of a deal, this episode will annoy you. Good.

**The recipe, fast (1:30–2:40)**

Three points.

One. DSCR. Debt service coverage ratio. The cash the business makes before debt, divided by every debt payment you owe in a year. Bank, seller, all of it. If the business makes $300,000 and your payments are $200,000, your DSCR is 1.5.

[SCREEN: DSCR = cash available for debt ÷ total annual debt payments · $300,000 ÷ $200,000 = 1.5× · Illustrative]

Two. 1.5 is the floor, not the target. For every dollar you owe, a dollar fifty comes in. That fifty cents pays for what goes wrong. A key person leaves. A big customer moves. Rates move. Something always happens in year one.

Three. Test the bad year before you sign. Build three versions of the numbers. A base case, a bear case where profit drops, and a bull case. Then ask one question: in the bad year, can the business still pay everyone and stay inside what the bank allows? If the answer is no, you change the structure. You don't change the spreadsheet.

Measure it, respect the floor, test the bad year. That's what Chad did.

*// LESSON · NOT-YOUR-FAULT · BELIEF SHIFT: THAT'S NOT HOW IT WORKS · TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**The lesson (2:55–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice · All numbers illustrative]

If you've looked at deals and felt you couldn't tell a safe one from a dangerous one, it has nothing to do with your intelligence or how long you've been investing. It has everything to do with what you're shown. Sellers and brokers show you the good year. Nobody hands you the bad one.

And most people think a deal is safe if it pays in an average year. They think a good price makes a good deal. They think a strong topline means strong cash. That's not how it works. A deal is safe if it still pays in its worst year. Once you see it that way, vetting a deal stops being a guess. It's a test, and you know how to run it.

Start with the top of the fraction. The cash available for debt. That's your normalised profit, after paying whoever runs the business. Same number as the speed run. If you got it wrong there, everything here is wrong too.

On the bottom: every debt payment in a year. Principal and interest. The bank loan, the seller note, any equipment finance. People forget the small ones. Don't.

Now, the bank. When a bank lends to you, it writes a covenant into the loan. A minimum DSCR you have to stay above, tested regularly. Every lender sets its own number, so ask. For this example, say it's 1.25. Drop below it and you're in breach, and a breach gives the lender rights you really don't want them to have over your business.

[SCREEN: Covenant headroom · How far can profit fall before you breach?]

So the real number to know is how far profit can fall before you breach. That's your headroom.

[SCREEN: Base case · Profit $300,000 · Debt payments $200,000 · DSCR 1.5×]

Base case. Profit $300,000. Payments $200,000. DSCR 1.5. Covenant 1.25. Profit can fall to $250,000 before you breach. That's about 17% of headroom.

[SCREEN: Bear case · Profit down 20% → $240,000 · DSCR 1.2× · Below a 1.25× covenant]

Bear case. Profit drops 20%. One big customer leaves, or a key person walks. Profit is $240,000. DSCR 1.2. You're in breach. And this deal was at 1.5. At the floor.

[SCREEN: Stretched deal · Payments $250,000 · Base 1.2× · Bear $240,000 ÷ $250,000 = 0.96×]

Now James's deal. He stretches. Payments of $250,000 on the same $300,000. Base DSCR 1.2. Looks fine on a good year. In the bear case, profit is $240,000 and the payments are $250,000. The business can't pay its own debt. He's topping it up from his pocket, or talking to the bank about why he can't.

That's what "keeps you alive" means. 1.5 is the minimum that gives you a chance in a bad year. It's not greed. It's survival.

And when the bear case fails, you change the structure, without bringing cash.

[SCREEN: Same $1.2M seller note at 4% · 7 years ≈ $200,000 a year · 10 years ≈ $148,000 a year · Illustrative]

Take a $1.2 million seller note at 4%. Over seven years, that's about $200,000 a year. Over ten, about $148,000. Same price. Same seller. On ten years, your base DSCR is about 2. Your bear case is about 1.6. You've bought yourself a bad year just by asking for a longer term.

Other levers: a payment holiday at the start, an interest-only period, part of the price as an earn-out, a lower price, or the seller rolling part of their value into shares instead of debt. That last one is episode 14.

Pick the bear case from the actual business, not a number you made up. What's the biggest customer worth? Take them out. Who's the one staff member everyone relies on? Price in replacing them. What did the worst of the last three years look like? That's your bear case. Then add the cost of taking over. A new system, a lawyer, a manager overlapping with the old owner. Put it in year one.

One more ratio banks look at: interest cover. Profit divided by just the interest. It matters most on interest-only loans, where the DSCR looks great until the principal comes due. Ask your lender which ratios they test, and how often. You want to know before you sign, not when the letter arrives.

Now, Daniel. Revenue doesn't keep a business alive. Cash does. Daniel Iles built Viral Coach to around $5 million a month. Early on, revenue went from $120,000 a month to about $700,000 in five months. Looks brilliant. But he was spending about one and a half times his revenue to get customers. He was losing ten to twenty thousand dollars a day, funded it with about $500,000 of unsecured credit card debt, and got down to $400 in the bank. In his words, he couldn't buy milk with confidence.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "How I went broke trying to get rich…" 7:46 · "Why I disappeared…" 4:31 · Not affiliated with Daniel Iles or Viral Coach]

The topline hid it. The rule he took from it: be cash-on-cash profitable every 30 days.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "You won't make 1M/mo until you learn this" 0:30 · "Be cash-on-cash profitable every 30 days"]

Here's how a buyer uses that. Once you own the business, every month you check one thing. Cash actually collected, minus costs, minus debt payments. Is it positive? Not invoiced revenue. Cash in the bank. Keep a reserve, and don't stack unsecured debt on top of a seller note to paper over a bad month.

So the order is this. First you normalise the profit. Then you build the base and the bear case. Once the bear case passes, you sign. And once you own it, you track DSCR monthly on the last twelve months, so if it slides from 1.6 to 1.4, you see it months before the bank does.

**The lesson (2:50–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice · All numbers illustrative]

If your first instinct was to find a partner with money, it has nothing to do with being naive or greedy or scared. It has everything to do with how buying a business gets explained. Everyone talks about raising money. Almost nobody shows you the bill at the exit.

And people will tell you that to get serious people around you, you have to give away a big chunk of the company. So look at mine. My board was found entirely through LinkedIn and cold outreach. The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder. And I still keep 73% of my company. My board does get equity. Founders' equity, earned by helping build the company. That's paying for credibility, not cash. Which means you can bring in serious people and keep control, at the same time.

[SCREEN: Josh's company · Board found via LinkedIn and cold outreach · Josh keeps 73%]

Now let's put a number on dilution. Round numbers, illustrative.

[SCREEN: Illustrative · Business profit $500,000 · Price $2M (4×)]

A business makes $500,000 profit. You buy it for $2 million, four times.

Route one, investors. They put up the money and take half the company.

Route two, debt. Seller note or bank, seven years at 4%. About $333,000 a year. DSCR 1.5. Right on the floor, and the business pays it off over seven years.

Now say you grow it. Profit gets to $1 million. You bolt it into a group and sell at eight times. $8 million.

[SCREEN: Exit $8M · Investor route: you keep 50% ≈ $4M · Debt route: debt repaid by the business, you keep it (less board equity)]

Investor route, you get half. About $4 million. The investors get the other $4 million. Debt route, the debt was paid by the business along the way. You keep the lot, less your board's equity. The lender's money cost you interest. The investor's money cost you $4 million, and they had a vote on when and how you sold.

That's debt over dilution.

Investor money is sometimes right. If the debt won't fit inside 1.5 however you structure it, and the deal's good enough, a small amount of equity can close the gap. But it's the last tool, not the first. Try the longer term, the holiday, the blend and the earn-out first. And if you do bring investors in, give them economics, not control. Drag-along, no veto, set up from the start.

Next, personal guarantees. A guarantee means if the business can't pay, the lender can come after you personally. On larger deals, over about $5 million, you often don't need one. An asset-rich business gives the bank its security. That's typically. Every lender and every deal is different.

[SCREEN: Personal guarantees · Over ~$5M: often not required · Asset-rich businesses give the bank security · Typically; every lender and deal is different]

On smaller deals, expect to be asked. Don't just sign. Ask what else would satisfy them. Tighter monthly reporting. A cash reserve requirement. Security over specific assets instead of everything. Get your lawyer to read exactly what you're guaranteeing. And buy through a company set up for that deal, so if it goes wrong, the damage stays inside that company. A personal guarantee is exactly the document that lets it out, which is why you read every line.

Third, working capital. Buying the business is one loan. Running it is another. You want a working capital line, approved before settlement and drawn on the day. It covers legal and accounting fees at close, which is how the deferred fees from episode 8 get paid. It covers integration. It gives you runway.

[SCREEN: Working capital line · Often around 10% of annual revenue · Approved before settlement · Drawn at close · Illustrative]

As a rough guide, it's often around 10% of annual revenue. On a business doing $3 million, that's about $300,000. My good friend Cam, on his two deals in 23 days, got $500,000 of working capital from the bank.

[SCREEN: Results not typical; records on file]

The word that matters is approved. Not promised, not "should be fine". If the line isn't approved and available on settlement day, you could be paying the lawyers out of your own pocket in week one.

Now, Daniel. Two cash lessons from Daniel Iles fit here.

The first: collect upfront. Viral Coach billed clients monthly. They switched to six-month terms paid upfront, and went from about $1.3 million to $3 million a month in roughly 30 days.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Focus on revenue" 2:01 · Not affiliated with Daniel Iles or Viral Coach]

You can't copy that into a plumbing business word for word. But you can ask where customers can pay before you deliver. Deposits. Prepaid service plans. Annual maintenance contracts. Every dollar that arrives before the cost does is working capital you don't have to borrow.

The second: the reserve. Daniel's jump from about $1.5 million to $3 million a month waited until he had savings on the sidelines. And you've heard where he was before that: half a million in card debt and $400 in the bank.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Why I disappeared…" 11:46 and 4:31]

So set a reserve floor. A number the business's cash never drops below, for example a few months of payroll and debt payments. No distributions below it. And never stack unsecured debt on top of a seller note to get through a bad month. That's how the $400 moment happens.

So here's Capital in order. First you work out what the business can pay. Then you test it at 1.5 against the bad year. Once it passes, you take it to the lenders on your bench, and blend with the seller if they want cash. And once the debt's agreed, you put the protections around it: the guarantee read line by line, the working capital approved, the reserve written down.
