### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Most people pick their company structure the week before settlement, in a hurry, with whatever their accountant has lying around. Then they spend deal five paying lawyers to undo it. The structure isn't paperwork. It decides who'll lend to you, and what a buyer will pay at the end.

[SCREEN: "Structure decides two things: who lends to you, and what the buyer pays."]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Grow" lit, "Exit" glowing. New label: "Act 13: build the group."]

New act. Scale: building the group, episode one of five. Last time we finished Act 12 on culture when the owner changes. You've got one business that's settled and running. Now we turn it into a group. First job: the structure it all sits in.

This one's for people who plan to own more than one business. If you'll only ever own one, a simpler setup may be fine, and your accountant can tell you. And a heads-up: this is education, not financial or legal advice. The rules change by country. Talk to your accountant and your lawyer before you set anything up.

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice. Talk to your accountant and lawyer.]

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Holding company on top. It's the permanent vehicle. Your board's equity sits there. You may own it through a family trust or your own company, depending on your advice.

Two. One deal, one company. Every business you buy goes into its own new company under the holdco. Its own bank account, its own lender, its own numbers.

Three. Build it for the lender and the buyer, not for you. The lender wants to see one business's cash flow paying one business's debt. The buyer wants clean pieces they can buy one at a time or all together. If your structure gives them that, you'll borrow more easily and sell for more.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's the shape. I help people do this, from a first deal with little to no money down through to a group someone will pay a premium for. If you want your structure planned before your next deal, book a call below. You'll talk to me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Episode 33 drew the picture. Today I want to explain why each line on that picture is there, because once you know why, you'll stop arguing with your lawyer about it.

There are four levels of structure, roughly.

[SCREEN: Four levels. 1 Partnership. 2 Single company. 3 Holdco + subsidiaries. 4 Holdco + arms ("octopus").]

Level one, a partnership. No real protection between you and the business. Don't buy a business this way.

Level two, one company. You own it, it owns the business. Simple, fine for one deal. The problem starts at deal two. If you buy the second business into the same company, both businesses now share one set of debts, one set of risks, one set of books.

Level three, a holding company with a company underneath for each business. This is where most groups should live.

Level four, the octopus. Under the holdco you've got arms, say one per region or one per industry, and each arm has its own businesses, its own manager, maybe its own lenders. Lose an arm, the animal lives. You won't need this for a while. But design level three so it can grow into level four without being torn down.

Now, why it matters for lending.

When you go to a bank for business two, they want to see a clear line: this business's profit pays this business's debt. If business two sits in its own company, the bank can lend to that company and look at its numbers alone. It also means the bank's security can sit over that company's assets, not over everything you own.

If it's all one company, the bank sees one pile. They'll want security over the whole pile. Now a problem in business two can reach business one.

Same with the seller. In a vendor-finance deal you want the seller's note sitting in the company that owns their old business, limited to that company where you can get it, and behind the bank. Your lawyer will use words like non-recourse and subordination. Ask them to explain both on your structure.

[SCREEN: Each company: its own bank account, its own lender, its own seller note. No cross-guarantees without your lawyer reading every word.]

One rule I'd push on hard. Don't sign a guarantee where one company stands behind another's debt unless your lawyer has read it and you understand it. That one clause can turn five ring-fenced businesses back into one pile.

Now, why it matters for the exit.

Here's a worked example. Round numbers, illustrative.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical.]

You own three businesses. Three hundred thousand profit, four hundred thousand, and three hundred thousand. A million in profit across the group.

A buyer comes. Maybe they want all three. Maybe they only want the biggest one. If each sits in its own company with its own clean books, you can say yes to either. If they're tangled together, the buyer's diligence team has to unpick three years of shared accounts before they'll make an offer, and every question they can't answer comes off the price.

And the multiple. Daniel Iles makes a point I like here. Multiples rise with size. He says a business making about a million in profit might sell around four times, and one making ten million might get six to eight, because a bigger business is more stable and has already solved more problems.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Why $10k per month is a dangerous place to be", 3:35. "~4× on $1M profit, 6–8× on $10M profit." Link in description.]

That's the whole reason for a group. A group of small businesses, reported cleanly as one, starts to look like a bigger, more stable business. Which is what a buyer pays more for. But only if the numbers are clean and comparable. The structure is how you keep them that way.

Two more things to set up at the start.

First, shares. When you set up the holdco, issue plenty of shares. A million, not a hundred. Your board's founders' equity sits at this level, and it's much easier to hand someone ten thousand out of a million than to reissue later.

Second, minority holders. If anyone else will own a slice, board members, a seller rolling over equity, a manager, set the rules from day one. Drag-along, so a minority can't block a sale of the group. No veto rights and no voting control for minorities. Put it in the shareholders' agreement before anyone signs.

[SCREEN: Shareholders' agreement: drag-along, no minority veto, transfer rules. Set up before the first share is issued.]

That's how I've done it. My board sits at the top, the founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder, and I keep 73% of my company.

[SCREEN: Josh's own company. Results not typical; records on file.]

[JOSH: one or two plain lines on how your healthcare group is structured, e.g. the holdco and how many operating companies sit under it, or cut this line.]

Last thing: money moving between companies. Groups often have the holdco charge each business a fee for real services it provides, like finance, HR and the board. It has to be for real services, at a fair price. How that's taxed depends on where you are. This is exactly the conversation to have with your accountant, not with me and not with a YouTube video.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

One. Draw what you own today on one page. Every company, every owner, every loan, every guarantee. If you can't draw it, that's the first finding.

Two. Book an hour with your accountant and your lawyer together. One question: "If I buy three more businesses in the next three years, what should this look like?"

Three. Find every guarantee you've signed. Personal, and company to company. List them.

Four. Check your holdco's share count and your shareholders' agreement. If there's no drag-along, put it on your lawyer's list.

## Episode 62 · Shared services: run the back office once

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Shared services: run the back office once for every business
  2. Three businesses, one back office
  3. What to centralise in a group (and what never to touch)
- **Thumbnail text:** ONE BACK OFFICE
- **Runtime target:** 11:30 (≈1,750 words)
- **Stage lit:** Grow → Exit (Act 13, Scale: build the group, episode 2 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

The cheapest way to add profit to a group isn't selling more. It's stopping paying for the same thing three times. Three bookkeepers, three payroll systems, three insurance brokers, three accounting packages. The customers never see any of it. That's exactly why you can fix it.

[SCREEN: "3 bookkeepers. 3 payroll systems. 3 brokers. Customers see none of it."]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Grow" lit. Act 13 label.]

Building the group, episode two of five. Last time: the holdco, one deal, one company. Today we put a shared back office on top of those companies so the group costs less to run than its parts.

This is for people who own two or more businesses, or are about to. If you own one, the ideas still help you tidy it up before a sale. If you're hoping a back office will fix a business that doesn't make money, it won't. Fix the business first.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Centralise what customers never feel. Bookkeeping, payroll, HR admin, insurance, legal, compliance, software, buying. Same job in every business, so do it once.

Two. Leave alone what customers do feel. The person who answers the phone, the person on the tools, the clinician, the local name on the van. That's what you bought. Don't centralise it to save a few dollars.

Three. Move in order, one function at a time, and let the people who use a tool help pick it. Books first, because you can't manage a group whose numbers come in five different shapes. Then payroll and HR. Then insurance and buying. Marketing last, and carefully.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's it in three. I help people build groups like this, starting with a first business bought with little to no money down. If you've got two or three businesses and the back office is a mess, book a call below. You'll talk to me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Daniel Iles has a simple way of splitting any business. Every role either gets clients, keeps clients, or keeps the other two out of jail. That last bucket is ops: legal, finance, HR, support.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "How to build a 100-person team without killing profit", 0:00. Get / Keep / Jail.]

In a group, get and keep stay local. Every business keeps its own customers, its own service, its own team. The jail bucket is the one that moves up to the holding company and serves everyone.

Let's go through it in order.

First, the books. One accounting system for the whole group. One chart of accounts, so wages are wages and rent is rent in every business, with the same names. One person, or one firm, closing the month for all of them. Why first? Because the Monday numbers from episode 30 only mean something if every business reports them the same way. Until then, you're comparing apples with invoices.

Second, payroll and HR. One payroll system. One set of employment contracts and policies, adjusted where the law or the industry needs it. One person who knows the awards or the employment rules in your sector. This is also where staff notice a change, so tell them what's changing and what isn't before you do it. Episode 60 matters here.

Third, insurance and buying. One broker for the group. Group buying on the things every business uses: fuel, consumables, phones, software seats. Suppliers give better terms to a bigger customer, and now you are one.

Fourth, marketing. Careful with this one. A central engine can work well: one person who runs the ads, the reviews, the reporting, for every business. But the customer still sees the local name. Don't rename a forty-year-old local business after your holdco because it looks tidy on a slide.

Now tools. This is where people waste the most money.

Daniel did a video walking through the sixty-four tools behind his business. Two lessons from it fit a group perfectly.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "These 64 tools are the secret to my $3M per month business", 3:05 and 5:09. Links in description.]

One, for payroll and HR he picked his system after looking at about twenty options. And the books are reconciled in an accounting package and sent to his accountant.

Two, he let the team that uses a tool choose it. His account managers tested two setups for about six months and picked the one they wanted.

And the warning. In another video he says he spent around a hundred thousand dollars over six months moving to a big-name CRM because his peers used it. His sales team hated it. He rolled back within fourteen hours.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "I lost millions learning these harsh business lessons", 0:00. "Don't copy your friends' tech stack."]

So for your group: don't force the fanciest system on a twelve-person plumbing business because it looked good in a demo. Pick tools that fit how the work is actually done. Test with the people who'll use them. Then roll out one at a time.

Daniel also asks a good question before any new software: does the time to set it up beat just doing more of what already works?

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "What did I learn making $3M in one month? (Q&A)", 5:40.]

Now how the holdco pays for all this. Here's a worked example. Round numbers, illustrative.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Education, not financial or legal advice.]

Three businesses. Revenue of four million, three million and one million. Eight million in total.

The shared team at the holdco costs two hundred and forty thousand a year. A controller, a bookkeeper, and a payroll and HR person.

A common way to split that is by revenue. Half the group's revenue is business one, so it carries half: a hundred and twenty thousand. Business two, three-eighths: ninety thousand. Business three, an eighth: thirty thousand.

[SCREEN: $240K shared team ÷ revenue share → $120K / $90K / $30K.]

Before, each business was paying its own people and firms for the same work. Say a hundred and forty, a hundred, and fifty. Two hundred and ninety in total. Now it's two hundred and forty. Fifty thousand a year back in profit, plus one set of numbers you can trust.

Groups often do this through a management fee the holdco charges each business. It has to cover real services, at a fair price, and how it's taxed depends on where you are. Talk to your accountant and lawyer before you set one up.

[SCREEN: Management fees: real services, fair price. Talk to your accountant and lawyer.]

And notice it's not a huge saving on day one. That's normal. The bigger win comes at business four and five, when the same team can absorb another business for a small extra cost. And at the exit, when the buyer sees one finance function, one set of books and one set of policies, instead of five little kingdoms.

What not to do. Don't centralise before you've got at least two businesses running steadily. Don't move all four functions in the same month. And don't take the one competent office manager out of a business without a plan for the hundred small things they did that nobody wrote down.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

One. List every back-office cost in each business: bookkeeping, accounting, payroll, HR, insurance, software. Put them in one table, side by side.

Two. Count the tools. How many accounting packages, payroll systems and logins do you have across the group? Write the number down.

Three. Pick the first function to share. For most groups, it's the books.

Four. Ask the person who'd use the new tool to test two options with you before you buy anything.

**The turn (9:00–9:30)**

You can do all of this alone. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. Here, the mistakes are expensive and slow. They centralise everything at once and the businesses stop working for a month. They buy software because a friend uses it. They strip out the local touch customers were paying for. ## Episode 63 · Bolt-ons: small deals that raise the group's value

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Bolt-ons: small deals that raise the whole group's value
  2. Why the best acquisitions are often the small ones
  3. Platform and bolt-on: how groups really grow
- **Thumbnail text:** SMALL DEALS, BIG GROUP
- **Runtime target:** 11:30 (≈1,750 words)
- **Stage lit:** Grow → Exit (Act 13, Scale: build the group, episode 3 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Everyone wants the big deal. But a lot of a group's value is built with small, boring ones. A business making a hundred and fifty thousand a year, owner retiring, no successor, nobody else bidding. On its own it's a job. Bolted onto a group, it can be worth far more than you paid.

[SCREEN: "Small on its own. Valuable inside a group."]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Grow" lit, arrow looping back to "Closing".]

Building the group, episode three of five. So far: the holdco, then a shared back office. Today we use both, by adding small businesses to the group. In the trade they're called bolt-ons, or add-ons.

This one's for people who own at least one solid business, the platform, and want to grow it into a group. If you haven't bought business one yet, watch this for where it goes, then start with episode one. And if you're thinking of collecting tiny businesses for the sake of a count, this isn't that.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Platform first. A platform is the business everything else plugs into. It has a manager who isn't you, clean numbers, and a back office that can take more work. No platform, no bolt-ons.

Two. Bolt-ons are small, cheap and close. Small enough that most buyers ignore them. Cheap because small businesses usually sell at lower multiples. Close, meaning same industry and ideally same region, so the platform can absorb them.

Three. The value comes from two places. Costs the bolt-on no longer needs once it's inside the group. And the higher multiple a bigger, cleaner group can earn at exit. You price the deal on the small business as it stands today, never on what it's worth once it's inside your group.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's the model. I help people buy businesses with little to no money down, and then build them into groups like this. If you've got a platform and want a bolt-on plan, book a call below. You'll talk to me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Let's start with why this works at all.

In the masterclass I teach the roll-up as multiple arbitrage. Buy businesses at around four times profit. Combine them, grow them. Sell the group for eight, ten, sometimes more. Episode 31 covered it.

[SCREEN: Buy ~4× → combine and grow → group sells for more. Illustrative. Results not typical.]

Bolt-ons are how you do that one small step at a time.

Daniel Iles gives the reason buyers pay more for bigger businesses. He says a business making about a million in profit might sell around four times, and one making ten million might get six to eight, because it's more stable and has solved more problems.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Why $10k per month is a dangerous place to be", 3:35. Link in description.]
