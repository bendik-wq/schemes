## 5 board equity mistakes that give away 40% before your first deal

Four board seats at 10% each is 40% of your company gone before you've bought a single business.

There are two types of owners building a group. The ones who spend equity like currency, and the ones who sit on 100% of one business for twenty years.

I pay my board in equity and keep 73%. Here are the five mistakes that stop people doing the same.

Equity is the one thing you can't easily take back. Give too much and there's nothing left for you, the next hire or the exit. Give too little and nobody good says yes.

This is for owners building something to scale or sell. If you want one business you run on your own, you don't need a board, and this isn't for you.

Small slices, earned on a deal, with the job written down.

If you're stuck at one business, it has nothing to do with your work ethic or your industry. It has everything to do with never being shown how to pay people before you have cash.

Most owners think a bigger percentage is a safer percentage. That's not how it works.

Equity is the currency you spend before you have cash. Five ways people spend it badly, ranked from cheapest to most expensive.

If you want me to look at your split before you offer it to anyone, book a call below. Now the detail.

**Mistake 1. Too few shares.**

People set up the holding company with 100 shares. Then someone needs 7.5% and it's a restructure.

Issue a million at the start. Handing someone 70 thousand shares out of a million is easy.

The holding company owns the group. Each business you buy sits in its own company underneath, so each deal's debt stays in its own box.

And the board owns a slice of the whole group, not one clinic.

It also protects your number later. When a seller keeps a stake in the business they sell you, that stake usually sits in the company that owns that one business.

So a seller's rollover doesn't dilute you or the board at the top.

**Mistake 2. Same slice for every seat.**

Founders' equity runs 2 to 10% per person.

Where each seat lands depends on three things. How much their name moves a bank or a seller. How many hours they'll give you. And how hard they'd be to replace.

The chair usually wins on the first, so the chair goes higher.

Weigh the work, not the title. A sector operator on seller calls every week can earn more than a lawyer who reviews two documents a quarter.

And leave headroom. Keep yourself at 70% or more after the board, so there's room for a group CEO later, or another seat when you move into a second industry.

Four seats at 10% leaves you 60% and nowhere to go. Four seats between 6 and 8% leaves you 73% and room to grow.

**Mistake 3. Vesting at the handshake.**

Nothing vests on a handshake. A portion vests when you sign the sale agreement on your first deal, the SPA. The rest vests over the 12 months after closing.

Leave early, and the unvested shares come back. Your lawyer calls these leaver terms.

If they don't help you get a deal done, they don't own anything. That one rule filters out people who only wanted a title.

It's also fair to the good ones: they know exactly what earns their shares, and when.

**Mistake 4. No written job.**

A board seat is a senior hire paid in equity. Set the job before they say yes, not after.

Put it in a one or two page letter per person. Role, percentage, vesting, monthly commitments, leaver terms, confidentiality.

Signed before their name goes on your executive summary. If someone won't sign a page that fair, you've learned something cheaply.

**Mistake 5. Giving away the exit.**

This is the expensive one. Minority holders get no veto and no voting control.

Add a drag-along, so if you sell the group they sell with you on the same terms. Add transfer restrictions, so nobody sells to a stranger.

Set it up from day one, not when there's a problem. A minority holder with a veto can block a sale years later.

Good board members will ask about one more thing: directors' and officers' insurance. Have it. It protects them, and it tells them you're serious.

**[JOSH: one line on how you set control terms with your own board.]**

**The split.**

Issue a million shares. You keep 730 thousand, 73%. The chair gets 8%, the finance lead 7%, the legal lead and the sector operator 6% each.

Now what 7% is worth. Five businesses making $500 thousand profit each is $2.5 million of profit. Bought at about four times, around $10 million. Sold as a group at eight times, $20 million. 7% of that is $1.4 million to the finance lead.

That's your pitch to a former CFO. Not "help me out". A ground-floor stake in something worth a lot if you build it. And you still hold 73%.

If giving away 27% feels like losing, run it the other way. 27% of a $20 million group is real money for them. 73% of it is real money for you.

100% of one business capped at your own hours is a ceiling. And none of their 27% exists unless a deal gets done.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Promising "a piece" on a handshake that two people remember differently. That ends in a lawyer's letter, not a deal.

Two. Starting at 60% after the board. There's no room left for the people who'll actually grow the group.

Three. Haggling percentages on the first call. The best people walk, and you're left with the ones who only wanted a title.

Or you can keep doing what you're doing. Keep 100% of a business that only grows as fast as you work. Keep waiting for cash before you buy the second one.

## $70K of deal fees: pay upfront vs pay at settlement

Owners who can read a P&L better than most buyers still don't buy a second business, because of one question: who pays the lawyers?

On a $3 million deal that's about $70 thousand of legal and accounting fees. Most people pull it out of the business they already own.

You don't have to. Here's the version where the deal pays its own costs.

$70 thousand out of your current business is a buffer gone, a hire you don't make, or a slow month you can't survive. Do it on a deal that dies and it's gone.

This is for people buying real businesses, usually a million dollars or more in value. If you're buying a small online store off a marketplace, skip this one.

Two ways to pay your deal team. Option A: pay as you go, from your own cash. Option B: pay at settlement, from money the bank lends the business on the day.

If you've stalled here, it has nothing to do with being cheap or scared. It has everything to do with nobody telling you fees can sit at settlement.

I'll cost both, give you a test, and a verdict.

Getting good firms to wait for their money comes down to how you pitch it. I help people with that. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Option A. Pay as you go.**

You engage a firm on hourly terms. They bill monthly. You pay from your current business.

What it costs: the full bill, upfront, whether the deal closes or not.

And open hourly terms invite fee-jacking. Every small point becomes a memo, and the bill doubles.

What it gets you: control over the firm, and nothing owed if you walk. That's it.

**Option B. Pay at settlement.**

Three moves.

One. Build the bench first. An M&A lawyer. An accountant who can do a quality of earnings review, which is a check that the profit is real.

Big Four or a strong regional firm. And an industry adviser if the sector has rules you don't know.

Two. Ask for deferred or rolled fees. Some or all of the bill is paid at settlement instead of as they go.

Three. At settlement, the bank funds a working capital line into the new company. As a rough guide it's often around 10% of annual revenue.

It pays the firms that afternoon, and the rest is runway.

**Why a good firm says yes.**

You're not one deal. You're a group buying business after business.

To a law firm that's a repeat client. To an accountant it's years of work: diligence, then the books, tax and audits for every business you add. You pitch the pipeline, not the job.

And top-tier matters for the same reason the board does. A bank trusts a quality of earnings report from a firm it knows.

A cheap firm nobody's heard of costs you more in delay than it saves in fees.

A proper quality of earnings review checks five things.

One. Whether revenue will keep coming. Two. Whether one or two customers carry too much of it. Three. Whether the seller's adjustments to profit are fair.

Four. How much working capital the business really needs. Five. Whether the cash flow holds once you own it.

That's the report your bank leans on to say yes.

**Where to find them.**

Ask your board's legal and finance leads who they'd use. Then LinkedIn, partners who list acquisition work. Then ask your lenders which firms' reports they trust.

Some firms will say no. Some defer half. Some do the early work at a fixed fee and the heavy work at settlement. Ask five.

**The proof.**

"A bank won't do that for me."

My good friend Cam did two deals in 23 days with 100% vendor finance, and $500 thousand of working capital from the bank. Not from his pocket. From the deal.

And Jy Kingston, one of my mentees, had accountants and lawyers on board within about two weeks of starting.

**Settlement day.**

A business doing $3 million in revenue and $500 thousand in profit. Legal fees, $40 thousand. Accounting and the quality of earnings review, $30 thousand.

The bank's working capital line at about 10% of revenue is $300 thousand. The $70 thousand of fees comes out of it, and $230 thousand is left in the new company.

Option A: $70 thousand out of your pocket before you know the deal closes.

Option B: $0 out of your pocket, fees paid on the day, $230 thousand left to run the business. The bank sets the size of the line, not you.

**The test and the verdict.**

The self-test. Before you spend a dollar on lawyers, has the seller sent three years of financials, matching tax returns and revenue by financial year?

If not, they're not serious, and neither option is worth paying for yet.

The verdict: Option B, in this order. Board, then bench, then deals.

Fixed fees per stage, deferred to settlement, with a capped amount if it dies. Aim for about four weeks to paper the contracts.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Hiring a cheap firm the bank doesn't trust. The saving comes back as weeks of delay and a tougher credit team.

Two. Signing open hourly terms. No scope, no cap, and the bill doubles.

The fix is leadership: a tight scope, a timeline, and a ranked issues list so the big points get solved first.

Three. Spending the working capital line on yourself. It's runway for the business. Spend it and you're back at the bank in six months with a worse story.

## Every partner setup from 50/50 handshake to bankable, explained

Buying a business with a partner doubles your hours. Done on a handshake, it can also halve your equity.

It goes from sharing the work to arguing about the split. From one plan to two. From a mate to a lawyer's letter. And it usually happens when the money shows up, not before.

Here's every level from a 50/50 handshake to a setup a bank and a seller will back.

In years one to three you're paying the seller and the bank, settling staff in, working long hours. Later the debt's paid down and the business throws off cash.

That's when an unwritten split starts to cost real money, and you can't renegotiate it calmly once there's money on the table.

If you're buying alone, stay anyway. This applies to anyone who'll ever own a piece of what you build.

A partner should bring something you can't borrow. Four levels. Most pairs are stuck on level one.

When partnerships blow up, it usually has nothing to do with one of you being greedy or lazy. It has everything to do with terms written for year one still being used in year five.

Before you know that, a partnership feels like a question of trust. After you know it, you see it's a question of conditions.

Conditions change. The paper has to change with them.

I help co-buyers set this up before a seller and a bank are asking who's in charge. If you're buying with someone, book a call below and bring them. Now the detail.

**Level 1. The handshake.**

Two people, 50/50, nothing written, often trading as a partnership.

The trap: a partnership gives you no protection between the partners and the business's liabilities. A 50/50 split with no tie-break means every disagreement is a deadlock.

And the bank will ask who owns it and who signs, and you won't have one answer.

The one move to level two: decide whether you need a partner at all. There are three good reasons.

One. Hours, because this takes 40 to 100 a week.

Two. Assets, because cash, an existing business or property makes it easier and faster.

Three. Skills you don't have.

No to all three? Buy alone. You can borrow credibility from a board without giving anyone a co-owner's share.

**Level 2. A company and separate jobs.**

Use a company, not a partnership. Then split the jobs so they don't overlap.

There are three. Deals: sourcing, seller calls, offers. Operations: running what you buy, staff, customers. Capital: lenders, lawyers, numbers.

Two of you can split three jobs. Two of you can't both own "deals".

Each person has final say in their own lane: the operator on staff, the deal-finder on which seller to chase. Nobody relitigates the other's area.

For anything that crosses lanes, write a deadlock rule now: who breaks the tie, or who you bring in to break it.

A 50/50 with no tie-break isn't equal. It's stuck.

**Level 3. Shares that match the work.**

Each person's shares vest on their role. The deal-finder's on signing the first SPA. The operator's over the first 12 months of running it.

Write in a date each year to review the terms, because conditions will change. One of you may drop to part-time. One may put in more cash.

The review date is when you adjust, calmly, instead of in the middle of a fight.

And a partner doesn't replace the board. A partner is a co-owner with a full-time job in the business.

A board member is a few hours a month, a name a bank trusts, and a small slice that vests on a deal.

Two co-buyers with no board are still two people with no track record.

**Level 4. Bankable: signed before the LOI.**

A shareholders' agreement with five things. Transfer restrictions. Pre-emptive rights. Drag-along and tag-along. A deadlock rule. Exit terms.

Inside it, the buy-sell clause: if one of you leaves, gets sick or dies, who buys the shares, at what price, worked out how, paid over how long.

Agree the formula, not the number.

Then personal guarantees. If a lender wants one, someone signs.

Larger deals, typically over about $5 million, often don't need one, because the business's assets give the bank its security.

Every lender and deal is different. Agree now who signs and how you share that risk.

And it all goes before the LOI. Once a seller signs, you're in 60 to 90 days of exclusivity.

The seller wants to know who'll run it. The bank wants to know who owns it and who signs. You don't want to negotiate with your partner and the seller in the same week.

At level one, that split sits on a handshake. At level four, it's the same split, vested on role, with a buy-sell at last year's profit times an agreed multiple, a deadlock rule and an annual review.

The split is identical. The difference is what happens when one of them stops working in year four.

Level one can cost you the business. Level four costs a few hours.

**Where people lose money.**

Two. Both wanting the same job. One of you is paid for work the other is doing.

Three. Leaving personal guarantees unspoken until the bank asks. That's the worst week to find out you disagree.
