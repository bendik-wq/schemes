## The 2-hour board meeting for a 5-business group (copy it)

There's a well-kept secret among owners who run groups of businesses.

Their board meetings aren't for updates. They're for decisions.

And one decision, made on time, can be worth $120 thousand a year in profit, and close to $1 million at exit.

Here's the whole meeting, in the order your board sees it. Copy it.

Your board is why sellers and banks took you seriously in the first place. After settlement, the same people should be making you money.

Board members on founders' equity, typically 2 to 10 percent each, give you a few hours a month.

Spend those hours reading them numbers they could have read at home and you've paid equity for nothing.

This is for owners building a group to sell. It's not for anyone who'd rather nobody looked at the numbers.

The whole idea in one line: a board meeting exists to make decisions. If it could have been an email, it wasn't a board meeting.

There are six parts, in this order.

The pre-read pack. Last month's decisions. The numbers. The decision papers. Capital and deals. The decision list.

You were told a board is a formality for big companies. Compliance and minutes. You were told small businesses don't need one.

For a group that borrows money and plans to sell, none of that is true. A working board is how several businesses become something a bigger buyer wants.

And if your meetings drift, it has nothing to do with your board's talent or your charisma.

It has everything to do with a meeting that was never designed to decide anything.

**The pre-read pack.**

The pack goes out at least two days before the meeting. One page per business, one page for the group.

Same numbers, same order, every month, so the trends jump out.

Per business: revenue against budget, gross margin, profit, cash, debtor days, the one sales number that drives it, staff turnover, and the GM's one problem.

For the group: total profit, debt service cover against the 1.5 floor, cash against your reserve floor, the deal pipeline, and lender covenants.

Every number has a named owner who explains it. If nobody owns it, nobody fixes it.

And send the pack to your GMs too. If they can see what the board sees, they fix things before the board asks.

**Last month's decisions.**

Ten minutes. The meeting opens with the list from last time. Done, not done, why.

Anything that's slipped past two meetings gets discussed on its own. That's what stops "let's look at it next month" becoming the culture.

**The numbers.**

Twenty minutes. No walk-through. The board has read the pack.

Questions only, and only on what's off budget or trending the wrong way. A site that's on plan gets no airtime. That's the reward.

The question a good board asks more than any other: what do you expect to happen, and how do you know?

**The decision papers.**

Forty minutes, one or two decisions. Each comes with a one-page paper: the options, the numbers, your recommendation.

**Here's one.**

Five clinics, each booking 400 appointments a month at $100. That's $200 thousand a month for the group.

The paper proposes two moves. Price up 5 percent at all five: $10 thousand a month.

Fill two empty practitioner days at the two busiest sites: another $10 thousand.

Each move has an owner, a date, and the number that shows it's working.

"Grow 20 percent" isn't a decision. That is.

Agree who decides what before the meeting. The operator decides operations. The finance lead decides how the debt is structured.

You break ties as the owner, and the minutes record who decided.

**Capital and deals.**

Thirty minutes. Pipeline, lenders, covenant headroom. Whether to sign the LOI on business six.

This is where your finance lead earns their equity.

Then ten minutes on risk: what could hurt the group in the next 90 days.

The board also sets the scorecard for every senior hire before the search starts. Never let a new GM write their own.

The person being measured shouldn't choose the ruler.

**The decision list.**

Ten minutes. Decision, owner, date. Read it out before anyone leaves.

That list is item one next month.

When people like that give you two hours, the meeting has to be worth it.

## 5 things I'd never sign to buy a business (1.5× DSCR first)

This one isn't for most people. If "no money down" sounds like "no risk" to you, it isn't for you.

No money down is a capital strategy, not a risk strategy.

Deals that blow up rarely blow up on price. They blow up on something someone signed.

Here are five things I'd never sign, ranked from expensive to the one that costs you the exit.

When you buy with seller finance and bank debt, your equity is the thin layer on top.

A 20 percent dip in profit doesn't cost you 20 percent. It can cost you the business, because the repayments don't dip with it.

This is for owners and capital deployers building something big with borrowed money.

It's not for anyone who'll sign whatever's put in front of them to get a deal done.

The whole idea in one line: write your risk rules on a calm day, because on a bad day you'll talk yourself into anything.

There are five lines. When an owner loses a business this way, it has nothing to do with being reckless or greedy.

It has everything to do with having no written rules before the pressure arrived.

Most people think nothing-down deals are fragile by design. Look at Cam, a good friend of mine.

Two deals in 23 days, 100 percent vendor finance, and $500 thousand of working capital from the bank.

Nothing down, with a cushion built in from day one. Structured right, those two go together.

I help owners structure deals the business can actually carry. Book a call below. Now the list.

**Mistake 1: a deal below 1.5 times.**

DSCR is free cash flow divided by debt repayments. The floor is 1.5.

A business throws off $300 thousand of free cash flow. Divide by 1.5, and the most it should pay in debt service is $200 thousand a year.

The other $100 thousand is your buffer.

**Now the bear case.**

Profit drops 20 percent. $300 thousand becomes $240 thousand. Against $200 thousand of repayments, that's 1.2. Tight, but you're paying.

Close at 1.2 instead, and the same dip puts you under 1. You're funding the bank from your own pocket.

**The rule.**

No deal closes below 1.5 in the base case. Check each business on its own, and the group as a whole.

A strong business can hide a weak one in the group total, and the bank will find it before you do.

**Mistake 2: settling with no cushion.**

Being undercapitalised at close is one of the most common ways a good deal goes bad. Wages, a slow debtor and a broken machine, all in month two.

Write a reserve floor. Months of fixed costs plus debt repayments, held in cash or an undrawn facility, that you never go below.

The right number depends on your business and your lender. The point is that it's written, and it's on the board pack every month.

Then put a stop-loss on every new bet. A new site or service line gets a budget and a kill line before it starts.

$50 thousand. If it isn't paying back by month four, we stop. Illustrative, and written in the minutes.

**Mistake 3: a personal guarantee by default.**

On larger deals, often over about $5 million, an asset-rich business can give the bank its security, and a personal guarantee often isn't needed.

Typically. Every lender and deal is different.

On smaller deals you may have to sign one. Then you negotiate it.

Cap the amount. Put a time limit on it. Have it fall away when the debt drops to a set level.

Never sign the default. The first draft is the bank's opening position, not its last.

**Mistake 4: unsecured personal debt.**

Credit cards, personal loans, anything that can follow you home. It's fast, which is the problem.

It lets a business that's bleeding look like one that's growing, and it's the dearest money there is.

Never stack it on top of a seller note. You'd be paying the seller from the business and the bank from your house.

Your personal costs should be covered before you sign. Savings, a partner's income, or a salary the business can carry.

If your rent depends on the business having a good month, your risk is personal, not commercial. That's when people sign everything on this list.

**Mistake 5: equity sold to plug a cash gap.**

This is the most expensive line, and it feels like the safest. Selling equity in a panic has no repayments, so it looks like the low-risk option.

It isn't. Debt gets paid off. Equity is forever.

Sell 10 percent to cover a bad quarter, and if the group later sells for $16 million, that bad quarter cost you $1.6 million.

Maybe the control over when and to whom you sell, too. A new investor who came in on a bad day has every reason to want a say on the good one.

Debt over dilution, inside the 1.5 rule. It's why I keep 73 percent of my company.

**[JOSH: your own reserve floor or the one line you won't cross, and why; any document you've refused to sign, without names.]**

At 1.5, a 20 percent dip leaves you at 1.2. Close at 1.2, with $250 thousand of repayments, and the same dip leaves you at 0.96. That's why the floor is 1.5.

And 10 percent sold in a cash gap is $1.6 million handed over at a $16 million exit. That's why you cover a gap with debt you can carry, not equity you can never buy back.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Running only the base case. The base case never arrives on time.

Two. Counting property or equipment as the reserve. It doesn't pay this Friday's wages.

Three. Hiring fixed overhead ahead of proven revenue. Payroll doesn't fall when sales do.

Learn these lines alone, usually right after you've crossed one. Or have someone check every structure before you sign it, in the exact moment you want to break a rule.

## 50/50 handshake vs 4 clauses: how partnerships break when the money arrives

There are only two types of business partnerships.

The ones that wrote down what happens when the money arrives, and the ones that break when it does.

Partnerships rarely break when things are hard. They break when things start working.

On a $16 million exit, four clauses decide who gets what.

Here's the handshake against the agreement, and a test to tell you which one you've actually got.

In the 3C model you'll have more partners than you think.

Co-buyers. Board members on founders' equity. Sellers who roll equity into the new company. Each one owns a slice of the exit.

If the paperwork doesn't say what happens when someone stops, leaves or disagrees, the exit is when you find out.

That's the worst possible day to negotiate.

This is for anyone buying with a co-buyer, building a board on equity, or taking a seller's rollover.

It's not for anyone hoping a handshake will hold for ten years.

The whole idea in one line: contribution changes over time, so the terms have to be able to change too.

Two options. Option A is the handshake. Fifty-fifty, goodwill, sort it out later.

Option B is four written clauses: roles and vesting, deadlock, buy-sell, and minority exit. Plus one trigger.

When a partnership breaks, it has nothing to do with bad people, or greed, or one partner getting lazy.

It has everything to do with terms written for year one that nobody touched again.

I help owners structure this from the start, so the group survives the money arriving. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Option A. The handshake.**

People choose it because it's fast, it's friendly, and writing things down feels like you don't trust each other.

**Here's how it plays out.**

Two co-buyers, fifty-fifty, buy a business making $400 thousand. One runs it full time. The other finds the next deal.

By year three the seller note's paid down, the business throws off cash, and the deal-finder has taken a job elsewhere.

Same fifty-fifty. Completely different contribution.

The operator is doing all the work for half the profit, and resentment does the rest.

Now add a board member given 5 percent on a handshake, who stopped turning up after six months. And a seller who rolled in with a veto.

The group grows to $16 million, a buyer wants 100 percent, and three people can each slow the deal down.

Most people think trust is enough. They think writing it down means you don't trust each other.

That's not how it works. Writing it down is what lets you keep trusting each other in year five.

**Option B. The four clauses.**

Your lawyer drafts these. You decide what goes in.

**One. Roles and vesting.**

Who does what, how many hours, and what happens if someone stops.

Only partner where the jobs don't overlap. One person finds and finances deals, the other runs the businesses. Two people doing the same job is one too many.

Equity vests against contribution. For board members, that's founders' equity, 2 to 10 percent, vesting from the signed purchase agreement.

**Two. Deadlock.**

Agree decision rights in advance. The deal person decides on structures. The operator decides on operations.

Then a formal last resort, which your lawyer sets out.

**Three. Buy-sell.**

If a partner wants out, dies, divorces or stops contributing, how is their share valued, who can buy it, and how is it paid for?

Agree the formula now, while nobody knows which side of it they'll be on.

**Four. Minority holders on exit.**

Drag-along, so a minority can't block a sale of the group. No veto and no voting control for small holders. Set up from the start.

**Then the trigger.**

The terms reopen when the seller note is paid off, or when anyone's hours drop below an agreed level.

Maybe the operator gets a salary first, then profit splits. Maybe the partner who stepped back sells some shares under the buy-sell formula.

The conversation is already in the contract, not left to resentment.

**Sellers are partners too.**

Any seller who rolls equity into the new company is a partner who used to be the boss.

My friend Jason Nagy's first deal was a $5.5 million radiology business, zero down, on a sixty-forty structure.

Seven years later he sold to private equity for $200 million.

At that end of the market, the buyer wants a clean shareholder register. So the same rules apply to the seller.

Their role after settlement. What they can and can't vote on. And drag-along, so they come with you when the group sells.

It's also why I care about control. I keep 73 percent of my company.

Equity can be shared. Control should sit where the decisions get made.
