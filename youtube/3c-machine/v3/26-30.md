## 5 mistakes that sink a $1M vendor-finance deal in the first 100 days

Two new owners buy the same $2 million business on the same seller note.

James restructures in week two. By month three his best supervisor has gone, a big customer has followed, and the first seller payment is due on profit that no longer exists.

Chad changes nothing big for a month. He makes every payment.

Same business. Same debt. The difference is five mistakes. Here they are, ranked, with what each one costs.

**Why the first 100 days matter.**

In a 3C deal the seller and the bank are paid from the business. Your whole structure rests on one ratio: free cash flow covering debt payments at least 1.5 times.

That's DSCR, debt service cover. Break the cash flow in the first 100 days and you break the structure, before you've pulled a single growth lever.

Keep one number in your head. A business doing $250 thousand profit can carry about $166 thousand a year to the seller at that cover, and it leaves less room than it sounds.

This is for people who've bought, or are about to, and plan to own for years. If you want to buy a job and coast, skip this one.

The first 100 days have one job: keep the machine you paid for running long enough to understand it.

Most new owners think day one is where you make your mark. They think the new boss has to look decisive. They think change is what they bought.

That's not how it works. You bought cash flow that already exists. Your first job is to not interrupt it.

And if you came out of a corporate job, the urge to change everything isn't your fault. It has nothing to do with your judgement or your ambition.

It has everything to do with years of being rewarded for visible change. In a business paying off its own purchase price, visible change is the risk.

I help owners plan this before settlement, so the structure and the first 100 days fit together. Book a call below. Now the five.

**Mistake 1. Restructuring before you measure.**

Week two, new org chart. Week three, new software. Every change you make before you have a baseline is a guess with borrowed money.

**The rule.**

Thirty days of real numbers before your first big change. Revenue by source. Gross margin, meaning what's left after the direct cost of the work.

How full the team's week is. Cash in the bank every Friday.

Here's why it costs so much. Most changes are cheap to make and expensive to undo.

New scheduling software takes a week to install and three months to bed in, and while it beds in the crew bills fewer hours.

On a $2 million business at a 40% gross margin, a 5% dip in billable work is $40 thousand of profit you didn't plan for.

**Mistake 2. Going silent with the staff.**

You paid for the people. They walk faster than customers do. In silence, staff fill the gap with the worst story.

Day one, stand in front of them and say what isn't changing. Pay, roles, how the work gets done. Be specific.

Your key people should already be on employment agreements as a condition of closing, not an afterthought. Lose one supervisor and you can lose the customers who trust them.

**Mistake 3. Not visiting the top customers with the seller.**

Pull your top 20 customers and the share of revenue each one is.

Then visit or call them with the seller beside you, introducing you as the person who looks after them now.

If one or two customers are over 30% of revenue, that's a red flag in diligence. If you bought one anyway, those customers get the first visits and a call from you every month.

**Mistake 4. Letting the seller disappear.**

Negotiate a written transition, six months to three years. A seller being paid over time wants you to succeed, because their money depends on it. Use that.

Book a weekly handover. Ask for what isn't written anywhere. Who to call when the van breaks down. Which customer pays late but always pays.

Then hand each relationship to a named person in the business, not just to you.

Put the handover in the sale agreement, with hours and a schedule, so it's a term and not a favour.

**Mistake 5. Buying growth before you find the ceiling.**

This is the expensive one, because it feels like progress.

Ask one question. If sales doubled next month, what breaks first? The one supervisor? Scheduling? Quoting?

That answer is your first hire or your first system. It is never your first marketing campaign.

Buying customers you can't serve costs you the customers, the staff and the reviews at once.

A capacity fix pays twice. It protects the revenue you bought, and it's what lets every other growth lever work.

**The order.**

Days 1 to 30, listen. Days 31 to 60, measure and find the ceiling. Days 61 to 100, make one fix.

My first deal, a physio clinic, came with a nine-month payment holiday. That wasn't luck.

It was a term in the deal, there to buy time to learn the business, not rebuild it.

## A 10% price rise vs 10% more customers ($450K vs $330K profit)

Most professional buyers look at the price list before they look at marketing.

Here's why. On the same $2 million business, 10% more customers adds about $80 thousand of profit. A 10% price rise adds about $200 thousand.

Same revenue. More than double the profit. I'll show you the maths, the test for whether you're cheap, and the order to do it in.

**Why it matters.**

Profit sets the price of a business, not revenue. Every extra dollar of profit is worth three to five dollars when you sell, and it makes every seller payment safer from the first month.

At four times, $200 thousand of extra profit is $800 thousand of extra value.

This is for owners and buyers who want more out of capital already in the business.

It isn't for anyone who wants to be the cheapest in town. That's a race you win by going broke slowly.

Price is the biggest profit lever in a boring business, because a price rise carries no extra delivery cost.

Everything you were told points the other way. You were told the cheapest quote wins. You were told a business grows by adding customers. You were told a price rise loses the customers you've got.

And that's exactly why so many boring businesses sit on a price list from years ago while costs rise every year.

If you own one of these businesses, being cheap isn't your fault. It has nothing to do with your skill or your work ethic.

It has everything to do with nobody putting the margin maths in front of you.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and then do exactly this. If you want the numbers run on your deal, book a call below. Now the two options.

**Option A. Win 10% more customers.**

Revenue goes from $2 million to $2.2 million. But every new job carries its delivery cost. At a 40% gross margin, $200 thousand of new revenue leaves $80 thousand.

And it isn't free. You need the capacity to deliver it, and you pay to win those customers. Profit lands around $330 thousand before acquisition costs.

Now add them. Say the average job is $5 thousand, so you need forty new customers, and each one costs $2 thousand to win.

That's another $80 thousand. In year one, the volume play nets close to nothing.

**Option B. Raise prices 10%.**

Same $2.2 million of revenue. Same jobs, same crews, same materials.

So the extra $200 thousand lands almost entirely as profit. You go from $250 thousand to about $450 thousand.

And the part that matters for a sceptic. At a 40% gross margin, a 10% price rise still breaks even if one in five jobs walks. Most businesses lose far fewer than that.

**The self-test.**

Three checks for whether you're cheap.

One. When was the last price rise? If it's more than a year ago and your costs have moved, you're almost certainly undercharging.

Line the price history up against wages, materials and insurance. If costs are up 20% since the last rise, a 20% rise only gets you back to where you were.

Two. What's your quote win rate? Count last quarter's quotes and how many you won.

If you win almost every quote, you're not great at sales. You're cheap.

Three. Is there a budget option that eats your crew's time? Kill it or price it properly. One offer, one price.

**Price first, then volume.**

Start with new customers. New quotes, new contracts. Nobody has a reference point.

Then move existing customers with at least a month's notice, a short letter, the new rate and no apology.

Put the seller beside you on the calls to your biggest accounts, because those customers trust the seller more than you yet.

Move in jumps big enough to read. A tiny change disappears in the noise.

Pick a step that shows up in the numbers, run it on new quotes for a few weeks, and watch your win rate. You don't double prices overnight in a trades business.

**[JOSH: confirm]**

If you have contracts, read them for a review clause. Plenty of service contracts allow an annual increase that nobody has used in years.

That's a price rise the customer has already signed.

And never discount to win. If a customer pushes back, change the terms, not the price. Deposit up front, staged payments, a longer contract.

The ceiling is your team and your customers' satisfaction. Push past it and you'll know, because good customers start leaving for the right reasons.

**For the capital deployer.**

Here's the opportunity. A business that hasn't moved its prices in years is the cheapest value you'll ever buy.

You pay a multiple of today's profit. The price rise is profit the seller left on the table.

Two. Tweaking prices by 2% a year. Too small to read, too small to cover rising costs.

## Every level from referrals to buying customers at $4,000 each

Every owner of a boring business gets the same growth advice: post more, hire an agency, wait for referrals.

When it doesn't work, you're out the money and the months. And referrals arrive when they feel like it, not when the seller payment is due.

How about something different. You buy customers, on purpose, at a price you worked out before you spent a cent. Here's every level from word of mouth to that.

**Why it matters.**

A business that grows on referrals is valued on what it did.

A business that can show cost per customer, lifetime value and revenue by source is valued on what it will do. That's a better story in front of a bank and a better multiple in front of a buyer.

This isn't for anyone hoping a logo refresh will do the job, or anyone who wants to spend on ads before the business can deliver.

A real business can turn money into customers at a known return. Five levels get you there. Each one adds one constraint.

If your business has stalled on referrals, that's not your fault. It has nothing to do with your service or your reputation.

It has everything to do with how most boring businesses were built: one owner, one phone, and word of mouth.

I help owners buy boring businesses with little to no money down and build this on top. Book a call below. Now the levels.

**Level 1. Referrals only.**

Good customers send friends. It's a gift, not a system. You can't choose to grow in a given month, and you can't plan seller payments around a gift.

When I look at a deal, I ask one question: can revenue be bought here, with outbound or ads? If yes, there's upside the seller isn't pricing in.

And it cuts both ways at the exit. A referral-only business sells for less, because the buyer can't see where next year's customers will come from.

**Level 2. The owner sells.**

Growth now equals the owner's hours. Quotes, follow-ups, the big accounts. It works, and it's a ceiling.

If the owner can quote fifteen jobs a week, the business tops out at fifteen quotes a week, whatever the market wants.

It's also key-person risk, and a buyer will discount it.

**Level 3. Know what a customer is worth.**

Lifetime value is the gross profit a typical customer brings over the whole time they stay. Gross profit, not revenue, because revenue doesn't pay for marketing.

If you can't calculate it yet, take your last twenty customers and what they've paid you so far. A rough number beats none.

A common rule: lifetime value should be at least three times what it costs to win the customer. Below that, you're buying revenue at a loss once overheads land.

Then work backwards to a budget per quote. In the example on screen, that's up to $1 thousand to get each quote in front of a decision-maker.

Now you can judge a channel before you spend.

And from today, tag every new customer with its source. Referral, outbound, ads, website. Blended numbers hide which channel actually pays.

**Level 4. One channel with an input you can count.**

I know what you're thinking. My industry runs on relationships. Outbound doesn't work here.

I made about 1,000 cold calls before my first deal.

Saul, a member of the Academy, found an electrical and air-con business, 25 staff, about $3.8 million in revenue, with a cold call straight to the owner.

The question that finds a seller is the same muscle as "who looks after your cleaning at the moment?"

For a business-to-business service, outbound is often the first machine. A list, a script, a number of dials a day.

For a consumer business, it's usually paid ads. Either way, the input is counted.

**Here's the maths.**

Here's how that reads, illustratively. One caller on $70 thousand makes 40 dials a day.

Two hundred dials a week gets twenty conversations, four quotes and one new contract.

Fifty-two contracts a year at about $1,350 each, against a $4 thousand ceiling. That's a channel worth feeding.

Before you scale it, check the offer sells to strangers, not just to friends of existing customers. If cold leads don't buy, fix the offer first.

**Level 5. The volume knob.**

Once a channel pays back, you feed it. Turn it up when you want growth. Turn it down to build systems. Growth stops being something that happens to you.

Two limits. Delivery first: before every push, ask what breaks first if the work doubled next month.

And keep the 1.5 times cover in front of you every month, because your marketing is paid from the same cash flow that pays the seller.

The first marketing money often comes from the deal itself. The working capital line is drawn at settlement.

Cam, a good friend of mine, did two deals in 23 days with 100% vendor finance and got $500 thousand of working capital from the bank.

It's borrowed money. Test small, set the test size before you start, and never fund marketing with unsecured debt.

A channel that gets you in front of a decision-maker for under $1 thousand a quote is worth running. Above that, it's not, however good the agency's report looks.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Hiring an agency in month two and spending the working capital on clicks the crew can't deliver.

Two. Calculating lifetime value on revenue, not gross profit, and overpaying for every customer.

Three. Judging a channel on lead count. A cheap channel full of tyre-kickers can lose money while it looks busiest.

**What to do.**

You can build this alone, by trial and error with the money that's meant to pay the seller. Or you build the acquisition plan into the deal model before you close.

## Why $380K profit sells for more than $500K

There are two types of business owners: the ones who own a business, and the ones who own a job with a business around it.

They can make the same profit. They sell for very different prices.

Here's the contrarian part. Cutting your profit by $120 thousand can add $400 thousand to what the business is worth.

**Why it matters.**

Small businesses trade at roughly three to five times profit. Where you land in that range has a lot to do with one question: does the business need its owner?

If the owner wins the customers and keeps them happy, a buyer sees key-person risk. If the owner leaves, so do the customers, and the buyer prices that in.

This is for anyone who wants to own more than one business, or sell one for a proper price. It's not for people who want to be the hero who does everything.

Owner-independence is valuation. Three boxes, three named people, and proof that it runs without you.

If you're the owner who's still the business, that's not a character flaw. It has nothing to do with being a control freak or not trusting your people.

It has everything to do with how small businesses grow. The founder does every job first, and nobody ever hands them on.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and set them up to run without the owner. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Why people believe the opposite.**

Owners are paid on profit, so every salary feels like money out of their pocket. "Why pay a manager $120 thousand to do what I do for free?"

Because you're not free. Your time is the cost the P&L doesn't show, and the buyer sees it straight away.

Think of two rental properties with the same rent. One has a tenant on a five-year lease.

The other only earns if you live in it and run it as a guesthouse. Same income. Nobody pays the same for both.

**The market has moved.**

A generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. They want out, and they want to be looked after.

And most of their businesses run through them. Twenty years of being the first call for every customer, every quote, every problem.

That's often why they're selling. Saul, an Academy member, is in early due diligence on an electrical and air-con business: 25 staff, about $3.8 million in revenue, and an owner retiring in two years.

That's the pattern. So the buyers who know how to take the owner out of these businesses are the ones who capture the gap between three times and five times.

Once you've bought, the owner in the middle is you.

**Three boxes, three names.**

Split the business into three boxes.

Win the work: everything that brings customers in. Quoting, sales, the big accounts.

Do the work: everything that delivers and keeps customers. Crews, clinicians, account managers.

Protect the business: everything that keeps the other two out of trouble. Finance, payroll, compliance, HR.

Write a name under each. Every box with the owner's name, or the seller's, is key-person risk.

Fix one box at a time. Low revenue is a Win-the-work problem. Churn is a Do-the-work problem.

A missed invoice or a licence that nearly lapsed is a Protect-the-business problem.

In a 3C deal, your board and external team already cover a lot of Protect the business. Your job is a named person in the business for each box, not just on a board.

**Build it, then prove it.**

Set the scorecard before you hire for a box. A manager who writes their own scorecard writes one they can hit.

Pay by box. Win the work and Do the work earn money, so tie part of their pay to results.

Protect the business is a cost centre, so it's salaried. Buyers like a pay structure they can see tied to results.

Write the SOPs. Every repeatable job written down or screen-recorded, so a new hire does it well in week one.

Record one job a week and in a year you have fifty jobs that don't need you.

Watch labour cost per revenue dollar. As systems make jobs easier, it should fall as you grow. If it's rising, you're adding people, not systems.

Work it out for the last twelve months now, so you have a starting point a buyer can compare against.

Use the seller transition. The seller's job isn't just to answer questions.

It's to hand their relationships to named people, so the next buyer doesn't depend on you either.

Then prove it. The strongest evidence you can hand a buyer is a stretch where the owner was away and the numbers held.

Two weeks is a start. A month is better. Write down what broke and fix that next.

I run a healthcare group full time. I built my board from LinkedIn and cold outreach, and I keep 73% of my company.

## The 9-number Monday sheet buyers read before your P&L (copy it)

**Why it matters.**

At least three to one.

What's left of each dollar after the direct cost of the work. It's where price shows up.

It should fall as systems make jobs easier.

How many customers you lost, or how many came back. Recurring, sticky revenue is what lenders and buyers pay for.
