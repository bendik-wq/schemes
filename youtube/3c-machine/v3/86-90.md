## 4 mistakes that cost $240,000 when the seller owns the building

How come buyers with real capital still overpay for businesses?

They've got money, advisers and patience. But when the seller also owns the building, a lot of them miss one line in the accounts.

In the example I'll show you, that line is worth $240 thousand.

Here are the four mistakes, cheapest to most expensive.

In owner-run businesses, the owner often owns the property too.

That changes the profit you're paying a multiple on, the cash you need at settlement, and how keen the seller is to carry finance.

Get it right and the building helps fund your deal. Get it wrong and you pay for profit that doesn't exist.

This is for buyers who want to operate businesses. If you'd rather be a landlord, that's a different game.

When the seller owns the building, the lease is part of the price. Four mistakes, ranked.

When a buyer overpays here, it has nothing to do with being careless or bad with numbers.

It has everything to do with accounts prepared by an owner who is also the landlord. They were never built for a buyer.

I help buyers structure deals where the property is part of the picture. If your target's owner also owns the building, book a call below.

Now the detail.

**Mistake 1. Missing the above-market rent.**

The cheapest mistake is the one you miss in your favour.

Some owners charge their own business above-market rent, because it's a tax-effective way to pay themselves. Profit looks worse than it is.

Normalise it and you can be the buyer who sees value the others don't.

That's money left on the table, not lost. It's still a mistake.

**Mistake 2. Treating the lease as paperwork.**

When the seller is your landlord, the lease is negotiated in the same conversation as the price. That's leverage.

Rent in arrears: you pay after the month, not before.

On my first deal, a physio clinic, the rent was in arrears. No deposit, a nine-month payment holiday on the vendor finance, and rent paid after the month.

No bond: cash at settlement you don't have to find.

A deferral: six months' rent deferred and paid back over two years. At $60 thousand a year, that's $30 thousand that stays in the business while you settle in.

And a long lease with options, because lenders won't lend comfortably against a short one.

How to ask: agree the headline price first. Then raise the lease.

"Given the transition, I'd like rent in arrears, no bond, and the first six months deferred. You still get every dollar."

**Mistake 3. Pricing on profit with no rent in it.**

Illustrative. A business shows $300 thousand of profit. The owner owns the building and charges the business nothing.

Market rent is $60 thousand a year.

Once you own it, you pay rent. So real profit is $240 thousand.

At four times, that's $960 thousand, not $1.2 million. A $240 thousand swing from one line.

Before any offer you want three years of financials, matching tax returns and a revenue breakdown.

Add one question: who owns the building, and what does the business pay for it? Then get a local agent's market-rent opinion.

Your coverage maths has to include the rent too. If it only works with no rent, it doesn't work.

**Mistake 4. Buying the building.**

Buyers with capital think the building is the safe part. Property is the real asset, the business is the risky bit, so owning both is conservative.

That's not how it works. The business is the asset.

Cash flow pays everyone, and cash keeps a newly bought business alive.

Tie that cash up in bricks early and you've slowed the part that builds value: cash flow now, and multiple arbitrage when you sell a group.

And the seller keeping the building works in your favour. Sell the business, keep the property, and they have two incomes: your payments and your rent.

The rent only keeps coming if the business keeps going. So their retirement depends on you doing well.

That's alignment you can't buy.

It also shrinks what you fund. If you're not buying the property, you're not borrowing against bricks. The price is just the business.

That's a smaller number for the seller to carry and a smaller number for a bank to look at.

Default: buy the business, lease the building, revisit the property in a few years.

You'll also hear "sale-and-leaseback": sell a property to an investor and lease it straight back. Cash now, rent for years.

It only makes sense if the rent still fits inside 1.5 coverage.

Priced on real profit, the deal clears 1.5 comfortably and the seller still gets about $184 thousand a year from you.

Priced on the flattered number, you've paid $240 thousand for rent you'll be paying anyway.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Closing without the lease signed. The seller can raise the rent once you own it. Set rent reviews and options in writing first.

Two. A short lease with no options. The bank won't lend against it, and the deal stalls at finance.

Three. Forgetting the rent in your coverage. Seller payments plus rent, against what the business really makes. If it only clears 1.5 with free rent, it doesn't clear.

Four. A third-party landlord ignored. The lease still has to be assignable and long enough for a lender. You just have less to negotiate.

## $1M of unpaid invoices is an $800K loan

Every day, someone online is telling business buyers how to fund the gap. Raise from investors. Take a personal loan. Save for two more years.

How about something different.

A business with a million dollars of eligible unpaid invoices can support around $800 thousand of borrowing. It's a loan the business hasn't asked for yet.

A newly bought business usually runs out of working capital before it runs out of profit. Wages, suppliers, the gap between doing the work and getting paid.

Fund that from your pocket and you've put your own money in after all. Fund it from investors and they get a say in your company.

Asset-based lending funds it from the business's own balance sheet.

It suits businesses that invoice other businesses on terms. It doesn't suit a café.

Asset-based lending, ABL, is a line of credit secured on the business's receivables and stock.

The lender advances a percentage of what's eligible. That total is the borrowing base.

When a newly bought business runs dry in month four, it has nothing to do with the new owner's effort.

It has everything to do with a structure that funded the price and forgot the working capital.

Three parts: why the receivables are the security, how the base is worked out, and the test.

I help buyers put the full stack together: seller, bank, working capital. If your target carries big receivables or real stock, book a call below.

Now the detail.

**The lender isn't lending against you.**

Before you know how a lender reads a business, receivables look like admin. Money that turns up eventually.

After you know, they're a facility.

Every invoice under 90 days is borrowing power. Every invoice that ages past it is borrowing power you just lost.

That's why this works for a buyer with no track record. The lender is looking at the invoices, the stock and your board.

It's still credibility first, but the asset carries most of the weight.

It suits manufacturers, distributors, logistics, trades doing commercial work on 30 or 60 days.

On bigger deals, roughly over $5 million, banks often don't need a personal guarantee, because an asset-rich business gives them their security.

Typically. Every lender and deal is different.

**How the borrowing base works.**

Illustrative. A distributor turns over $8 million and makes $800 thousand of free cash flow.

Receivables are $1.2 million.

Lenders usually exclude very old invoices, say over 90 days, money owed by related parties, and anything above a limit for one big customer.

Say $1 million is eligible. Advance rates on receivables are often around 70 to 85%. Use 80%: $800 thousand.

Stock is $1 million. It's harder to sell if things go wrong, so the rate is lower. Use 40%: $400 thousand.

Borrowing base: about $1.2 million. It's a ceiling, recalculated every month as receivables and stock move.

Use it for working capital first. A working capital line at settlement is often around 10% of revenue. Here, about $800 thousand.

My good friend Cam got $500 thousand of working capital from the bank on top of 100% vendor finance. That's the job this line does.

Then, maybe, part of the price. Some buyers draw on the line to pay part of the price so the seller carries less.

It can work, but only with real headroom. If you spend the line on the price, there's nothing left for the slow month.

**The test.**

Before you count on a line, answer three questions about the target.

**One. What are the debtor days?**

If customers pay in 75 days and the cut-off is 90, one slow quarter pushes invoices out of the base.

**Two. What share of receivables is the biggest customer?**

One customer at a third of the ledger gets capped, and if they stop paying, the line wobbles.

**Three. Who owns collections after settlement?**

With ABL, your receivables ledger is the loan. Collections stops being admin and becomes a finance job.

Somebody owns the aged-debtors report every week.

If you can't answer all three, you don't know the size of your line yet.

And if the target has almost no receivables or stock, ABL isn't your tool. Vendor finance and a normal working capital line are.

The line moves with the business. When sales grow, it grows. When sales drop, it shrinks, right when you'd want it most.

So it never replaces the 1.5 rule. Seller payments, bank payments and ABL interest, against free cash flow. 1.5 minimum.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Spending the line on the price. Then there's nothing for the slow month, and a bad quarter puts you over your limit.

Two. Springing subordination on the seller in week ten. An ABL lender wants first security over receivables and stock, so the seller's note ranks behind it.

Explain it at the LOI: "The bank gets paid first on the invoices and stock. You still get every payment we agreed."

Three. Ignoring the costs and covenants. Interest on what you draw, set-up fees, sometimes an unused-limit fee.

Break a covenant and the lender can freeze the line.

**Two paths.**

You can keep doing what most buyers do. Count every receivable when half are ineligible. Spend the line on the price.

Let collections slide after settlement, so the line shrinks just as payroll is due.

Or you can build the stack properly the first time, with someone who's done it.

## Every layer of a $10M deal, from bank debt to equity, explained

There are only two types of buyers on bigger deals.

The ones who fill the gap with debt and keep the company. And the ones who fill it by giving the company away.

Here's every layer of the stack, from first paid to last.

Bigger deals are where the real money is.

This isn't for a first-time buyer looking at a $500 thousand business. Park it. It'll matter later.

A deal is a stack of layers. Each one ranks in order of who gets paid first, and the further down you go, the more it costs.

Five levels.

When owners stall at one business, it has nothing to do with ambition or work ethic.

It has everything to do with only being shown two options: save more, or sell equity.

**Level 1. Senior bank debt.**

Top of the stack. First in line, cheapest money.

Mezzanine sits below the bank and above your equity. It's usually subordinated, ranking behind the bank.
