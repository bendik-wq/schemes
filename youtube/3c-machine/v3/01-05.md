## Every way to buy a business, from 100% down to 0% down (the 3C map)

There are two types of people who want to own a business. The ones who save until they can afford one.

And the ones who work out the business can pay for itself.

I'm Josh Li. At 25 I was unemployed, with no savings and no experience.

Take a business making 300 thousand a year in profit. At four times profit, it's priced at 1.2 million.

How you fund that 1.2 million decides how much of the 300 thousand you keep, how much of the company you own, and who gets the exit.

Same business. Very different outcomes.

Before I owned anything I worked in banking. More than 30 million dollars of deals by 21, and I've been involved in over 100 million of transactions.

This is for owners, executives and people with capital who want to own cash flow. If you want a four-hour week, it isn't for you.

You were told you need a deposit. You were told the bank decides.

You were told that if you don't have the money, you raise it.

None of that is the whole picture. The cheapest money in most deals is the seller's.

The scarcest thing is credibility.

There are four levels of funding a business. Most people are stuck on Level 2.

Now the detail.

**Level 1: Pay cash.**

You write a cheque for 1.2 million. You own 100 percent and you keep all 300 thousand.

That's a 25 percent return on your cash before tax. Not bad.

The problem is you can do it once. Your capital is now locked in one business, and the next one needs another 1.2 million.

**The rule.**

Your own cash is the most expensive money in a deal, because it's finite.

**Level 2: Deposit plus bank.**

Say the bank wants 30 percent down. That's 360 thousand of your money, and the bank lends the rest.

Better return on your cash. But you spend years saving the deposit.

Then the bank takes months to decide, and you're bidding against buyers with more money than you.

This is where most people sit. They're not short of ability. They're short of a structure.

**Level 3: Investors.**

You put in nothing. Investors put in the lot.

It sounds like the answer. It isn't.

Every cheque is months of meetings. Then you've given away a large slice of the company, a say in how it's run and usually a say in when it's sold.

If investors end up with half, you get half the exit on a business you run every day.

The rule I use: debt over dilution. Borrow inside safe limits rather than sell the company.

**Level 4: The structure.**

Here's the third way. The seller gets paid over time.

Sometimes the seller keeps a slice, which we call a rollover. The bank fills the gap, and little to none of it is your cash.

The limit is one number. Free cash flow has to cover the debt payments at least 1.5 times.

On 300 thousand of profit, that's no more than 200 thousand a year going to payments, and 100 thousand of buffer.

What you give instead of cash is equity to a board. Experienced operators, two to ten percent each, who make banks and sellers take you seriously.

I built mine through LinkedIn and cold outreach with no network. The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder.

I still own 73 percent of my company.

My physio clinic was this level. 100 percent vendor finance, seven years at four percent, principal and interest.

A nine-month payment holiday. Rent in arrears. No deposit.

I pulled 150 thousand out in year one and built mid six figures in equity.

**The map.**

Level 4 runs in five stages, in order.

Credibility. No track record, so you borrow one. A board, good lawyers, good accountants.

Capital. Seller, rollover, bank. The 1.5 rule and debt over dilution sit over all of it.

Closing. You call owners directly. Before any offer you get three years of financials, matching tax returns and revenue by year.

You offer three to five times profit in a letter of intent. Then due diligence on whether the earnings are real, then legals.

Grow. Every extra dollar of profit is worth several dollars when you sell.

Exit. Small businesses sell at small multiples. Groups sell at bigger ones.

Buy ten at around four times, combine them, and the group can sell at eight, ten, sometimes fifteen.

The first three Cs get you the business. Grow and Exit are where the money is made.

Level 4 caps payments at 200 thousand a year and leaves 100 thousand of buffer. You keep the cash flow and the exit, and your own money stays free for the next one.

Two. Giving away half the company at Level 3 to fund a business that could have funded itself.

Three. Agreeing to payments the profit can't carry. Break the 1.5 rule and one bad year takes the business.

Four. Trying to master all five stages before starting the first one.

The person most likely to fund your next business isn't a bank. It's the owner selling it.

Paid over time, a seller can often get six to seven times profit instead of the usual three to five.

**The seller's chair.**

Picture someone who's run a business for decades. It pays well. It also owns them.

That's the burden of ownership. A whole generation of owners in traditional industries is carrying it and ready to retire.

They want out. And they want the business, the staff and the customers looked after.

Their options are a cash buyer who needs a bank, or private equity that only wants bigger businesses and beats the price down.

Round numbers. The business makes 200 thousand a year in profit.

**Line 2: the timeline.**

Offer A waits on a bank. Six to sixteen weeks, a thousand questions, and it can still fall over.

**Line 3: the terms, and your test.**

Now your side. Profit of 200 thousand. Payments of 130 thousand.

200 divided by 130 is about 1.54. That passes the 1.5 rule.

The business pays for itself with a buffer.

If it doesn't pass, you don't do the deal. That's the line between a good structure and a trap.

Then the order. Agree the headline price first.

Once that's agreed, ask for a payment holiday of nine to twelve months, framed honestly. It gives you time to make sure the business keeps performing, which protects their payments.

If you need to, offer a slightly higher rate in return.

**The words.**

The seller is taking a risk on you. Three things make it small.

One. A credible board behind you.

Two. The seller staying on for a handover, usually six months to three years.

Three. A structure the business can carry.

Then the language. Open with one question: "Have you ever thought about selling?" Then listen.

The business funds both of you, as long as the 1.5 rule holds.

## $1M in a rental vs $1M in a business (the math)

James did everything he was told. He saved a deposit, bought a rental, then another.

Sam didn't. Sam bought a boring business from a retiring owner and paid for it over time out of its profit.

Ten houses are worth ten houses. Ten businesses, put together, can be worth a lot more than ten businesses.

Here's the math.

If your capital sits in property or the market earning a thin return, this is the comparison nobody ran for you.

Same million dollars. One option pays a yield. The other pays cash flow now and a bigger multiple later.

If you want something you can buy and forget, property may suit you better. I'll say so honestly.

Most people think property is the safe road and a business is a job with extra risk. That's not how it works.

A business is priced on what it earns. A property is priced on what people think it's worth.

Once you see that, the comparison changes.

Three differences: cash flow, time and the multiple. Then a self-test and a verdict.

And if most of your money is in bricks, that's not bad judgement. It's what everyone around you did, and nobody showed you the other column.

Now the detail.

**Option A: the rental.**

A million-dollar rental. The bank wants a deposit, say 200 thousand, saved or tied up first.

Rent after costs, say 40 thousand a year. Then interest on 800 thousand of loan.

Depending on rates, you keep a little, or you top it up from your wages.

**Option B: the business.**

A business priced at a million, at four times profit. That's 250 thousand a year in profit.

Dollar for dollar, the business throws off far more cash.

The reason it's priced cheaper is that it needs work, and buyers discount anything that needs work. That discount is your margin.

And the property you already own isn't wasted. It's an asset a lender can see, which can make the business deal easier and faster.

**Time, both sides.**

Be honest about this. Property is mostly passive.

A business isn't, at first. Staff, customers, suppliers. That's the work you're paid for.

But the time that matters more is how long it takes to start. A deposit takes years.

A retiring owner paid over time can get you in the door in weeks.

And growth compounds differently. More property usually means another deposit, or borrowing against the last one.

With businesses, the first one's cash flow and track record make the second easier, because lenders and sellers have seen you do it.

You also have levers. You can't double the rent because you ran the house better.

You can lift a business's profit by fixing price, cost and sales. And every dollar you add is worth several at the exit.

**The multiple.**

This is the one property can't do.

A small business with 250 thousand of profit sells for three to five times. It's small, it leans on the owner, and buyers price in the risk.

Put ten of them under one management team, with proper reporting and no single owner holding it together. That's a group.

Groups sell to private equity and bigger buyers at eight, ten, sometimes fifteen times.

Ten businesses at 250 thousand profit, bought at four times: 10 million in purchase prices, mostly paid over time.

Combined profit, 2.5 million. Sold at eight times: 20 million.

That's before debt, costs and everything that can go wrong.

Bought at four. Sold at eight. Same profit, bigger multiple.

A friend of mine, Jason Nagy, did a real version. His first deal was a 5.5 million dollar radiology business with zero down, on a 60/40 structure.

Seven years later he exited to private equity for 200 million.

**Self-test and verdict.**

Three questions.

One. Can you give this 40 hours a week or more for the first stretch? If not, stay in property for now.

Two. Do you want to improve an asset, or wait on one? If you'd rather wait, property is fine.

Three. Do you have equity in property already? Then you're not choosing. That equity can make your first business deal faster and easier.

The verdict: property for passive. Businesses for cash flow and the multiple.

For most people with capital, both, in that order. Property as the base, businesses as the engine.

The rental's return depends on the market. The business's return depends on you, which is the risk and the point.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Keeping all the capital in property because it feels safe, while the yield barely covers the interest.

Two. Buying the first business with no idea what the group looks like. Pick an industry nobody buys at scale and the multiple never shows up.

Three. Treating a business as passive. Without the hours in year one, the buffer disappears.

Four. Paying a property-style price for a business. Pay ten times profit for a small business and you've bought a yield, not a multiple.

## 5 mistakes that make a business impossible to fund (buy boring)

Every day, someone online is selling you the exciting business. The app. The brand. The café with the queue out the door.

The exciting ones are the hardest to fund, the easiest to copy, and they often walk out with the owner.

Here are the five mistakes that make a business impossible to fund, ranked, with the costliest last.

If you already run one business and you're stuck in it, the next one has to be different.

The wrong target costs you three months of diligence, legal fees and a deal the bank won't touch. The right one funds itself.

If you want trendy, this is the wrong video.

Buy boring. Businesses that have done the same useful thing for twenty years and will for twenty more.

Healthcare, trades, commercial and professional services. Fragmented markets full of owners ready to retire.

If you've looked at businesses before and nothing fit, it has nothing to do with your eye for a deal or your budget. It has everything to do with where you were looking.

The exciting stuff and the listings are where everyone looks.

Five mistakes. Each one costs you the deal.

I help people pick the right industry and the right business, through the Owners Academy or one-on-one. Book a call below.

Now the detail.

**Mistake 5: Buying exciting.**

Fads fade and anything trendy is easy to copy. Commodity businesses where the cheapest wins leave no margin to pay a seller.

Run the money. A café doing 1.2 million at under eight percent margin makes about 90 thousand.

Under the 1.5 rule, that's 60 thousand a year to pay a seller.

On a business priced at four times profit, 360 thousand, that's six years of payments with nothing left to pay you or a manager.

One bad winter and you're funding it yourself.

**The rule.**

A problem people will still have in ten years, at net margins of fifteen percent or more.

Pipes leak. Backs get sore. Offices need cleaning.

**Mistake 4: Buying what a bank can't lend against.**

Asset-light businesses with messy books don't get funded. Equipment, vehicles and receivables give a lender security.

On bigger deals, typically over about 5 million, that can mean no personal guarantee. Every lender and deal is different.

Clean numbers matter as much as assets. Before any offer, ask for three years of financials, the matching tax returns and revenue by year.

If they don't match, walk. A lender won't fund profit it can't see, and neither should you.

If a lender can't understand the business in five minutes, you'll struggle to fund it.

**Mistake 3: Buying the owner.**

If the owner is the business, you're buying a job, and a risky one. Check who the customers actually call.

Buyers price that in, and so do banks.

A business that depends on its owner is worth less the day the owner leaves. And you're the one holding the payments when that happens.

You want an owner ready to go within a couple of years, no one in the family taking over, and willing to stay for a handover.

That's also who says yes to being paid over time.

An owner who wants to walk out the day you settle is a red flag.

If you already run a business you can't step out of, you know exactly what that costs.

**Mistake 2: Buying a ceiling.**

Two ceilings to check. Location: a single café is capped by its street, a trades business covering a whole city isn't.

Concentration: if one or two customers are more than about thirty percent of revenue, one lost contract breaks your payments.

And look for the upside. Most boring businesses run on referrals and do no marketing.

That isn't a dealbreaker. It's what you fix in the Grow stage.

**Mistake 1: Buying too small.**

The costliest, because it looks like the safe option.

A business priced at 250 thousand, making 60 thousand. Under the 1.5 rule, no more than 40 thousand a year goes to the seller.

That leaves 20 thousand. That doesn't pay you, doesn't cover a manager and doesn't survive a bad year.

Under about 1 million, there's rarely enough cash flow to pay the seller, pay you and keep a buffer. Same work, more risk.

A bigger business also usually comes with a team, so you're buying a business and not a job.
