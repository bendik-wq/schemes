## The $800K customer eating your margin (P&L teardown)

There's a well-kept secret in businesses bought from retiring owners, and it isn't on the P&L.

In this one, the biggest customer brings in $800 thousand and leaves the least behind. Fix that one account and the business is worth about $224 thousand more.

Most new owners stare at revenue. That is… not it.

Here's the teardown, in the order you should read it.

In a 3C deal you pay the seller out of profit. So every dollar of margin you find lands twice.

Headroom on that payment now, and value at the exit, where profit gets multiplied.

This is for someone who has just bought, or is in diligence and wants to know what they're actually buying. It isn't for anyone planning to change prices in week one.

**The rule.**

Price every job before you change anything. Revenue tells you how busy you are. Margin tells you who's paying for the business.

We read it four ways. The P&L. The service lines. The customers. The jobs. Then what one fix is worth.

And if you've owned a business for a month and can't see the leaks, it has nothing to do with how smart you are or how many years you spent reading reports.

It has everything to do with how small businesses keep their books. The P&L is built for the tax return, not the owner.

**The P&L.**

The business is plumbing. $2 million revenue, $300 thousand profit after paying a manager to run it.

Bought on vendor finance, paying the seller $200 thousand a year. That's $300 thousand divided by 1.5, the minimum cover we structure to.

One definition. Gross margin is gross profit over revenue, and gross profit is revenue less the direct cost of doing the work.

Overhead is everything you can't pin on one job.

The P&L gives you one margin for the whole business. It's an average. Averages hide everything.

**Service lines.**

Split it three ways.

Emergency callouts, $500 thousand at 45% margin. That's $225 thousand of gross profit.

Maintenance, $700 thousand at 40%. $280 thousand.

New-build work for one builder, $800 thousand at 18%. $144 thousand.

About $650 thousand of gross profit in total. Take off $350 thousand of overhead and there's your $300 thousand.

**Customers.**

Most people think the biggest customer is the safest. They think more revenue means more profit. They think the seller knew where the money was.

That's not how it works.

The builder is 40% of revenue and about 22% of gross profit. That's normal, not bad luck.

Big customers negotiate, and over twenty years every negotiation went their way. Small customers never asked.

In diligence, one customer over 30% of revenue is a red flag. Once you own it, it isn't a flag. It's yours to manage.

**Rule.**

List your top ten customers by revenue, with gross margin next to each. The order usually changes.

**Jobs.**

Now go one level down. Pull twelve months of jobs.

For each one: what you invoiced, hours times the real cost of an hour, materials, and any return visit.

The real cost of an hour is the wage plus super, the van, fuel, the phone and the time between jobs. Cost it at the payslip rate and every job looks better than it is.

Sort worst to best. In trades and service businesses the same leaks turn up.

Variations done on the day and never invoiced. Return visits done free and never recorded.

A job an hour away charged like one around the corner. An old rate the previous owner agreed as a favour. And slow payers.

Then ask the techs one question: "What do we do that we never charge for?" Write down every answer.

Last cut: tag every job by where the customer came from. Referral, Google, the builder, the number on the van.

Your best margin often comes from the channel you spend least on.

In a clinic the job is the appointment. You cut by practitioner and by service. Same method.

**The fix.**

Month one, you measure and change nothing visible. Month two, you find why the builder's margin is low.

Say variations go unbilled and the rates haven't moved in years. At the next contract review, every variation gets billed and the rates move.

Margin goes from 18% to 25%.

Seven points on $800 thousand is $56 thousand more gross profit. Profit goes from $300 thousand to $356 thousand, and cover goes from 1.5 to about 1.78.

The seller payment doesn't move, so all of it is headroom.

And at an illustrative four times, one account review is worth about $224 thousand at the exit. The multiple depends on the market and the buyer.

**The decision rule.**

Any customer running at under half your average margin gets reviewed at their next contract date. Three options: reprice, rescope, or let them go.

And you only let one go when the seller payment is still covered 1.5 times without them. Here, it isn't. So this one gets repriced, not fired.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Cutting before measuring. You lose the best tech in week two, and the emergency customers who ask for him go too.

Two. Ringing the builder in month one with new rates. If they walk, $144 thousand of gross profit goes.

Profit drops to about $156 thousand against a $200 thousand seller payment. Cover falls below one.

Three. Costing labour at the wage. Every job looks profitable and the leaks stay hidden for another year.

**There are two paths here.**

Alone, it's your first time, so you make first-time mistakes. You cut early, ring the big customer early, or find the leak and fix it clumsily.

## Costs customers never see vs costs they feel ($32K found in $350K of overhead)

There are two types of new owners: the ones who cut what customers never see, and the ones who cut what customers feel.

That's it.

The first group finds about 10% more profit and nobody notices. The second saves a little and loses the customer.

Here's $350 thousand of overhead, line by line, and which list each line belongs on.

In a vendor-finance deal, every dollar of cost you remove goes straight to cover on the seller payment.

Cut the wrong dollar and you lose revenue you were counting on to make that payment.

This is for people who look at capital the way an investor does: what it costs, what it returns, what it protects. It isn't for the slash-and-burn crowd.

**The idea in one line.**

Cut once, from the list customers never see, then leave costs alone and go after income. Costs have a floor. Income doesn't.

Two options. Option A, the costs customers feel. Option B, the costs they never see. Then the test that sorts any line, and the verdict.

And if a business looks lean already, it has nothing to do with you missing something or not knowing the trade.

It has everything to do with time. Twenty years of small costs pile up, and nobody running the place reads the bank statement line by line.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down, then do this work in year one. If you want me on your numbers, book a call below. Now the detail.

**Option A: costs customers feel.**

You were told the way to fix a business is to cut the biggest line. You were told wages are the problem. You were told a new owner proves themselves by cutting hard.

None of that holds. The biggest line is usually the one customers feel. The money is in the small lines nobody reads.

List A is your best techs, the person who answers the phone, parts on the van, how fast you turn up. In a clinic it's your practitioners and your front desk.

Touch those and customers notice within a week. Pay properly here. Keeping a customer is worth more than the saving.

**Option B: costs they never see.**

Same illustrative plumbing business: $2 million revenue, $300 thousand profit, $200 thousand a year to the seller.

The $350 thousand of overhead is admin wages, vehicles, rent, insurance, software, accounting and bank fees, and phones and IT.

Walk list B.

Software: a twenty-year-old business often pays for three tools that do one job, plus seats for people who left. Say $8 thousand.

Insurance: re-quote through a broker at renewal, same cover checked line by line. Say $5 thousand.

Vehicles: sell the ute nobody drives and the lease, rego and insurance go with it. Say $7 thousand.

Bank and card fees: ask. Say $2 thousand.

One more that sits in delivery but customers never see: materials. Put more volume through fewer suppliers and ask for better terms.

A couple of points on $400 thousand is about $10 thousand.

Leave rent alone. If the seller owns the building, the lease was part of your deal. Note it for renewal.

**How you ask matters.**

Tell suppliers you're the new owner, you're reviewing every account and you'd like to keep them. Most would rather sharpen a price than lose the account.

Do it in one sweep, over a few weeks, not one supplier at a time over a year.

A new owner gets one natural moment to reopen every account. Use it once.

**The add-backs.**

In diligence you adjusted profit for the seller's personal costs: the car, the phone, sometimes a family member on payroll.

That adjustment assumed those costs stop. Check that they did.

If the seller's still on the fuel card six months after settlement, the profit you paid for isn't the profit you're getting.

**Labour per dollar.**

Watch labour cost per dollar of revenue, not the wage bill.

Tech wages of $800 thousand on $2 million is 40 cents in the dollar. Each cent you take off is worth $20 thousand.

You get there through hours, not pay. Driving across town between jobs. A second trip for a part. Going back to fix a job twice.

Fix the scheduling, stock the vans, lift the first-time fix rate, and the same team bills more hours. Nobody takes a pay cut.

**The test and the verdict.**

The test for any line: would a customer notice within a week if this changed? Yes, it's list A. Protect it. No, it's list B. Cut it.

Verdict: list B, once, properly, in month two. Then stop.

Six months squeezing the phone plan is six months not spent on income.

**Where it doesn't apply.**

If the overhead really is lean, skip the sweep. Go straight to the add-backs and labour per dollar. That's where a lean business still hides money.

And if labour per dollar is rising month on month, that's not a cost problem. It's a scheduling problem, and you fix it in the diary, not the payroll.

Add up the sweep. Software, insurance, the idle vehicle, bank fees and materials come to about $32 thousand.

Profit goes from $300 thousand to about $332 thousand, and cover on the $200 thousand seller payment goes from 1.5 to about 1.66.

About 10% more profit from lines no customer will ever see. Worth having, and it has a ceiling, which is why the bigger work after that is income.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Cutting list A because it's the biggest number. The good techs leave, and the revenue goes with them.

Two. Adding overhead ahead of income: a GM you don't need yet, a new system, a nicer office. Every new fixed cost comes out of the cash that pays the seller.

Three. Leaving the add-backs running. The profit you paid for quietly leaks back to the seller.

You can keep doing what most new owners do. Cut from the wrong list.

Squeeze the last $500 out of the phone plan while your biggest, lowest-margin customer keeps eating the margin. Wonder why the good techs leave.

Or you can cut once, properly, with someone who knows where to look, and get back to income.

## 4 guarantee mistakes that cost more than the guarantee ($12K worked example)

Every trades business tries to win quotes the same way: a sharper price, a faster reply, and "satisfaction guaranteed" on the van.

Nobody believes the van. So it wins nothing.

How about something different. A promise built from your own job data.

In this illustrative business it costs about $12 thousand a year, and it pays for itself if it wins $30 thousand more work. Here are the four mistakes that make guarantees cost more than that.

Price is the most expensive way to win work. Every dollar you drop comes straight off margin, and margin pays the seller.

A guarantee lets you win at the same price.

This is for owners who've hit the ceiling of what one good name can carry, and who'll count before they promise. Not for anyone who wants a slogan.

One line: a guarantee is only cheap when you're already good, and you only know you're good if you've counted.

Four mistakes, ranked. Mistake two is the expensive one. The last one is the one most owners never think of.

And if you've tried a guarantee before and it did nothing, it has nothing to do with your industry or your customers.

It has everything to do with where the promise came from. Copied off another van, it can't mean anything.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down, then do this kind of work in the first year. If you want help with yours, book a call below. Now the detail.

**Mistake 1. A promise nobody believes.**

You were told the bigger the promise, the more it sells. You were told a guarantee has to be bold to be noticed.

It works the other way. A promise that sounds too good makes people doubt the whole business.

Promise something specific and modest that you control. Pick one.

One: "Fixed price before we start. No surprises on the invoice."

Two: "If the same fault comes back within 90 days, we come back free."

Three: "We text when we're 30 minutes away. Miss the window you picked and the callout fee's on us."

Why fixed price works so well: people hate a surprise on the invoice far more than a higher number on the quote.

You're not promising perfection. You're removing the thing they're actually afraid of.

Put it where the decision happens: one line on the quote, near the price, and in the tech's mouth at the door.

In a clinic, never guarantee a clinical outcome. Guarantee the experience: on time, a clear plan, a call back the same day.

**Mistake 2. Writing it before you've counted.**

Same illustrative plumbing business: $2 million revenue, $300 thousand profit, $200 thousand a year to the seller. About 4,000 jobs a year.

Say 3% need a return visit. That's 120 trips at about $150 each.

$18 thousand a year you're already spending, for free, with no credit. A guarantee puts in writing what you already do.

Now model the downside. Putting it in writing pushes return visits to 5%, because a few people try it on.

200 visits, $30 thousand. Extra cost: $12 thousand.

**Rule.**

If your return-visit rate is 15%, don't write a guarantee. Fix the work first.

And if you've just bought and have no data, don't launch in month one. Track return visits for a quarter. The promise you write in month four will be one you can afford.

**Mistake 3. Ten conditions.**

Conditions full of gotchas turn a guarantee into fine print, and customers read it as a trick.

Worse, your own team stops believing it, and they're the ones who have to say it out loud.

Keep two or three, each one something the customer wants anyway, plus one catch-all.

One. Invoice paid on time. It filters out the customers hardest to look after.

Two. They went ahead with the fix you recommended. Choose the cheap patch and the guarantee covers the patch.

And the catch-all: nobody else has worked on it since.

One legal line. In Australia customers already have rights under consumer law, and other countries have their own.

Your guarantee sits on top of those rights, never in place of them. Get the wording checked by a lawyer before it goes on a quote.

**Mistake 4. Using one tool for two problems.**

Most quotes aren't lost to a no. They're lost to hesitation.

An outcome guarantee removes the fear of it going wrong. It doesn't remove the fear of starting.

So on bigger jobs, add the second tool: a small deposit to lock the booking, fully refundable until you order parts.

Saying yes becomes easy. Saying no becomes a decision. And cash arrives before the work, which matters, because cash is what pays the seller.

Now the payback. The guarantee costs an extra $12 thousand a year. At 40% margin, $30 thousand of extra work covers it. Every further $40 thousand won is $16 thousand of gross profit.

On a business quoting well over a million a year, $30 thousand is a small lift in quotes won. Past that point, the guarantee is making money.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Copying a competitor's guarantee without knowing your own return rate. It either means nothing or costs a fortune.

Two. Launching it in month one. Track return visits for a quarter first.

Three. Leaving it off the quote. A guarantee the customer never sees at the moment of decision wins nothing.

Or you can keep doing what most businesses do. Copy a promise off a van. Bury it in conditions.

Keep dropping price to win the quote, and pay for it out of the margin that pays the seller.

Or build it from your data, with someone checking it, and let it do its job.

## Profit doesn't pay the seller. Cash does. (60 to 30 debtor days = $165K)

How come a profitable business can run short of cash on the first of the month?

It has customers, a full diary and a P&L that says $300 thousand. But the bank balance never lies.

In this illustrative business, $165 thousand of cash is sitting with customers. Here's how to bring it home, without finding one new customer.

In a vendor-finance deal the seller gets paid from the bank account, on a date. Every dollar stuck with a customer is a dollar that isn't there on that date.

Run short twice and you're renegotiating with the person you just bought from.

This is for owners who check the balance with a knot in their stomach. Not for anyone who thinks cash flow is the accountant's job. In a 3C deal, it's yours.

One line: profit is an opinion on the P&L. Cash is what pays the seller.

Most people think profit is money. They think a good year means a full account. They think the seller gets paid from profit.

That's not how it works. The bank account only knows what's been collected.

Five parts. Measure it, find it, move it forward, chase it, and use the deal's own cash tools properly.

And if you've ever been profitable and short of cash at the same time, it has nothing to do with being bad with money or soft on customers.

It has everything to do with a P&L that counts money when you earn it, not when it arrives.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down, then make sure the business can carry its own debt. If you want me on your numbers, book a call below. Now the detail.

**Measure it: debtor days.**

Same illustrative plumbing business: $2 million revenue, $300 thousand profit, $200 thousand a year to the seller. About $16,700 a month.

Debtor days is what customers owe you, divided by revenue, times 365.

At year end they owe $330 thousand. $330 thousand over $2 million, times 365, is about 60 days.

The business waits two months to get paid for work it has already done.

**Find it.**

Do the same sum customer by customer. One or two accounts usually hold most of it.

Here, one customer, a builder, owes $200 thousand of the $330 thousand.

That's not a collections problem. That's one conversation. And you have it at the contract review, with that customer's margin numbers in front of you.

"We want to keep the work. To do that, we need fourteen-day terms or a rate that carries sixty." Either answer is better than what you have now.

And it's often the same customer with the weakest margin. Low margin and slow money together means you're funding their cash flow out of your seller payment.

**Move it forward.**

Five ways, in order of how fast they work.

**One. Paid on the day.**

For homes. The tech takes payment on site. Card fees are real, so price for them.

A couple of percent to be paid today usually beats sixty days and three phone calls.

**Two. Deposits.**

On quoted work, so materials are paid for before you order them. A refundable deposit to lock in the booking does double duty.

**Three. Progress payments.**

On bigger jobs. Never carry a whole job on your own cash.

**Four. Shorter terms.**

For businesses. Seven or fourteen days where you can get them. At the builder's contract review, terms go on the table next to the rates.

**Five. Upfront plans.**

Customers pay for the year's maintenance before the visits. Cash before the work.

**Chase it.**

An aged-debtors list, every Monday. A reminder the day it's due. A call at seven days. A firmer call at fourteen.

A written rule for when new work stops. Written down, so it isn't personal.

And take payment while you're in the room. A tech who leaves without payment has just become a debt collector.

"I'll pay when I get the invoice" loses you more money than any follow-up call ever wins back.

Set the techs up to take it, and make it the default, not the exception.

**Use the deal's cash tools.**

Two from the deal itself.

The working capital line. A bank will often put one in place at settlement.

My good friend Cam got $500 thousand of working capital from the bank across his two deals.

It's runway. It isn't a permanent loan to your slowest customer. If debtors keep growing and the line keeps getting drawn, the bank will notice before you do.

The payment holiday. My first deal, the physio clinic, had a nine-month payment holiday on the vendor finance. Seven years at 4%, nothing down.

A holiday like that is when you fix collections, before the first payment is due.
