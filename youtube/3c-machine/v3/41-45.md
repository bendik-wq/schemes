## 4 reasons sellers walk before settlement (ranked)

There are only two types of buyers. The ones who get the seller to settlement day, and the ones who get a phone call the week before.

The difference has nothing to do with price. A seller rarely pulls out on the day they pull out.

They pull out weeks before. They just tell you on the day.

Here's why price is rarely the reason. Take a business making $250 thousand profit. A cash buyer at four times offers about $1 million.

Paid over time inside the 1.5 rule, the seller gets about $166 thousand a year. Over ten years that's $1.66 million. Illustrative.

When a seller walks from that, something other than money got louder.

If you've taken one business as far as it goes and the next step is buying a second, this is how you keep that deal alive.

The idea in one line: the objection at the end is a question you didn't ask at the start.

Sellers walk for four reasons. I'll rank them from least to most dangerous, by how late they show up and how hard they are to fix.

Staff. Advisers. Remorse. Family.

You were told sellers sell to the highest bidder. You were told the price is the deal. You were told a seller who signs an LOI has decided.

None of that is the full story. A lot of sellers sell to whoever makes them feel safe about their name, their family and their people.

Every one of those is something you can deal with.

So if a seller has pulled out on you, it has nothing to do with your offer being too low or you being too young.

It has everything to do with a conversation nobody taught you to have in the first meeting.

I help people run their deals so the seller's still at the table on settlement day. Book a call below.

Now the four.

**Staff.**

Owners often care about their people more than they let on. A deal can die because the owner pictures telling the team.

The fix: show them the plan for the staff before they ask. You keep the team.

You meet key people before close, with the seller's permission, at the right time. Retention bonuses for key staff can go into the structure.

It's last on the list because it's the easiest to fix. Once the seller knows their people are looked after, it usually goes away.

**Advisers.**

The seller's accountant, lawyer, or a mate at the golf club says, "You could get more than that."

Sometimes they're right. More often they're comparing your offer to an all-cash price that doesn't exist for this business.

The fix: get the adviser in the room early and walk them through the maths.

A higher multiple paid over time out of profit looks very different once someone explains it properly.

And never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time."

**Remorse.**

Thirty years. Their name on the van. Their number on every invoice.

Then someone tells them, nicely, that in six weeks it won't be theirs.

That isn't about money. It's about who they are on Monday morning.

The fix: ask about life after the sale in the first meeting. "What does your week look like a year after this?"

If they can't answer, you've found your biggest risk.

Then build them a role. A transition, typically six months to three years, with a title and dates. A seller with somewhere to go is far less likely to walk.

**Family.**

The most dangerous one is the person you never met.

A spouse who wants the cash now. A son or daughter who assumed they'd inherit it. A sibling who owns 20% that nobody mentioned.

It's first because it surfaces last, when they can only say no.

The fix is one question, early. "Who else has a say in this? Who would you talk to before you signed?"

Then you meet them. Over coffee in week two, not at the signing.
