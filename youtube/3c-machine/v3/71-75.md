## What private equity pays for: every level from 3× to 8×

There are two types of people who buy a business. The ones who buy themselves a job, and the ones who buy something a bigger buyer will pay for.

Same $1 million profit. One sells for $3 million. The other sells for $5 million.

Nothing about the second one is clever. Here's every level between them, and how to build it in from the day you buy.

The idea in two lines. Credibility and Capital get you a business with little or none of your own money in it, and Closing gets it signed.

Then you grow it, and the exit is where you get paid for all of it.

A buyer pays a multiple of profit. One turn of multiple on $1 million of profit is $1 million.

So the only question here is what moves the multiple. If you want a lifestyle business you never sell, skip this one.

The multiple is the buyer's answer to one question: will this profit still be here after the owner leaves? The surer they are, the more they pay.

You were told value comes from hard work, years in the chair and revenue growth. If you believed that, it's not on you. It's what owners tell each other.

Buyers don't price any of it. They price certainty.

There are four levels. Each one adds one thing a buyer can trust.

I help owners and buyers do exactly this: buy with little to no money down, then build the business up these levels. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Level 1: A job you own. Around 3 times.**

The owner wins the work and does the work. Customers come back every year, but on a handshake.

The books get done once a year, for tax. One customer is a third of revenue.

A buyer sees profit that walks out the door with you. So they offer around 3 times, and hold part of it back in an earn-out to keep you tied in.

The test for Level 1 is simple. Take four weeks off with your phone off. If revenue drops, you're here, and so is the price.

If you're buying, this matters. Most retiring owners sell you a Level 1 business. That's partly why you can buy at 3 to 5 times profit.

It's not a problem. It's the gap you get paid to close.

**Level 2: Revenue a buyer can count. Around 4 times.**

Contracts, maintenance plans, memberships, retainers. In most traditional businesses the work already repeats. It just isn't on paper.

A contract is revenue a buyer can count. A handshake is revenue they have to guess at, and buyers discount guesses.

Turning handshakes into contracts is usually a letter and a phone call to customers who already come back every year.

On $1 million of profit, moving from 3 times to 4 times is $1 million of exit value. That's the best-paid admin you'll ever do.

**The rule.**

Get more than half of revenue on contract or a fixed schedule. Then spread it.

Once one customer is over 20 per cent, a buyer prices in the day that customer leaves.

**Level 3: Runs without you, numbers they can check. Around 5 times.**

This is the biggest jump. A general manager runs the day to day. You don't win the work and you don't do it.

And there's a stretch of months that proves it ran while you weren't there.

Next to that, numbers that check out. Monthly profit and loss reconciled to the bank. Revenue by customer and by source. Every add-back with a document behind it.

Clean numbers shorten diligence, and a short diligence keeps the price you agreed.

**The rule.**

Hire the GM one to two years before you want to sell, not one month. A buyer wants to see the year after you stepped back, not your promise.

**Level 4: Big enough for private equity. 8 times and up.**

Size changes who's buying. One business making $1 million attracts individuals and competitors.

A group with a management team attracts private equity, and private equity pays more for size and systems.

That's the roll-up in one line. Buy ten businesses at around 4 times profit, grow and combine them, sell the group at 8, 10, sometimes 15 times.

You don't need any one of them to be special. You need each one at Level 3 before it joins the group.

My friend Jason Nagy bought one radiology business for $5.5 million with zero down, using a lender plus the seller staying in.

Seven years later, private equity bought the group for $200 million.

The seven years are his story to tell. What it proves is that a zero-down first deal doesn't cap where you finish.

**Here's the example.**

Business A is Level 1. $1 million of profit at 3 times is $3 million, and part of that is deferred.

Business B is Level 3. The same $1 million of profit at 5 times is $5 million, mostly paid at settlement.

Same profit, $2 million apart. And B is paid mostly at settlement, so the buyer's risk isn't parked in an earn-out that might never pay.

First you buy A. Then you put in the contracts, the GM and the monthly books.

Once you have those, you own B, and you never paid a B price.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Chasing revenue instead of certainty. Another $2 million of handshake revenue still sells at a Level 1 multiple.

Two. Hiring the GM in the sale year. Buyers see it. The price drops a turn or comes with an earn-out. On $1 million of profit, a turn is $1 million.

Three. Leaving one customer at a third of revenue. The buyer cuts the price or walks.

Four. Buying without scoring the target. You can't plan a fix you never measured.

Score every target one to five on contracts, owner-dependence, concentration and monthly books.

**Two paths.**

Buy whatever you can get, run it hard, and find out at the sale it's worth $2 million less for things you could have fixed three years earlier.

Or build the exit in from deal one.

## The 7-folder data room that protects a $6M sale (copy it)

There's a quiet rule among people who buy businesses for a living. Most deals don't die on price.

They die in diligence, waiting for documents nobody can find.

On a $6 million sale, six slow months can cost you $500 thousand.

This is the folder that stops it, opened in the order a buyer's accountant opens it. Copy it.

A data room is one online folder holding every document a buyer will ask for, with access you control.

It pays twice. On the way in, it's your checklist: you can find the gaps in a target in an afternoon.

On the way out, it's what keeps the price you agreed. If you won't keep monthly books, nothing here will help you.

Most people think diligence is the buyer's problem. That's not how it works.

The price in your LOI is a draft. Diligence is where it gets tested, and every missing document is a delay, a price cut or a reason to walk.

If you've looked at a deal and couldn't tell a good one from a bad one, it has nothing to do with your intelligence or how much you've read.

It has everything to do with never having seen a complete set.

Seven folders, in five stops.

**Folders 1 and 2: Financials and tax.**

Opened first, every time. Three years of annual accounts.

Monthly profit and loss and balance sheet for at least the last two years, reconciled to the bank. Tax returns that match the accounts.

Monthly matters because buyers look at trend and seasonality, not just the year. An annual figure can hide a bad last quarter.

A buyer will find it, and price it as if it's the new normal.

When you bought, you asked the seller for three years of financials, matching tax returns and revenue by year. That was their data room. Now keep yours.

Revenue by customer, by service line and by source, every month. Your top twenty customers and their share. Who left, and why.

**Folders 4 to 6: Contracts, people, licences.**

Company records, the shareholders' agreement, board minutes, any disputes.

This is where your day-one structure pays off. Drag-along rights, and no veto for minority shareholders.

If someone with 3 per cent can block the sale, the buyer finds it here.

Fix it early. Changing a shareholders' agreement before a sale is a lawyer's afternoon.

During a sale, the minority holder knows they have leverage, and they'll price it.

The KPI pack is the story the folders prove. Twelve months of revenue per customer, gross margin, churn and concentration.

If you've been tracking your numbers every Monday, you already have it.

Access: signed NDA first, serious buyers only. Customer names and staff pay only after a signed LOI.

It's the same rule we use as buyers: the NDA is the first test of who's serious.

## Sell 100% vs sell 60% (the $32M second bite)

Growing a group of businesses is a great position to be in. Cash flow, a team, control.

Until the next step needs more money and more people than you have.

That's when most owners think there are two options: stay stuck, or sell the lot.

There's a third. In the example I'll show you, the second cheque is bigger than the first.

It's called a recapitalisation, or recap. A buyer, usually private equity, buys a majority of your company, say 60 per cent.

You're paid for that now and you keep the rest. When the group is sold again, your 40 per cent gets paid too. That's the second bite.

This is for owners who've hit a ceiling and think the next five years are worth more than the last five.

If you want one clean cheque and never to think about the business again, sell 100 per cent. That's a fine answer.

Most people think selling means walking away. At the top end, that's not how it works.

A recap trades certain money for a bigger, uncertain second cheque, and trades control for a partner with more capital than you.

If you've hit the ceiling, it has nothing to do with your work ethic or your industry. It has everything to do with arithmetic.

One owner's money and one owner's hours only stretch so far.

Option A, option B, the rights that decide it, and a test.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and structure them for an exit like this. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Option A: Sell 100 per cent.**

Certain, simple, done. You get today's multiple on today's profit.

What it costs you is the next jump.

The buyer is paying you a group multiple so they can buy more businesses at 3 to 5 times, combine them, and sell at 8, 10 or more.

Sell everything and that arbitrage is theirs, not yours.

In our example, the group makes $4 million of profit and sells at 8 times. That's $32 million today, before debt, fees and tax. Nothing left at risk and nothing left to gain.

It's the right call if you're done, or if you don't believe the next five years beat the last five.

**Option B: Sell 60 per cent, keep 40.**

This is the mirror image of buying with a 60/40 structure.

When you buy that way, a lender funds most of the price, and the seller rolls part of their value into equity and stays in.

In a recap, you're the seller who stays in.

In our example the buyer pays $19.2 million for 60 per cent, and your 40 is worth $12.8 million on paper.

Paper, not cash. It only turns into cash at the next sale.

The buyer wants you to keep 40 per cent. It keeps you motivated and tells them you believe the numbers.

If an owner wants every dollar out on day one, a buyer asks why.

**Now the case against.**

Profit could fall. The buyer could overpay for what they add, or load the group with debt.

And you stop being the boss. Every rule you set for your own minority shareholders, drag-along, no veto, no voting control, now applies to you.

**The rights that decide it.**

Agree these before you sign, while you still have something they want. Proper lawyers. Not a template.

One. Tag-along. If they sell their 60, you can sell your 40 on the same terms.

Two. Information. Monthly accounts, the board pack, the debt position.

Three. A board seat, or at least the right to attend.

Four. Consent on the big things: much more debt, new shares that dilute you, selling a major part of the business.

You won't get a veto on everything. Spend your leverage on debt and dilution, because those are what shrink your 40.

Five. Leaver terms. What happens to your 40 per cent if you leave or they push you out. Read it twice.

**The test and the verdict.**

Write down your first-bite number: the amount that changes your life even if the second bite never comes.

If 60 per cent of today's value clears it, a recap is on the table. If it doesn't, sell 100 per cent or don't sell yet.

Then check what the second bite depends on: the buyer's plan, how much debt they'll put on the group, and whether you'll still be running it.

Ask to see the plan before you sign. If they won't show you, that's your answer.

The verdict: recap when the first bite clears your number, you believe in the next five years, and you've got the rights in writing.

Otherwise, take the whole cheque.

Here are the cases, before debt, fees and tax. Today you take $19.2 million for 60 per cent.

Good case: in year five profit is $8 million and the group sells at 10 times, $80 million. Your 40 pays $32 million, $51.2 million in total.

Bad case: profit falls to $3 million at 7 times, $21 million. Your 40 pays $8.4 million, $27.6 million in total.

The good case pays $19.2 million more than a full sale. The bad case pays $4.4 million less.

It's a bet, so size the first bite to be fine if it loses.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Signing the buyer's shareholders' agreement as drafted. Their lawyers, their terms. You find out what's in it in year three.

Two. A first bite too small to live on. If the second never comes, you've sold 60 per cent and carried all the risk on the rest.

Three. No tag-along. They sell their 60 to someone you didn't pick, and your 40 is stuck with them.

Four. Weak leaver terms. Get pushed out and your 40 per cent can be bought back at a discount.

Five. Ignoring the debt stack. Lenders are paid before equity.

Load the group with debt and your 40 per cent of the sale price shrinks after they're paid.

**Two paths.**

On the first, the minority agreement is written by the other side's lawyers and you find out what it means three years later.

On the second, your structure is set up from deal one, so at the end you've got options, not surprises.

## 5 earn-out mistakes that turn a $1M sale into $700K

How come people with capital, good lawyers and a signed deal still end up in a fight two years after settlement?

They had the money. They had the advisers. What they also had was an earn-out written like a handshake.

On a $1 million deal, that's $300 thousand riding on one paragraph. Here are the five mistakes that lose it, cheapest first.

Typical shape: a base of 70 to 80 per cent, and an earn-out of 20 to 30 per cent paid over one to three years if the business hits agreed targets.

You'll meet it from both sides. As a buyer, it means you don't pay for "maybe" at settlement.

As a seller, it's how buyers bridge the gap between your price and their doubts.

This is for people doing real deals. If you're still deciding whether to start, this one can wait.

You were told an earn-out is a bonus. Upside. A sweetener.

It isn't. It's part of the price that might never arrive, and whether it does depends entirely on what's written down.

If you've seen one go badly, it had nothing to do with trusting the wrong person. It had everything to do with a target nobody defined properly.

Here's the example we'll use. A $1 million sale. $700 thousand is the base, paid on a vendor note over seven years at 4 per cent.

The rest is up to $100 thousand a year for three years, if revenue targets are hit.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and structure deals like this so they hold. Book a call below. Now the mistakes.

**Mistake 1: Too many metrics.**

Five targets is five arguments. Revenue, margin, headcount, new customers, retention, each with its own definition.

Hit three, miss two, and now you're arguing over what a partial payment should be. Every one becomes a dispute about how it was counted.

Each argument costs legal fees, management time and goodwill with the person you need running the handover.

On a $300 thousand earn-out, one dispute can eat a third of it before anyone wins.

**The rule.**

One or two metrics. Never five.

**Mistake 2: No cap, no reporting.**

As the buyer, an uncapped earn-out means one lucky year can cost you more than the business was worth.

Say a big one-off contract lands in year one. Uncapped, you pay a share of revenue you'll never see again. Cap it at the agreed amount.

You also keep control of operations. A seller chasing a number will run the business for this year's target at the expense of the next five.

And both sides get monthly reporting. Nobody should find out at year end that the target was missed in March.

If the seller sees the trend in month four, they can still act on it. If they see it in month thirteen, they call a lawyer.

**Mistake 3: Undefined terms.**

Most earn-outs are a paragraph that says "revenue" and stops. Four fights come up again and again. Each one has a clause that stops it.

One. "You changed how revenue is counted." Define revenue line by line, the way it was counted in the accounts you diligenced.

Two. "You moved my customers into your other business." Say what happens to revenue that moves between businesses.

Three. "You fired the team that was going to hit it." Agree what the buyer can and can't change during the earn-out.

Four. "You sold before year three." Say what happens to the unpaid earn-out on a sale. Usually it's paid out, or a fair share of it.

**Mistake 4: A profit target instead of revenue.**

This one costs the whole earn-out. Profit depends on a hundred cost decisions, and after settlement the buyer makes all of them.

They merge your team into theirs, move overheads around, and your profit target is missed through nothing you did.

Say the buyer moves $80 thousand of group overheads onto your business. Revenue hits the target. Profit misses by $80 thousand. Earn-out: zero.

**The rule.**

Revenue wherever you can. It's harder to move and easier to check.

**Mistake 5: Accepting a base you wouldn't take alone.**

This is the most expensive one, and the test is simple. Would you accept the base alone?

If not, you haven't sold the business. You've sold part of it and taken a bet on the rest.

$3 million with two of it in earn-out is really $1 million and a lot of hope.

Here's what most people miss. The fix isn't in the contract.

A buyer loads the earn-out when the business depends on you: they think the profit walks out with you.

Make it run without you before you sell, and the base goes up.

As a buyer, offer one in two cases only.

First, when you'd otherwise be arguing about price. The seller says this year will be the best yet, so you pay for it when it happens, out of the extra profit it brings.

Second, when the seller holds the customer relationships and you need them through the handover. That's the six-month to three-year transition, with money attached.

Write it as a defined revenue target and the seller receives the full $1 million: the $700 thousand base plus $300 thousand of earn-out.

Write it as an undefined profit target, the costs get moved, and the earn-out is zero. The seller receives $700 thousand.

Same business, same performance. One paragraph decides $300 thousand.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Agreeing the earn-out before the base. Negotiate the base as a fair price on its own first, then talk about upside.

Two. Planning your life on the total. Plan on the base. Treat the earn-out as money you might not see.

Three. "We'll sort the detail later." The detail you didn't write is the fight in year two.

Four. Stacking an earn-out on an all-vendor-finance deal. Getting paid over time already shares the risk. An earn-out is for the gap that's left.

Or you can keep doing it the usual way. One paragraph, agreed over lunch between two people who like each other.

Year two is when you find out what it meant, and what it cost.

## Sell to your own managers for $1.66M (vendor finance in reverse)

Two owners, same business, both ready to retire.

James lists with a broker, opens his books to strangers and waits a year. Tom sells to his general manager, who pays him over ten years out of the business's own profit.

On $250 thousand of profit, that can be about $1.66 million to Tom. He never met a stranger.

I know this structure from the other side. At 25 I was unemployed, with no savings and no experience.

I bought my first business, a physio clinic, on 100 per cent vendor finance. Seven years at 4 per cent, a nine-month payment holiday, no deposit.

I pulled $150 thousand out in year one.

A management buyout is that deal with you as the seller. It's for owners stuck inside their business.

If you need the highest cash price at settlement, private equity is usually your buyer, not this.

Your best buyer might already be on your payroll, and the money to pay you is already in the business.

If you feel trapped in your own business, it has nothing to do with being a bad manager or picking the wrong industry.

It has everything to do with being told there's one way out: find a stranger with cash.

Strangers with cash are rare, slow and picky. Your managers are already there.

Until now, the debt maths was buyer's maths. Now it's your exit plan. Five parts.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and plan the exit from day one. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Why my seller said yes.**

A generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire, and they carry the burden of ownership.

They don't want to wait sixteen weeks for a bank or haggle with a broker. They want out, and they want the business and the people looked after.

**[JOSH: one line on why your physio seller agreed to be paid over time.]**

And getting paid over time can be worth more than cash. Sellers on terms can get 6 to 7 times where cash offers sit at 3 to 5 times.

And they close in two to four weeks instead of six to sixteen. One day that seller is you.

**Why your managers can be the better buyer.**

Four reasons.

One. Speed: they know the business, so diligence is shorter.

Two. The team stays, and customers never see a stranger walk in.

Three. Your numbers don't go to competitors who might be fishing.

Four. You're paid on terms, at a better price.

Your managers usually don't have the cash, and they don't need it. Neither did I. The money comes from the business. That's the whole model.

The case against: a bigger buyer may pay more at settlement, and you carry the risk. If your managers run it into the ground, your money goes with it.

**Leave the 1.5 headroom.**

Here's the maths from the seller's side. Profit divided by 1.5, the cover rule, is what the business can safely pay in debt each year.

Say a bad year cuts profit by 30 per cent, to $175 thousand. At $166 thousand of payments, the business still pays you.

At $250 thousand of payments, it's $75 thousand short, and the business is in default in year one.

As a buyer, the 1.5 protected you. Now it protects you as the seller.

Squeeze the payments up to every dollar of profit and the first bad year breaks the business, and your money with it.

**The terms you'll want as a seller.**

As a buyer I got no deposit and a nine-month holiday. Your managers will ask for the same. Decide in advance what you'll trade it for.

One. Security over the business, so you can step back in if payments stop.

Two. Quarterly reporting, at least.

Three. A default clause: what happens if a payment is missed, and how long they have to fix it.

Four. Covenants: a minimum level of cover, and limits on new debt, so they can't borrow ahead of you.

If a bank lends part of it, the bank gets paid first and your note sits behind it. That's the subordination clause you accepted as a buyer.

**Build your buyer years out.**

First, name the person. Ideally a GM hired one to two years before you want to go, who already owns a whole part of the business, so owning all of it is a smaller step.

Then give them a small stake early, vesting over time, so they think like an owner.

Once they're ready, the bank will want what it wanted from you: financials, a model with a bad-year case, the cover rule.

A proper data room does most of that.

And help them build their own credibility: an accountant, a lawyer, a small board. It's the 3C model, pointed the other way.

**Here's the maths.**

$250 thousand of profit divided by 1.5 is about $166 thousand a year in payments. Over ten years, that's about $1.66 million to you, with about $84 thousand of headroom left each year.

That's well above what many cash buyers would pay for a business this size. The $84 thousand of headroom is what keeps it paying in a bad year.

One. Squeezing payments to 100 per cent of profit. First bad year, they default and you're owed money by a broken business.

Three. Picking the buyer in the last year. No track record of running it without you, so the bank won't lend.

Four. Selling on a handshake to a loyal manager. Loyalty isn't a covenant.
