## 100 enquiries, 40 booked: where the other 60 go (step-by-step)

A business you buy comes with an enquiry flow nobody has measured.

A clinic or a trade is a great business. Steady work. People need you.

Until the phone rings more than anyone can answer. Then it goes from booked out to busy, and somehow not growing.

This is for owners and buyers of service businesses: clinics, trades, local services. It isn't for a business that's already at capacity. Fix capacity first.

Speed to lead means one thing: every enquiry gets a human reply in minutes, not hours, on every channel it came in on.

There are four stops on the way from first ring to booked job. The door, the first five minutes, the hours, and the chase.

If a business is slow to answer, it has nothing to do with lazy staff, or a bad receptionist, or impatient customers.

It has everything to do with one thing. Nobody owns the number.

Now the detail.

**The door.**

Map every way a customer can reach the business. Phone, website form, email, Google messages, Facebook, Instagram, the after-hours voicemail.

Next to each, write who answers it and how fast. Most small businesses have at least one door nobody owns.

Then mystery-shop it. Call at 7:30 on a Tuesday night. Fill in the form on a Sunday. Message the Google profile. Time every reply.

**The rule.**

Every door has a name next to it. No name, no door. Shut it, or give it to someone.

**The first five minutes.**

Three touches. An automatic text the second the form lands, so they know a human saw it.

An email with what happens next. Then a real phone call.

Give it one owner. A boring business already has a receptionist, an office manager, or the owner's partner doing the books.

Give one of them first response and one number: minutes to first contact.

Speed you don't measure drifts back to "I'll call them after this job".

**The hours.**

Enquiries arrive when customers are free. Lunch, evenings, Sunday night. Not when the office is open.

In a trade it's worse. The person who should answer is on a roof.

So move the hours, not the headcount. An office person on 11 to 7 instead of 8 to 4. An after-hours answering service.

In a clinic, someone clearing online booking requests in the evening, because that's when patients sit down and search.

Pull a month of enquiry times and count them by hour. The answer is usually obvious.

If a third of enquiries land after 5pm and nobody answers until 9am, that third is going to whoever picks up first.

Speed doesn't mean ringing someone at 9pm. Calling-hour and privacy rules exist. Build them into the system.

**The chase.**

Some enquire and don't book. Chase on a fixed rhythm: the same day, then once a day for a few days, then a monthly check-in.

Cancels and no-shows get the same. Every message carries a link to rebook, because without one people cancel and never come back.

Don't overdo it. A 70-year-old physio patient doesn't want eight emails. One text, one reminder, one call.

A customer who replies "yep, see you Thursday" turns up. One who goes silent needs a call.

## 5 mistakes that let quotes die after you buy a business

Most quotes don't lose on price. They die in silence.

How come a business with good reviews, fair prices and a solid team still loses jobs it should win?

The quote goes out, and nobody calls again. The job goes to whoever followed up.

In a trade sending 100 quotes a month, five more wins is about $40,000 a month of revenue, from quotes you've already paid to produce.

Here are the five mistakes that kill them, worst last.

In a lot of small businesses the owner is the sales process. He knows who to ring back and what to say.

When he retires, it walks out with him, and your profit drops in year one while you're paying him.

This is for owners who've hit the ceiling of what they can sell themselves, and buyers taking over from a founder.

It isn't for anyone who wants pressure tactics. They don't work on a 60-year-old getting a quote for a new roof.

A sales process is the written path from enquiry to deposit, simple enough that someone other than the owner can run it.

Five mistakes break it. If quotes are dying, it has nothing to do with your price, your workmanship or "customers these days".

It has everything to do with nobody ever writing it down.

Now the detail.

**Mistake 1. The process lives in one head.**

You were told good work sells itself. You were told selling is pushy. You were told if they want it, they'll call.

None of that's true. The businesses that win more jobs aren't better tradespeople. They ask better questions, make one clear recommendation and ring back.

So write the stages on one page: enquiry, triage, visit, quote, follow-up, deposit.

But sell it yourself first. As the new owner, go on 20 quote visits or sit in on 20 consults.

Write down every question, objection and reason someone said no. Then write the page.

The page is short on purpose. If it runs past one page, nobody reads it, and the team goes back to doing it their own way.

Saul, an Owners Academy member, is in early due diligence on an electrical and aircon business. 25 staff, about $3.8 million in revenue, owner retiring in two years.

**Mistake 2. Pitching instead of asking.**

Five questions do most of the work. At the kitchen bench, for a ducted aircon quote.

One. "What made you call us now?" Broad gets you the real reason.

Two. "Is it just you deciding?" Partner, landlord, body corporate.

Book the visit when both partners are home and "I need to talk to my wife" goes away.

Three. "What's it costing you?" The power bill. The bedroom nobody sleeps in over summer.

Four. "Have you had anyone else out?" Either they haven't, or something didn't sit right.

Five. "When do you need it done?" Last. Asked early, it sounds like pressure.

The customer does most of the talking. By the time you quote, they've told you why they need it, what it's costing them and who signs off.

The quote just confirms what they already said.

**The rule.**

No price until you've heard the problem in their words.

**Mistake 3. Leaving without a decision.**

You're the expert. Say what you'd do, give the price plainly, ask for the go-ahead. No fake urgency.

If they say yes, take the signature and the deposit there and then. More jobs are lost to "I'll sign it tonight" than are won in follow-up.

In a clinic the same bones look different. A physio doesn't sell, they recommend care.

But the next appointment gets booked before the patient leaves the room. A lot of clinics lose patients after visit one simply because nobody booked visit two.

## Salary vs pay on cash collected: your first salesperson inside a 1.5 DSCR deal

Your first salesperson can break the deal before they sell a thing.

There are two types of owners. The ones who still quote every job at night, and the ones who hired someone and paid them for cash in the bank.

On a business making $250,000 a year and paying the seller about $166,000, there's $84,000 of room. A $90,000 salary eats all of it.

Here's salary versus pay on cash collected, a test for when to hire, and my verdict.

In a vendor-financed deal, the cash flow that pays a new hire is the same cash flow paying the seller.

DSCR is free cash flow divided by debt service. Keep it at 1.5 or above.

The 1.5 DSCR rule applies every month, not just at settlement. Get this hire wrong and you're late on the seller in month six.

Get it right and you stop being the business's only salesperson, which is what holds its multiple down.

This is for owners whose quote calendar is full. It isn't for someone hoping a hire fixes a business that can't deliver.

Pay for cash in the bank, not for turning up.

Two options. Option A, a salary. Option B, a smaller base plus commission on cash collected, taken back if the job is refunded.

First, a self-test for whether you're ready to hire at all.

If your last hire didn't work, it had nothing to do with being a bad judge of people, or "nobody wants to work".

It had everything to do with the setup: no target, no trial, and pay that didn't depend on results.

I help owners buy businesses with little to no money down, then make them run without the owner doing everything, sales included. Book a call below.

Now the detail.

**The self-test.**

Three questions. One: have you, or a manager, sold it yourselves and written the process down on one page?

Two: are there more quote requests than you can get to? Three: do you know what a good month looks like in dollars?

Three yeses, you're ready. Any no, you're not.

Without selling it yourself, you can't set a target, you can't judge a trial, and you can't know what to pay.

And the target comes from your own month. If you sold $60,000 of work last month, that's the yardstick.

Expect a new hire to do about half of you at first. Half of you, while you work on the business instead of in it, is a good trade.

**Option A: salary.**

A salary is a fixed cost. You pay it in the month they sell nothing, and month one is usually that month.

On our illustrative deal, cover goes from 1.5 to below 1.0 before the first job comes in. That's a deal paying the seller out of your savings.

Salary does buy one thing: loyalty from someone who won't take a commission-heavy role.

If you go this way, size the debt for it in the deal model, before you settle.

**Option B: base plus cash collected.**

A smaller base, plus commission paid when the cash lands. Part when the deposit clears, the rest when the final invoice is paid.

If the job's cancelled and refunded, the commission comes back.

Cash collected, not jobs signed. A signed job that cancels has paid you nothing, so it pays them nothing.

That one rule lines their month up with your DSCR.

Use tiers, so the more they sell the better the rate. A flat amount per job lets people hit a comfortable number and coast.

And never change the plan mid-stream. Your best sellers leave first.

Before I knew this, I'd have assumed you need cash to attract good people. At 25 I had none.

I built my board by offering founders' equity instead of fees. Good people took upside over a fixed cheque.

Pay that grows with results attracts people who expect results.

**Who, and the trial.**

Hire someone who's already sold in your trade or a close one. Don't hire hopefuls you have to train.

Find them like customers: ask your suppliers, your accountant, your best tradespeople.

Hiring from a competitor is fine within the law. Hire the skill, never the customer list.

Everyone sounds great in an interview. So the trial is real work: the first 30 quotes, with a pass mark set from your own numbers.

Judge them on revenue per enquiry, not close rate. Close rate is easy to game by only chasing the easy ones.

In a clinic, be careful. I wouldn't put a front-desk coordinator on commission for treatment sold.

Measure that role on speed to lead, bookings and shows.

Here's the maths. Option A, a $90,000 salary. Option B, a $40,000 base plus 8% of cash collected. Same $250,000 of profit, same $166,000 to the seller.

Before they sell anything, cover is 0.96 on the salary and 1.27 on the base. If they collect $900,000, the salary pays them $90,000 and Option B pays them $112,000. Illustrative, not a forecast.

Option B costs you $50,000 less if they sell nothing, and pays them more if they sell a lot. That's the trade you want.

**Where people lose money.**

One. A friend of a friend on a big salary in month one, no target, no trial, no clawback.

Two. Hiring before you've sold it yourself, then paying for a guess.

Three. Paying on jobs signed, then paying again when the job cancels.

Four. Putting a clinic coordinator on commission and getting a call from the regulator.

**My verdict.**

Option B, built into the deal model before you settle. Base small enough that cover stays above the line if they sell nothing for a quarter.

And your market: if every good estimator in your trade expects a salary, you may have to pay one.

Then pay less base, add a bonus on cash, and size the debt for it. Hire two if the cash flow allows, so you can compare them.

The cheapest lead is often the most expensive one you'll buy.

Five levels. Each one has to work before you climb to the next.

If ads have burned you before, it had nothing to do with your industry or your budget. It had everything to do with being sold the wrong number.

**Level 1. Strangers buy.**

Before a dollar on ads: when a stranger calls, do you win the job?

If you only win work from mates of existing customers, ads will only make that louder.

When ads fail, the cause is usually the offer, not the ad. Fix the offer first.

**Level 2. The ceiling.**

Work out the most you'll pay to win one customer, before you spend.

I work it off gross profit, not revenue, because in a trade materials and labour eat a big chunk of every job.

Lifetime value, times gross margin, gives lifetime gross profit. I cap acquisition cost at about a quarter of that.

Win one in four quotes, and that's $250 a quote. Half of enquiries book a quote, so $125 an enquiry. Illustrative, not a forecast.

Lifetime value is install plus servicing plus the next replacement. Use what the business's records show, not what you hope.

If the seller can't tell you what a customer is worth over five years, pull the customer list and work it out in due diligence.

**Level 3. Search, for urgent jobs.**

A blocked drain or a dead aircon in January, people search. They're comparing two or three firms right now.

Google search ads and your Google Business profile matter here. You're paying for intent, and you're competing on speed and reviews.

Search clicks cost more, but the person is ready to buy today.

For an urgent trade, this is usually the first dollar I'd spend. In a tight radius, on the jobs with the best margin, not every job the business does.

**Level 4. Meta, for planned jobs.**

A new kitchen, solar, a ducted system. People often don't know they're in the market yet.

That's where Facebook and Instagram work, in a radius around the business.

And the market has moved. On Meta now, the ad itself does most of the targeting.

The platform reads what's in it to decide who sees it. Fiddling with interests matters less.

So the ad should sound like your customers. Name the suburb. Show the van. Show the actual team.

Say what the job is, never who the person is. No "struggling with…" call-outs. In health, advertising rules are stricter again.

**Level 5. Measured to cash.**

Every enquiry gets a source: a tracking number per channel, a source field on the form, and "how did you hear about us?" at the counter.

Once a week, one table by channel: spend, enquiries, quotes booked, jobs won, cash collected.
