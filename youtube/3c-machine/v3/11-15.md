## A $1.66M Offer on $250K Profit (Cash Buyer vs Paid Over Time)

How come the buyer with less money can pay the seller more?

A business making $250 thousand a year in profit sells to a cash buyer for about a million.

The same business can carry an offer of about $1.66 million, paid over time, out of its own profit.

Not your money. The business's.

Here's the two-minute maths, and the three places it lies to you.

That gap is the whole of Capital. The cash route needs a deposit, a bank's yes and months of waiting.

The paid-over-time route has the seller fund the deal. You bring nothing, and you still put the biggest number on the table.

This is for owners, executives and people with capital who want a cash-flowing business without tying up their own money.

If you want a four-hour week, this isn't for you.

One line: don't ask what a business is worth. Ask what it can pay.

Two offers for the same business. Option A, the cash buyer. Option B, paid over time.

Then a test that tells you which one you can actually make.

And if you've spent years thinking you need a deposit first, that has nothing to do with your salary or your savings.

It has everything to do with a question nobody taught you to ask.

I help owners build these structures and put them in front of sellers. Book a call below. Now the detail.

**Option A. The cash buyer.**

A cash buyer pays three to five times profit. On $250 thousand, that's $750 thousand to $1.25 million. Call it a million.

To pay it, they need a deposit and a bank. The bank takes six to sixteen weeks.

The seller waits, and the deal can still die in credit.

For a retiring owner that's the worst of both. A lower price, and a slow close with no certainty.

**Option B. Paid over time.**

Three steps. I call it the speed run.

One. Real profit. Say $250 thousand.

Two. Divide by 1.5. About $166 thousand a year. That's the most this business should pay in debt each year.

The other $84 thousand stays in the business as a buffer.

Three. Multiply by the term. Ten years at $166 thousand is $1.66 million. About 6.6 times profit.

You've just outbid the cash buyer by about $660 thousand. And you can close in two to four weeks, not six to sixteen.

Now, you were told you need a deposit, a track record and a bank's permission. That's not how retiring owners sell.

My first deal was a physio clinic. I was 25, unemployed, no savings.

100% vendor finance, seven years at 4%, a nine-month payment holiday, rent in arrears, no deposit. I pulled $150 thousand out in year one.

The seller got paid by the business. Not by me.

**The test. Three checks before you say a number.**

The speed run is a ceiling, not a price. Three checks turn it into an offer.

**Check one. The profit is real.**

Every seller's profit number is a story. Some tell it high, some tell it low, most don't know it.

Get three years of financials, the matching tax returns, and revenue by financial year.

If the financials and the tax returns don't match, that's not a negotiation. It's a walk.

Then normalise it. Add back what the owner runs through the business that you won't. Take out a proper wage for whoever runs it after you.

Say the books show $280 thousand. Add $20 thousand of the owner's personal costs. Take out a $50 thousand manager. Real profit, $250 thousand.

Skip the manager and you'd use $300 thousand, and offer $2 million for a business that can only carry $1.66 million.

That's how people buy a job with a debt attached.

**Check two. Interest.**

The $166 thousand is principal and interest together. At 4% over ten years, it repays about $1.35 million of price.

That's your real number. Still above any cash offer.

**Check three. The levers.**

Term, rate, a payment holiday before the first instalment, and sometimes a balloon.

My good friend Cam did two deals in 23 days, both 100% vendor finance.

Interest-only for ten years, with balloons in years three and ten.

Interest-only keeps the payments small. The balloon is the principal in one lump.

Never sign one you haven't planned to pay, from cash you've built up or a bank refinance once you have a track record.

Option B wins when three things are true.

One. The profit survives normalising. Two. The value is big enough to be worth the work, roughly a million and up. Three. The seller can wait to be paid.

If they need every dollar on day one, you blend with a bank.

Self-test. Can you name this business's real profit, why the seller is selling, and how long they can wait for their money?

If not, you're not ready to make either offer.

One more rule. Never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time".

Something like: "I can get you more than a cash buyer would, paid over time out of the business you built, and we can close in weeks, not months."

Even after interest you're about $100 thousand above the top of the cash range. And the seller's money is doing the funding.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Using the seller's profit instead of the normalised one. On these numbers that's about $330 thousand overpaid.

Two. Opening with the ceiling. You've got nowhere to move, and the seller anchors on your top number.

Three. Signing a balloon with no refinance plan. The deal you won becomes the debt you can't pay.

## Every DSCR From 0.96× to 2× (and Why I Sign at 1.5)

Two buyers look at the same business making $300 thousand a year.

James wants it, so he stretches the debt until the seller's price fits. Chad won't sign below 1.5.

A year later, one big customer leaves. James is topping the business up from his own pocket. Chad's capital is still his.

Same business. One number. Here's every level of it, from 0.96 to 2.

If you've got capital, this number decides whether a business pays you or you pay it.

Get it wrong and the downside isn't a lower return. It's writing cheques into a business you bought to receive them.

This is for people who want to own businesses for the long run.

If you're trying to squeeze every last dollar of debt out of a deal, this will annoy you. Good.

DSCR, debt service coverage ratio: the cash the business makes before debt, divided by every debt payment you owe in a year.

Bank, seller, equipment finance, all of it.

There are four levels. 0.96, 1.2, 1.5 and 2.

Most stretched deals sit on level two and look fine until the first bad year.

And if you've struggled to tell a safe deal from a dangerous one, it has nothing to do with your intelligence or how long you've invested.

It has everything to do with what you're shown. Sellers and brokers show you the good year. Nobody hands you the bad one.

I help owners and investors structure deals that survive their worst year. Book a call below. Now the levels.

**One term first. The covenant.**

When a bank lends, it writes a covenant into the loan: a minimum DSCR, tested regularly.

Every lender sets its own, so ask. For this video, say 1.25.

Drop below it and you're in breach. And a breach hands the lender rights over your business you don't want them to have.

So here's how most people think about a deal. They think it's safe if it pays in an average year.

That's not how it works. A deal is safe if it still pays in its worst year.

All the levels below use the same business. $300 thousand profit in the base case.

A bad year is profit down 20%, to $240 thousand. One customer leaves, or one key person walks.

**Level 1. 0.96 times. The business can't pay its own debt.**

James's deal. He stretches to $250 thousand a year of payments on $300 thousand of profit.

In the bad year, $240 thousand comes in and $250 thousand goes out.

The business can't cover its own debt. He's $10 thousand short, in breach, and explaining himself to a bank.

That's the bottom of the ladder, and you get there from level two with one bad customer.

**Level 2. 1.2 times. Fine on a good year.**

Same deal, base case. $300 thousand over $250 thousand is 1.2. On a good year it pays, just.

It's already under a 1.25 covenant, so a bank won't touch it.

It only gets done because the seller carries the debt without stress-testing it. Any dip and it's level one.

This is where most stretched deals sit. They start with the seller's price and work backwards until the debt fits.

Then the add-backs grow and the bad year gets gentler, until a 1.2 looks like a 1.5.

**Level 3. 1.5 times. The floor.**

$200 thousand of payments on $300 thousand. For every dollar you owe, a dollar fifty comes in.

The real number is headroom: how far profit can fall before you breach.

At 1.5 with a 1.25 covenant, profit can fall to $250 thousand. About 17%.

A 20% bad year takes you to 1.2. Still a breach.

So 1.5 is the floor in the base case, not the target. It's the least that gives you a chance.

**Level 4. 2 times. Change the structure, not the spreadsheet.**

Say the debt is a $1.2 million seller note at 4%. Over seven years, about $200 thousand a year. That's level three.

Ask for ten years instead. About $148 thousand a year. Same price. Same seller.

Base DSCR about 2. Bad year about 1.6. You've bought a bad year just by asking for a longer term.

Other levers do the same job. A payment holiday at the start.

An interest-only period, though watch interest cover, profit divided by interest alone, and the year the principal comes due.

Part of the price as an earn-out. Or the seller rolling part of their value into shares.

**The rule.**

1.5 in the base case. Above the covenant in the bad year.

If the bad year fails, change the structure. Never the numbers.

Build the bad year from the actual business. Take out the biggest customer. Price in replacing the one person everyone relies on.

Look at the worst of the last three years.

Then add year-one takeover costs: systems, lawyers, a manager overlapping with the old owner.

Self-test. If you can't name the biggest customer and the DSCR without them, you're not ready to sign.

And once you own it, check one thing every month. Cash collected, minus costs, minus debt payments.

Track DSCR on the last twelve months. If it slides from 1.6 to 1.4, you see it months before the bank does.
