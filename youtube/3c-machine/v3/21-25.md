## Never name a price before these 3 documents (the $600K gap)

There are two types of business buyers. The ones who read the tax returns before they name a price.

And the ones who pay four times profit for profit that was never there.

Illustrative numbers. The P&L says $500 thousand. The tax return says $350 thousand.

At four times, that gap is $600 thousand of price.

So here's my rule. No documents, no number.

And the way a seller sends them tells you more than anything they say on the phone.

The first number you say becomes the anchor for the whole deal.

Say it too early and every later conversation is you climbing down from it, or the seller holding you to it.

This is for people who'll put serious hours into buying a business and want to pay for profit that exists.

It isn't for anyone who wants to fall in love with a business on the first call.

Price is the last thing you discuss, not the first. Three documents, one test, then a number.

If you've talked price too early before, it has nothing to do with being naive or bad with numbers. It has everything to do with the order you were shown.

Listings put the asking price at the top and the documents after you're attached.

Most buyers think naming a number early shows you're serious. That's not how it works.

A number without documents shows the seller you'll pay for a story. The serious move is the request.

Now the detail.

**Three years of financials.**

Profit and loss plus balance sheet, for three years.

One year can be a fluke: a big contract, a bad winter, an insurance payout. Three shows the pattern.

The P&L tells you what the business earns. The balance sheet tells you what it owes: tax, staff entitlements, equipment loans.

Those become your problem on settlement day.

Illustrative: a $200 thousand tax debt is $200 thousand off your price, or the seller's to clear before settlement.

Either is fine. Finding it after you've signed isn't.

Ask for accountant-prepared statements, not management accounts. Management accounts are where the optimistic numbers live.

**Matching tax returns.**

This is the one that sets the price.

The financials are what the owner tells you. The tax return is what the owner told the government.

Banks lend against what can be proven.

If the two don't match, one of two things is true. The owner under-reported to the tax office, or over-reported to you.

Neither is a business you pay the higher number for.

And "we run a few things through cash" is income no bank will lend against. The business is worth what it can prove.

When the owner asks why you need returns, tell them straight.

"The bank lends against the returns. If I want to pay you properly, I need them."

**Revenue by financial year.**

Split the way the business earns it: by service line, location or customer group. In healthcare, billings by practitioner.

Illustrative. One business goes $2.4 million, $2.7 million, $3 million. Another goes $3 million, $2.7 million, $2.4 million.

Same three-year total. One is growing, one is shrinking, and the owner of the shrinking one will quote you last year's profit.

The split also shows where the risk sits. If the retiring owner bills half the revenue, half the revenue retires with them.

And read gross margin across the same three years.

Revenue up with margin slipping means the owner has been buying growth with price. You'd be paying a multiple on a trend that's about to turn.

**The NDA test.**

Send a confidentiality agreement within a day of the "maybe".

Once it's signed, send one paragraph: three years of financials, matching tax returns, revenue by year, straight from the accountant.

Then count the days.

Serious sellers send in a day or two and start asking about your timeline. Curious ones take weeks and only want to talk price.

They don't get deleted. They go back on the follow-up list, and your week goes to the ones who sent.

## Every layer of due diligence, from $500K "adjusted" to $400K real

How come investors who'd never buy a share without reading the annual report will buy a whole business on the seller's adjusted number?

Illustrative. "Adjusted" profit, $500 thousand. Real profit, $400 thousand.

At four times, that's $400 thousand paid for nothing.

Due diligence is the last point where you can still change the price.

Here are the five layers between the seller's number and the real one.

You priced the business at a multiple of profit. Every dollar you prove or disprove now moves the price by three to five.

This is for people with capital who want it in a business whose profit is proven.

It isn't for anyone who'd rather skip the boring work and trust the seller.

Due diligence is about the quality of earnings.

Is the profit real, will it stay after the owner leaves, and how much cash does it take to run.

If you've been burnt before, it has nothing to do with being careless. It has everything to do with who wrote the number.

The adjusted figure is written by the one person who benefits from it being high.

Most buyers think diligence is about finding reasons to say yes. That's not how it works.

It's where you find out what you're buying, and reprice it.

I help investors and owners run this and take the findings back to the seller. Book a call below.

Now the layers.

**Level 1. Reported profit.**

The tax returns, matched against the financials. This is the floor: the profit the business has proven to the government.

Everything above it has to earn its place.

If the seller's pitch starts above this line, your diligence starts here.

**Level 2. Adjusted profit.**

Add-backs are the owner's adjustments to show what a new owner would earn.

Some are fair: a personal car through the books, a genuine one-off legal bill. Test every one.

The "one-off" legal bill that shows up every year isn't one-off.

The owner adding back his own salary forgets someone has to do his job, and that person costs money.

Rule: an add-back survives only if a stranger could justify it with paperwork.

Ask for the invoice, the payslip, the contract. No paper, no add-back.

Each $10 thousand that fails is $40 thousand off a four-times price.

**Level 3. Durable profit.**

Profit that stays after you settle. Two checks.

Concentration. Revenue by customer.

One or two customers over about 30% is a red flag. One over half, you walk or price it heavily.

Check their contracts can be assigned to you. And look at the share across all three years.

A customer that's gone from 15% to 35% is a concentration problem in the making.

Source. Where does each dollar come from: repeat customers, referrals, a contract, or the owner's golf mates?

If the honest answer is "the owner's relationships", that revenue might leave with him.

**Level 4. Cash profit.**

Working capital: the cash tied up in stock and unpaid invoices that pays wages before customers pay.

If the seller strips the cash before settlement and you put it back, that's a deposit by another name.

Agree the level in the contract and line up the working capital facility before close.

Watch debtors too. If customers have been taking longer to pay each year, profit can look fine while cash runs dry.

**Level 5. People.**

The financials tell you what happened. The staff tell you what's about to.

Interview key people before you settle, with the seller's agreement.

Ask what they'd change, who customers actually call, who's thinking of leaving.

If the business runs on two or three people, put retention bonuses in the deal, say at 90 days and one year.

Often it isn't the owner the business runs on. It's the office manager of fifteen years.

The worst finding is the one you get on day three, when your best technician resigns.

**Who does what.**

Your accountant runs quality of earnings. Your lawyer covers contracts, lease, licences, employment and disputes.

The operator on your board walks the site. You run the timeline, 90 days at the outside.

Now the detail.

**Option A. Share sale.**

A share purchase agreement, an SPA, means you buy the company itself.
