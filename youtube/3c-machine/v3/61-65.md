## Every group structure, from one company to a $1M-profit holdco

One business is easy to own. You know every number and every person.

Until you buy the second one. Then a structure you never thought about decides two numbers: how much the bank lends you, and what a buyer pays for the lot.

In the example I'll run today, that's the gap between $4 million and $8 million. Same businesses. Different paperwork.

This is for owners who plan to own more than one business. If you'll only ever own one, a single company may be fine, and your accountant can tell you.

Everyone else: the structure is cheap to set up before deal two and expensive to fix after deal four. It's also the first thing a lender and a buyer's diligence team look at.

The whole idea in one line: one deal, one company, with a holding company on top.

There are four levels of structure, and most owners are stuck on level two without knowing it.

If your first business went into whatever company was handy, that has nothing to do with how careful you are. It has everything to do with timing.

Nobody plans for deal five while they're scrambling to close deal one.

I help owners set this up, from a first deal with little to no money down to a group a buyer will pay a premium for. Book a call below.

Now the detail.

**Level 1: the partnership.**

You and the business are the same thing legally. Every debt and every claim can reach you.

Rule: don't buy a business this way. Ever.

**Level 2: one company.**

You own a company, the company owns the business. Fine for one deal.

The trap is deal two. Buy the second business into the same company and both now share one set of debts, one set of books and one set of risks.

The bank sees one pile and takes security over the whole pile. A bad year in business two can now reach business one.

Most people think structure is admin you sort out after the deal. That's not how it works.

Lenders price your structure. Buyers price it. It's part of the deal.

**Level 3: holdco plus one company per business.**

This is where most groups should live.

A holding company sits on top. It's the permanent vehicle.

Your board's founders' equity sits here. Every business you buy goes into its own new company underneath, with its own bank account, its own lender and its own seller note.

Why the lender cares: they want to see this business's profit paying this business's debt.

Ring-fenced, they can lend to that company alone and take security over its assets, not over everything you own.

Don't sign a guarantee where one company stands behind another's debt unless your lawyer has read it. That one clause turns five ring-fenced companies back into one pile.

Why the seller cares: in a vendor-finance deal, the seller's note sits in the company that owns their old business, limited to that company where you can get it, and behind the bank.

Your lawyer will call that non-recourse and subordination. Ask them to show you both on your own structure.

Why the buyer cares: clean pieces. A buyer can take one business or all of them.

Tangled, their diligence team spends months unpicking shared accounts, and every question they can't answer comes off the price.

**Level 4: the octopus.**

Under the holdco you run arms. One per region or one per industry, each with its own manager, its own businesses, sometimes its own lenders.

Lose an arm, the animal lives.

You won't need it for a while. The rule is to build level three so it can grow into level four without being torn down.

**Day-one settings.**

Two things to set up before anyone else owns a share.

Issue plenty of shares. A million, not a hundred.

It's far easier to hand a board member 10 thousand out of a million than to reissue later.

Set minority rules from the start. Drag-along, so a minority can't block a sale of the group.

No veto and no voting control for minorities. Board members, sellers rolling over equity and managers all sign up to the same agreement.

That's how I've done it. My board sits at the top.

The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is a shareholder. I keep 73% of my company.

The multiple gap is the roll-up. Buy at around 4 times, combine and grow, sell the group at 8, 10, sometimes 15.

It only shows up if a buyer can see clean, comparable numbers. The structure is how you keep them that way.

Private equity is the buyer at the top of that ladder.

## Centralise vs keep local: the $50K back-office decision

Three businesses. Three bookkeepers, three payroll systems, three insurance brokers.

How come owners with three good businesses often feel busier than when they had one?

Because they're paying for the same work three times, and they're the glue holding it together.

In a three-business group, fixing that is worth about $50 thousand a year. At exit, about $300 thousand. Customers never notice.

This is for people who own two or more businesses, or are about to.

The saving looks small at three. At five it's real money, because the same shared team absorbs each new business for a small extra cost.

And at sale, a buyer sees one finance function and one set of books instead of five little kingdoms.

If you're hoping a back office will fix a business that doesn't make money, it won't. Fix that first.

One line: centralise what customers never feel, keep local what they pay for.

So every function in your group is Option A, centralise, or Option B, keep local. I'll give you both, the test, and the order.

If your back office is a mess right now, that has nothing to do with discipline.

It has everything to do with how you got here: one deal at a time, each business arriving with its own setup.

I help owners build groups like this, starting from a first business bought with little to no money down. Book a call below.

Now the detail.

**Option A: centralise.**

You were told every business needs its own bookkeeper, its own office manager doing payroll, its own broker.

That was never a rule. It's how a standalone business has to run. Inside a group, it's a choice.

Centralise anything that's the same job in every business. Bookkeeping. Payroll. HR admin. Insurance. Compliance. Software. Buying.

One accounting system. One chart of accounts, so wages are wages and rent is rent in every business.

One person or firm closing the month for all of them. That matters more than the saving.

Until every business reports the same way, you can't compare their margins, and you can't see which one is quietly losing money.

One payroll, with one set of contracts and policies, adjusted where the law or your industry's award needs it. One broker.

Group buying on fuel, consumables, phones and software seats. Suppliers give better terms to a bigger customer, and now you are one.

**Option B: keep local.**

Keep anything a customer would notice changing. The person who answers the phone. The tech on the tools. The clinician. The local name on the van.

That's what you bought. Don't rename a 40-year-old local business after your holdco because it looks tidy on a slide.

Centralise the engine behind marketing if you like, one person running ads and reviews for every business, but the customer still sees the local name.

**The test.**

Ask one question of every function: would a customer notice if this changed tomorrow?

No: centralise it. Yes: leave it local.

Then a second question for anything you centralise: will the person who uses the tool help pick it?

Let the people who'll use a system test two options before you buy. Don't put the fanciest software on a 12-person plumbing business because it looked good in a demo.

The usual pushback is "my businesses are too different to share anything." Every business pays wages, does its books and buys insurance. Start there.

The other one is "my staff will hate it." Some will, if you do it in one go without telling them. Do it one function at a time and explain why.

**The order.**

Books first. You can't run a group whose numbers arrive in five different shapes.

Then payroll and HR, where staff notice change, so tell them what's changing and what isn't before you do it.

Then insurance and buying. Marketing last, and carefully.

Verdict: both, in that order, one function at a time. Not before you have two businesses running steadily. Never all four in the same month.

**Here's the maths.**

$50 thousand back in profit, plus one set of numbers you can trust.

And notice it isn't huge on day one. That's normal. The bigger win comes at business four and five.

A fourth business on its own would need its own bookkeeper and payroll. Inside the group, the same team takes it on with a small top-up, not a new hire.

Every dollar it doesn't spend on back office is a dollar of profit, and every dollar of profit is six at exit.

Groups often charge this through a management fee from the holdco. It has to cover real services at a fair price, and how it's taxed depends on where you are.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Centralising everything in one month. Cost: every business stops working properly for a month, and staff leave.

Two. Buying software because a friend uses it. Cost: a migration, then a rollback, paid twice.

Three. Taking the one competent office manager out without a plan. Cost: the hundred small things she did that nobody wrote down.

Four. Stripping the local touch. Cost: the customers you paid for when you bought the business.

**What to do.**

You can carry on as you are. Keep paying for the same work three times. Keep reconciling three sets of books on a Sunday.

Keep a group that, at sale time, looks like five small businesses in a trench coat.

Or you can fix it one function at a time, in the right order.

## This $150K bolt-on adds $1.2M to the group (teardown)

A business making $150 thousand a year, owner retiring, nobody else bidding.

There's a well-kept secret in buying businesses: a lot of a group's value is built with deals like this. Small, boring and ignored.

One line: buy small at a low multiple, make it part of a bigger business, and the bigger business sells at a higher one.

A bolt-on is a small business you add to a platform.

A platform is the business everything plugs into: a manager who isn't you, clean monthly numbers, and a back office with spare capacity.

Two common patterns. Buy the assets, not the shares, so old liabilities stay behind.

And if you're unsure how many customers stay once the owner leaves, tie part of the price to it: an earn-out on revenue from existing customers over the next year or two.

Where it sits, its own company under the holdco or folded into the platform, depends on the lender, the tax position and how you'll sell.

Decide that with your accountant and lawyer before the LOI.

It closes. It joins the shared back office. Its own bookkeeper, software and broker fall away. It picks up group buying.

Say that's $50 thousand of costs it no longer needs. It now makes $200 thousand.

The group goes from $1 million to $1.2 million.

You agree $450 thousand, paid out of the business's own profit, for something worth $1.2 million at exit.

None of it is guaranteed. The group multiple only shows up if the group is clean, growing and runs without you.

One. Paying the seller for synergies. Cost: your margin of safety, handed over at signing.

Two. Buying faster than the platform can absorb. Cost: integration falls behind, the manager drowns, the platform's profit slips.

Three. Drifting into another industry because the price looked cheap. Cost: no shared back office, no shared staff, no group multiple.

Four. Leaning on platform cash to cover a bolt-on below 1.5 times. Cost: one bad year in a small business hits the big one.

## 4 mistakes when buying your competitor (the $1.05M deal)

Two owners. Same town, same trade.

James runs more ads, sends more quotes and works more hours every year, and still wins half his jobs against the business across the road on price.

Lorenzo doesn't cut his price or work more hours. A few years ago he bought the business across the road.

In the deal I'll run today, that's $1.05 million, paid over time, for about $390 thousand a year of extra profit. And four ways to wreck it.

Your competitor is the one business you already know almost everything about. That makes it the easiest deal to value.

It also makes it the easiest to wreck, because they know you too.

This is for owners with a running business who've thought, "I should just buy that lot down the road."

If you want to buy them to punish them, stop. You'll overpay.

One line: approach carefully, price on their profit as it stands, and plan the people before you close.

Most owners think the way to beat a competitor is to outwork them.

In a boring local industry, with owners retiring and no successors, that's not how it works. Buying them gets cheap when you pay over time.

If you've been fighting the same rival for years, that has nothing to do with your effort. It has everything to do with a market that has two of you in it.

Here are the four mistakes, ranked by what they cost.

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down, including the one across the road. Book a call below.

Now the detail.

**Mistake 4: handing over your secrets.**

Competitors know each other. Same suppliers, same association, same gossip.

They'll take your call. They'll also wonder what you're after.

Be direct: "We've both been at this a while. Have you ever thought about selling? If you have, I'd like to be the first call."

Then protect yourself. Mutual NDA before numbers move either way.

Ask for their three years of financials, matching tax returns and revenue by year. Release yours in stages.

Before an LOI they need to know you can fund and close. They don't need your customer list, your pricing or your staff names.

A competitor who stalls on the NDA, then wants to "just have a coffee and compare notes", may be fishing.

In some industries and sizes, a competitor deal can raise competition-law questions. Ask your lawyer early.

**Mistake 3: losing the people.**

You'll have two of some roles. Two office managers. Two senior techs who both think they're the senior tech.

Decide before you close who stays and in which role. Meet their key people before settlement, with the seller's agreement.

For the ones you can't lose, a retention bonus at around 90 days and again at a year is common. Small next to losing the person every customer asks for.

Your staff are watching how you treat theirs. So is the town.

Then the culture. You're not just changing owners. You're merging two teams who spent years competing for the same jobs.

Pick one way of doing things, explain why, and give it time. The staff are most of what you're buying.

**Mistake 2: assuming every customer stays.**

Some customers used both of you on purpose, so they always had a second option. They'll find a new one.

Ask for revenue by customer under the NDA and match it against yours at the right stage, with your accountant handling the comparison if the seller's nervous.

Revenue from customers you already serve is worth less to you than it looks.

And if one big customer is a large share of both businesses, the merged business has a bigger concentration problem than either did alone.

**Mistake 1: paying for your own savings.**

This is the expensive one.

Price the deal on their profit as it stands. 3 to 5 times.

Count only the savings you control: one premises instead of two, one back office, one set of software and insurance.

Treat extra sales from cross-selling as a bonus. Never in the price, never in the lender's model.

Check cover on their numbers alone at 1.5 times, before any savings. The savings are your margin of safety, not your plan.

Paid over time, payment holiday if you can get it, its own company under your holdco.

And never say "vendor finance" to them. Say "getting paid over time".

A retiring owner who doesn't want to wait 16 weeks for a bank often prefers a buyer who knows the trade, will look after the staff, and can close in weeks.

If they won't move on price, your best tactic is the one most owners won't use: walk away. Second best is a time limit on the offer.

Still a good deal after the customer loss. But if you'd paid the seller for your $140 thousand of savings, it wouldn't be.

**Where people lose money.**

One. Numbers handed over at a coffee, nothing back. Cost: your pricing in their hands, no deal.

Two. Savings in the price. Cost: you pay twice for value you created.

Three. Every customer assumed to stay. Cost: a price built on revenue that leaves.

Four. Staff hear the news from the rumour mill. Cost: the senior tech walks, and his customers follow.

**What to do.**

You can keep quoting against them on every job for another 10 years.

Or you can plan the approach, the price and the people with someone who's done it, before you pick up the phone.

## Why the bank's best offer at deal three is the dangerous one

On deal one, nobody would lend to you. By deal three, the bank wants to lend to everything.

There are two types of owners who get there. The ones who use that offer to build the group, and the ones whose group gets tied in a knot by it.

In the example I'll run, one business losing $100 thousand in a bad year puts the whole group in breach. The difference is one clause.

This is for owners with two or more businesses, or one solid one and a pipeline.

The bank's group offer usually looks better on every line: bigger limit, lower rate, one facility.

The cost sits in the security clause, and most owners don't read it until something goes wrong. If you're still on deal one, get that deal done first.

One line: the rules don't change at deal three, but the bank's offer tries to change them.

Every deal still covers its own debt at 1.5 times, on its own numbers. Vendor finance is still the first tool.

What changes is that a lender will now look at the whole group, and that's a trade.

If it feels like you don't have the capital to buy more, that has nothing to do with your savings or your salary.

It has everything to do with what your businesses can now prove.

I help owners buy businesses with little to no money down and then finance the group as it grows, without tying it in knots. Book a call below.

Now the detail.

**Why the bank says yes now.**

You were told the bank decides how big you get, and that each new deal needs more of your own money.

That's not how a group gets funded. The businesses fund the businesses.

On deal one you borrowed credibility from a board.

By deal three you've got two businesses of monthly numbers, a lender who's watched you hit them, and sellers who've been paid on time. That's why deal three usually moves faster than deal one.

My friend Cam did two deals in 23 days, both 100 percent vendor finance, with $500 thousand of working capital from the bank.

That's what a lender relationship can do.

**What the offer costs.**

The bank says: "We'll refinance A and B and fund part of C in one facility. Lower rate, bigger limit. We just want security over all three, and the loans cross-default."

Cross-collateral means security over all your businesses.

Cross-default means if one business breaks its loan terms, every loan is treated as in default.

Separately, a bad year in C is C's problem. C's lender and C's seller talk to you about C. A and B keep running.

In one group facility, C's bad year is everyone's problem.

**How to take the money without the knot.**

First, every new deal stands on its own numbers anyway. If it only works because the bank lends across the group, it doesn't work.

Second, push back on security. Common fallbacks: security only over the business being funded, or security over named assets, not everything.

Or a higher cover covenant, say 1.75 times, in exchange for less security.

Third, check the seller notes. They usually sit behind the bank.

Make sure a group facility doesn't change that in a way the sellers' documents don't allow, and that a default in one place doesn't trigger defaults in your seller agreements.

Fourth, personal guarantees. On larger deals, over about $5 million, one often isn't needed, because the business's assets give the bank its security.

Typically. Every lender and deal is different. Ask again at every deal.

**Make lenders compete.**

Lenders lend on trust, and trust is a forecast you hit.

Every quarter, send each lender what you said each business would do, what it did, and why. A year of that and your next credit application half writes itself.
