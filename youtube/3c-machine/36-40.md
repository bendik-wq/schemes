**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Here's the whole answer in three points.

One. The seller is your first bank. A retiring owner who's done thirty years doesn't want to wait sixteen weeks for a lender. They want out, and they want to be looked after. So they let you pay them over time out of the business's own profit. That's vendor finance. You never call it that to their face. You say "getting paid over time."

Two. The business pays for itself, or you don't buy it. Free cash flow has to cover the repayments at least one and a half times. That's the 1.5 rule. If a deal can't clear it, the deal is wrong. Not your bank balance.

Three. The costs at the closing table get covered by the deal, not by you. Lawyers and accountants on deferred fees. A working capital line from the bank, drawn at settlement, pays them. Your board gets equity, not cash.

So "I don't have any money" isn't really a money problem. It's a structure problem. And structure is learnable.

One honest thing before the detail. Zero deposit doesn't mean zero cost. You pay in time, in energy, and in the equity you give your board. And it's easier and faster if you've got some cash, a business or property behind you. Possible without. Slower.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why the seller says yes (3:00)**

Start with the person everyone forgets. The seller.

People assume a seller wants all the cash today. A lot of them don't. There's a whole generation of owners in traditional industries ready to retire. Clinics, electrical firms, cleaning companies. Their problem isn't price. It's the burden of owning the thing.

If they sell the normal way, they wait 6 to 16 weeks for a bank, and they'll usually be offered 3 to 5 times profit.

Offer them getting paid over time and two things change. The price can go up, 6 to 7 times instead of 3 to 5, because they're being paid out of profit over years. And it can close in 2 to 4 weeks. For someone who wants out, speed and certainty can matter more than a cheque on day one.

[B-ROLL: an older owner locking up a workshop; paperwork spread on a kitchen table]

**Where every dollar comes from (4:15)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Let me show you with round numbers. This is illustrative. Not a real deal.

A business makes $250,000 a year in profit. Assume that's after paying someone to run it day to day, so you're not buying yourself a job.

Step one. What can it afford to pay the seller? Divide the profit by 1.5. That's about $166,000 a year.

Step two. Over ten years, that's about $1.66 million. Cash buyers would typically offer three to five times profit, so $750,000 to $1.25 million. Your offer, paid over time, is higher than most of what they'll see.

[SCREEN: "$250K ÷ 1.5 = ~$166K a year × 10 years = ~$1.66M"]

Step three. What do you bring on settlement day? Deposit: zero, because the seller carries the price. Lawyers and accountants: deferred to closing, then paid from a working capital line the bank gives you at settlement. Those lines are often around 10% of annual revenue. Say this business turns over $2 million. That's a line of around $200,000 to cover fees and give you runway.

Step four. What's left? $250,000 profit, minus $166,000 to the seller, leaves about $84,000 of headroom. That headroom is the point. It's what keeps you alive in a bad year.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

A hundred percent vendor finance isn't the only shape, either. A common blend is 60% from a commercial lender and 40% from the seller, rolled into equity or carried as a note. The bank's involvement validates the deal. The seller keeps skin in the game. My friend Jason Nagy did his first deal that way: a $5.5 million radiology business, nothing down.

**What "zero" doesn't cover (7:30)**

Let me be straight about the limits.

Zero deposit isn't zero risk. You're taking on debt. That's why the 1.5 rule isn't optional. Debt over dilution, yes, because you keep control and you keep the exit. But only inside 1.5.

Personal guarantees. On bigger deals, roughly over $5 million, banks often don't need one, because an asset-rich business gives them their security. Typically. Every lender and every deal is different.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

And small deals. Under about a million, there often isn't enough cash flow to pay the seller and pay you. Small isn't safer if it can't carry its own debt.

**What they're actually judging (3:00)**

Think about it from their side.

A bank is asking a narrower question. "Who's behind this, and have they done it before?"

Neither of them needs you to have done it before. They need someone in the room who has.

**How I did it (3:45)**

**Who to approach (4:30)**

The sweet spot is recently retired executives. People who ran something big, stepped away, and now miss being useful. Money isn't what they're short of. Meaning is.

So you don't pitch them a job. You pitch the vision and the ground floor. "I'm building a group that buys businesses in this industry. I want someone with your experience at the table from day one. There's founders' equity in it."

Keep the first message short. Three or four lines. Who you are, what you're building, why them specifically, and a request for twenty minutes. Don't attach a forty-page deck. Don't apologise for your age or your lack of experience. They'll see it. What they're judging is whether you're serious and whether you'll do the work.

And follow up. Busy, senior people miss messages. Silence isn't a no. Send a second and a third before you cross anyone off.

**Board first or deal first? (6:50)**

People ask which comes first. Either works. You can find a deal and build the team around it.

I recommend board first. An executive summary PDF, a handshake on equity, and then you go to market. Because the first time a seller asks "who's with you?", you want an answer.

**The packet (7:05)**

What goes on paper is simple. A one-page executive summary. A short thesis memo: the industry, why now, what you'll buy and how you'll fund it. The board, with real bios and real track records. An offer template. A diligence checklist.

When the board is built, you can write one line that does a lot of work. Something like "our board brings this many years of combined experience and this much in transactions." Use your real numbers only. Never round up.

**The bench (7:20)**

The second layer is your external team. A proper M&A lawyer. A good accounting firm that can do quality-of-earnings work, which means checking whether the profit the seller shows you is real and will still be there after you buy it.

**Then the banks (7:50)**

With a board and a bench, you go to lenders. Not as a beggar. As an interviewer. "I'm building relationships across the banking sector. Here's my board. Here's what we're buying. What are your parameters?"

Same person. Different conversation. Being respected isn't the same as being liked, and in this game respected wins.
