**The recipe, fast (1:55–3:25)**

Here's the whole answer in three points.

One. The seller is your first bank. A retiring owner who's done thirty years doesn't want to wait sixteen weeks for a lender. They want out, and they want to be looked after. So they let you pay them over time out of the business's own profit. That's vendor finance. You never call it that to their face. You say "getting paid over time."

Two. The business pays for itself, or you don't buy it. Free cash flow has to cover the repayments at least one and a half times. That's the 1.5 rule. If a deal can't clear it, the deal is wrong. Not your bank balance.

Three. The costs at the closing table get covered by the deal, not by you. Lawyers and accountants on deferred fees. A working capital line from the bank, drawn at settlement, pays them. Your board gets equity, not cash.

So "I don't have any money" isn't a money problem. It's a structure problem. And structure is learnable.

One honest thing. Zero deposit doesn't mean zero cost. You pay in time, in energy, and in the equity you give your board. And it's easier and faster if you've got some cash, a business or property behind you. Possible without. Slower.

**What you were told (3:40)**

Everything you were told about buying a business assumes you bring the money.

You were told you need a deposit.
You were told the bank decides.
You were told that if you don't have the cash, you raise it from investors.

And that's exactly why so many good operators are still on one business.

It has nothing to do with your discipline, your savings rate or how hard you work. It has everything to do with the fact that nobody showed you how a seller, a lender and the business's own profit fund the deal between them.

The two normal ways both hurt. Save a deposit: years, then the bank says no or private equity outbids you. Raise from investors: months per cheque, and you give away equity, control and the exit.

There's a third way. And it starts with the person everyone forgets.

**Why the seller says yes (4:45)**

People assume a seller wants all the cash today. A lot of them don't. There's a whole generation of owners in traditional industries ready to retire. Clinics, electrical firms, cleaning companies. Their problem isn't price. It's the burden of owning the thing.

If they sell the normal way, they wait 6 to 16 weeks for a bank, and they'll usually be offered 3 to 5 times profit.

And the reason getting paid over time changes their answer is simple. The price can go up, 6 to 7 times instead of 3 to 5, because they're paid out of profit over years. And it can close in 2 to 4 weeks. For someone who wants out, speed and certainty can matter more than a cheque on day one.

**Where every dollar comes from (5:40)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

A business makes $250,000 a year in profit, after paying someone to run it day to day, so you're not buying yourself a job.

First, you work out what it can afford to pay the seller. Divide the profit by 1.5. That's about $166,000 a year.

Then you stretch it over ten years. That's about $1.66 million. Cash buyers would typically offer three to five times profit, so $750,000 to $1.25 million. Your offer, paid over time, is higher than most of what they'll see.

[SCREEN: "$250K ÷ 1.5 = ~$166K a year × 10 years = ~$1.66M"]

The limits, straight.

**What they're actually judging (3:30)**

Think about it from their side.

A seller is handing over thirty years of work, and the staff they care about. They're not asking "is this person impressive?" They're asking "will this person look after what I built, and will I get paid?"

A bank is asking a narrower question. "Who's behind this, and have they done it before?"

So if you've been ignored, it has nothing to do with your age, your CV or your bank balance. It has everything to do with who's standing next to you when you ask. Neither of them needs you to have done it before. They need someone in the room who has.

Before I understood that, I was a 25-year-old with no network asking people to believe in me. [JOSH: one line on what those early conversations with owners or lenders were like, if you want it in]

**Who to approach (5:10)**

The sweet spot is recently retired executives. People who ran something big, stepped away, and now miss being useful. Money isn't what they're short of. Meaning is.

So you don't pitch them a job. You pitch the vision and the ground floor. "I'm building a group that buys businesses in this industry. I want someone with your experience at the table from day one. There's founders' equity in it."

Keep the first message to three or four lines. Who you are, what you're building, why them, and a request for twenty minutes. No forty-page deck. Don't apologise for your age or your lack of experience. They're judging whether you're serious and whether you'll do the work.

And follow up. Busy, senior people miss messages. Silence isn't a no. Send a second and a third before you cross anyone off.

*// THE MARKET HAS MOVED*

**Capital: forgetting the 1.5 rule (5:10)**

Here's something the save-a-deposit advice never mentions. The market it was written for has moved.

There's a whole generation of owners in traditional industries ready to retire. They don't want to wait 16 weeks for a bank. They want out, and they want to be looked after. Sold the normal way, they might get 3 to 5 times profit. Getting paid over time can mean 6 to 7 times, closed in 2 to 4 weeks. You're not asking for a favour. You're often making the best offer they'll get.

But the mistake at Capital is getting excited about "no money down" and forgetting the business has to carry the debt.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

The speed run, one more time. A business makes $250,000 profit. Divide by 1.5: about $166,000 a year can go to the seller. Over ten years, about $1.66 million. And the $84,000 left over is what keeps you alive in a bad year.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

My first deal ran on that logic. A physio clinic, 100% vendor finance, seven years at 4%, a nine-month payment holiday, no deposit. I took $150,000 out in year one.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a forecast or a promise."]
