**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Here's the whole lesson in three points.

One. There are two normal ways people try to get a business, and both are slow. You save for a deposit, which takes years, and then the bank says no or private equity outbids you. Or you raise money from investors, which takes months per cheque, and you give away equity, control and the exit.

Two. There's a third way. You buy a business that already works, from an owner who's ready to retire, and you pay for it over time out of the profit the business already makes. You don't need a big cheque. You need a structure the business can pay for, and you need people to take you seriously.

Three. That third way runs in five stages, in order. Credibility: you borrow a track record by building a board. Capital: you fund the deal with the seller, the bank, or both, and you never break the 1.5 rule, which I'll explain. Closing: you find owners directly, make offers and get through due diligence and legals. Then Grow: you make the business more valuable. Then Exit: you combine businesses and sell the group for a higher multiple than you paid.

That's the machine. Every episode in this series is one part of it.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Let's start with the slow roads, because most people are on one of them right now.

Road one is saving a deposit. I'll use round numbers, and they're illustrative only. Say the business you want costs a million dollars. Even a modest deposit is a few hundred thousand. If you can put away fifty grand a year, that's years before you can even make an offer. And when you finally do, you're competing with buyers who have more money than you.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example only.]

Road two is investors. It sounds faster. It isn't. Every cheque takes months of meetings. Then you've given away a slice of the company, a say in how it's run, and usually a say in when and how it's sold. You did the work, and someone else holds the steering wheel.

Road three is the one most people never hear about. Buy what already exists.

Now, there's a fourth option people love, which is to start something from scratch. I want to be fair to that, so I'll use someone who's done it well. Daniel Iles built a marketing business to around five million dollars a month. Before that worked, he had three businesses that each hit a ceiling. His real estate portfolio was capped by his local market, at three to five deals a year. His creator business was capped by him, because he was the one on screen, and by sponsors who only paid in certain seasons. His Snapchat publishing business was capped by the platform, which halved what it paid.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers". Link below. Not affiliated.]

And even when he did start the business that worked, he says he went in with five years' experience and around a million dollars he was prepared to lose, and still went half a million into debt. His words: taking all the risk with none of the knowledge behind it, he wouldn't wish that on his worst enemy.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "A message to my competitors". Link below.]

That's the build road. It can work. It's slow and it's expensive. Buying a business that already has customers, staff and profit skips the part that kills most startups.

So why would anyone sell you their business without a big cheque? Because there's a generation of owners in traditional industries who are ready to retire. They don't want to wait months for a bank. They want out, and they want to be looked after. Being paid over time gives them both. That's the whole of episode 2.

Now let me walk you through the five stages properly.

[SCREEN: Map, Credibility lit.]

Credibility. When you've got no track record, you borrow one. You build a board of experienced operators and give them founders' equity, usually two to ten percent each, instead of cash. You add good lawyers and accountants. Suddenly banks and sellers are dealing with a team, not a 25-year-old. I built my board entirely through LinkedIn and cold outreach. I had no network. The founder of the Australian Institute of Fitness is now a shareholder in my company, and I still own 73 percent of it.

[SCREEN: Map, Capital lit.]

Capital. Three sources. The seller, who gets paid over time. The seller keeping some equity, which we call a rollover. And the bank. Two rules sit over all of it. First, the 1.5 rule. The business's free cash flow has to cover the debt payments at least one and a half times. Second, debt over dilution. You borrow rather than sell equity, so you keep control and you keep the exit.

Quick illustration, round numbers. A business makes 300 thousand a year in profit. Under the 1.5 rule, no more than 200 thousand a year goes to paying for it. The other 100 thousand is your buffer.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example only. Education, not financial advice.]

[SCREEN: Map, Closing lit.]

Closing. You find owners directly, mostly by phone. Before any offer you get three years of financials, the matching tax returns and revenue broken down by year. You make an offer in a letter of intent at three to five times profit. Then due diligence, focused on whether the earnings are real. Then good lawyers.

[SCREEN: Map, Grow lit.]

Grow. Once you own it, you make it worth more. This is where Daniel's lessons come in through the rest of the series: price, profit, a sales machine, and a business that runs without its owner.

[SCREEN: Map, Exit lit.]

Exit. Small businesses sell for small multiples. Groups sell for bigger ones. Buy ten businesses at around four times profit, grow them, combine them and sell the group at eight, ten, sometimes fifteen times. That's the roll-up.

Here's my first deal, so you can see the stages weren't theory. The physio clinic was 100 percent vendor finance. Seven years at four percent, principal and interest. A nine-month payment holiday, so I paid nothing for the first nine months. Rent paid in arrears. No deposit. In the first year I pulled 150 thousand out of it, and I built mid six figures in equity.

[SCREEN: Results not typical. Records on file.]

And before that one closed, I'd had four deals fall over and made about a thousand cold calls. It's not magic. It's a process, and most of it is volume.

## Episode 02 · Why a retiring owner will let you pay them over time

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Why a Retiring Owner Will Let You Pay Them Over Time
  2. The Seller Is the Bank: Why Owners Say Yes to Being Paid Over Time
  3. Never Say "Vendor Finance" to a Seller (Say This Instead)
- **Thumbnail text:** THE SELLER IS THE BANK
- **Runtime target:** 11–12 minutes
- **Stage lit:** Capital

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

The person most likely to fund your first business isn't a bank or an investor. It's the owner selling it. Paid over time, a seller can often get six to seven times profit instead of the usual three to five, and close in two to four weeks instead of six to sixteen.

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice.]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: 3C map. Capital lit.]

This is the 3C Machine, episode 2 of 40, and today the light's on Capital. Specifically, the biggest source of it: the seller.

This is for people ready to put serious hours into buying a business. If you want something passive you can set and forget, this isn't it.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. There's a burden of ownership. A whole generation of owners in traditional industries is ready to retire. They're tired, and they don't want to sit through four months of a bank deciding whether their buyer is good enough. They want out, and they want to be looked after.

Two. Being paid over time can give them a better deal than cash. A higher total price, often six to seven times profit instead of three to five. A faster close, two to four weeks instead of six to sixteen. And interest on top while they wait.

Three. Language matters. Never say "vendor finance" to a seller. It sounds like you're broke and asking for a favour. Say "getting paid over time". Then talk about what they get: a bigger number, a faster exit, and someone who'll look after the business they built.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

This is the part I help people structure every week, through the Owners Academy or one-on-one. If you want help with your first seller conversation and structure, book a call below. It's with me. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[B-ROLL: An older owner locking up a workshop at dusk.]

Let's look at it from the seller's chair, because that's where every deal is decided.

Picture an owner who's run a business for decades. It pays well. But it also owns them. They're the one who gets the call when a staff member doesn't turn up. They haven't had a proper holiday in years. That's the burden of ownership, and it gets heavier with age.

Now picture their options. Sell to a cash buyer who needs a bank. The bank takes six to sixteen weeks, asks a thousand questions, and might still say no. Private equity might be interested, but only in bigger businesses, and they'll beat the price down. A broker will list it and charge a fee, and the buyers who show up want a bargain.

Or someone turns up, takes the business seriously, offers a higher total price, and can close in a few weeks. The catch is the money comes over a number of years instead of on day one. For a lot of retiring owners, that's not a catch. It's an income.

Let me show you with round numbers. These are illustrative only.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example only. Education, not financial advice.]

A business makes 200 thousand a year in profit.

Offer A, cash through a bank, at four times profit. That's 800 thousand. Sixteen weeks, if the bank says yes.

Offer B, paid over time at six and a half times profit. That's 1.3 million, paid over ten years. About 130 thousand a year, plus interest.

[SCREEN: Offer A: $800K, cash, ~16 weeks, bank-dependent. Offer B: $1.3M over 10 years, ~$130K/yr, 2–4 weeks.]

Now check it against the 1.5 rule. The business makes 200 thousand. Payments of 130 thousand. Two hundred divided by 130 is about 1.54. It passes. The business pays for itself, with a buffer.

So the seller gets half a million more on paper. You get a business without a deposit. And the business funds both of you. That's why this isn't a trick. It's a better structure for both sides when the numbers hold.

[SCREEN: Disclaimer: every deal and seller is different.]

Here's what a real version looked like. My first deal, the physio clinic, was 100 percent vendor finance. Seven years at four percent, principal and interest. A nine-month payment holiday. Rent paid in arrears. No deposit.

[SCREEN: Results not typical. Records on file.]

And a friend of mine in the US, Elliot, made around 40 offers and got three letters of intent signed at 100 percent seller finance. Some on very long "pension plan" terms. One example: 350 thousand a year for life, on a business with 500 thousand of net operating income.

[SCREEN: Results not typical. Records on file. LOIs signed, not closed deals.]

That word, pension, is the whole idea. For some owners, a steady payment every year is exactly what they want from the business they built.

Now, the seller is taking a risk. They're trusting you to keep the business running so the payments keep coming. Your job is to make that risk feel small. Three ways.

First, credibility. When a board of experienced operators sits behind you, the seller isn't trusting a stranger. They're trusting a team. That's the next act of the series.

Second, keep them involved. Most sellers stay on for a transition, anywhere from six months to three years. They help hand over staff and customers, and they get to see their business is in good hands.

Third, structure. The payment holiday is a good example. Agree the headline price first. Then ask for a payment holiday, nine to twelve months, framed honestly: it gives you time to make sure the business keeps performing, which protects their payments. If you need to, offer a slightly higher interest rate in return. The seller still gets their full price. It just starts a bit later.

Now the language, because this is where people blow it. Don't open with structure at all. Open with the golden question: "Have you ever thought about selling?" Then listen. Ask what they want for themselves, their staff and their customers.

When you get to terms, here's the shift.

[SCREEN: Don't say → Say instead.]

Don't say "vendor finance". Say "getting paid over time".

Don't say "I can't afford it upfront". Say "I can pay you more in total if it's paid over time".

Don't say "I need a payment holiday". Say "I'd like time to make sure the business keeps performing for you".

Same structure. Completely different conversation. One sounds like a favour. The other sounds like a plan.

And watch the signals. A seller who sends financials within a day or two of signing an NDA, asks about your timeline and talks about what they'll do afterwards is motivated. A seller who takes weeks to send basics and only talks about price is probably not ready. Don't push. Check back in six to twelve months.

[SCREEN: Checklist.]

Two. Take one business you know, guess its profit, and run the 1.5 rule. Profit divided by 1.5 is the most it can pay each year.

Three. Write down what a retiring owner in that industry would worry about most. Staff, customers, their name on the door. Plan how your offer answers each one.

"No seller will agree to this." Plenty won't. That's fine. You're not looking for every seller. You're looking for the retiring ones who want out and want to be looked after. Volume finds them.

## Episode 03 · Business vs real estate

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Business vs Real Estate: Why I'd Rather Own a Boring Business
  2. Ten Houses Are Worth Ten Houses. Ten Businesses Aren't.
  3. Cash Flow, Time, Multiples: Business vs Property Explained
- **Thumbnail text:** HOUSE OR BUSINESS?
- **Runtime target:** 11–12 minutes
- **Stage lit:** Exit (with Capital half-lit)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Ten houses are worth ten houses. Ten businesses, put together, can be worth a lot more than ten businesses. That one difference is why I put my effort into companies, not property.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: 3C map. Exit lit, Capital half-lit.]

This is the 3C Machine, episode 3 of 40. We're looking at the far end of the map today, Exit, and a bit of Capital, because the comparison with property touches both.

If you want something you can buy and forget, property might suit you better. This series is for people who want to own and run something, and build it into a bigger outcome.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three differences. That's the whole lesson.

One, cash flow. A good small business is usually priced at three to five times its profit. A property often returns a small percentage of its price in rent, and after interest there may be little or nothing left. Dollar for dollar, a business usually throws off far more cash.

Two, time. With property you usually need a deposit first, which means years of saving. A business can be bought with the seller paid over time, so you can start a lot sooner. The trade is that a business needs you to work on it. That's why it's priced cheaper.

Three, multiple arbitrage. Small businesses sell for small multiples. Bigger groups sell for bigger ones. Buy several small businesses at around four times, combine them, and the group can sell at eight, ten, even fifteen times. Property doesn't do that. Owning ten houses doesn't make each house worth more.

[SCREEN: Education, not financial advice.]

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy their first business and plan the group from day one, through the Owners Academy or one-on-one. If that's what you want, book a call below. It's with me. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[B-ROLL: Suburban street, then a busy trades workshop.]

First, I'm not anti-property. If you already own property, that's an asset. It can make buying a business easier and faster. I just think most people pick property by default, without ever running the comparison. So let's run it.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example only. Not financial advice.]

Round numbers, illustrative only. Two ways to spend a million dollars.

Option one, a million-dollar rental. The bank wants a deposit, say 200 thousand, that you have to save first. Say the rent, after costs, is around 40 thousand a year. Then you pay interest on the loan. Depending on rates, you might be left with a little, or you might be topping it up from your wages.

Option two, a business priced at a million dollars, at four times profit. That's 250 thousand a year in profit. Now pay the seller over time. The 1.5 rule says no more than about 166 thousand a year can go on payments. That leaves roughly 84 thousand of buffer every year, and you didn't need a deposit.

[SCREEN: Rental: $200K deposit, ~$40K net rent before interest. Business: $0 deposit, $250K profit, ≤$166K/yr payments.]

That's difference one. Cash flow. A business is priced on what it earns. A property is priced on what people think it's worth.

Difference two is time, and I want to be honest about both sides. Property is mostly passive. A business isn't, at least at first. You're dealing with staff, customers and suppliers. That's the work. But the time difference that matters more is how long it takes to start. Saving a deposit takes years. A retiring owner who agrees to be paid over time can get you in the door in weeks. And later in the series we'll cover how to put a manager in so the business runs without you. That's the Grow stage.

Here's a story from someone who's done both. Daniel Iles, before he built his current business, had a real estate portfolio.
