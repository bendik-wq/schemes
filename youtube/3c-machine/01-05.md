[SCREEN: Map, Capital lit.]

**The recipe, fast (1:05–2:30)**

Two. Being paid over time can give them a better deal than cash. A higher total price, often six to seven times profit instead of three to five. A faster close, two to four weeks instead of six to sixteen. And interest on top while they wait.

Three. Language matters. Never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time". Then talk about what they get: a bigger number, a faster exit, and someone who'll look after the business they built.

**The lesson (3:00–8:45)**

[B-ROLL: An older owner locking up a workshop at dusk.]

Most of what you were told about funding a business is wrong.

You were told you need a deposit. You were told the bank decides. You were told a seller wants all their money on day one. And that's exactly why so many good operators run one business for years and never buy a second.

The buyers who do this well don't start with the bank. They start in the seller's chair, because that's where every deal is decided.

Picture an owner who's run a business for decades. It pays well. But it also owns them. They're the one who gets the call when a staff member doesn't turn up. They haven't had a proper holiday in years. That's the burden of ownership, and it gets heavier with age.

Now picture their options. Sell to a cash buyer who needs a bank. The bank takes six to sixteen weeks, asks a thousand questions, and might still say no. Private equity might be interested, but only in bigger businesses, and they'll beat the price down. A broker will list it and charge a fee, and the buyers who show up want a bargain.

Or someone turns up, takes the business seriously, offers a higher total price, and can close in a few weeks. The money comes over a number of years instead of on day one. For a lot of retiring owners, that's not a catch. It's an income.

Round numbers, illustrative only.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example only. Education, not financial advice.]

A business makes 200 thousand a year in profit.

Offer A, cash through a bank, at four times profit. That's 800 thousand. Sixteen weeks, if the bank says yes.

Offer B, paid over time at six and a half times profit. That's 1.3 million, paid over ten years. About 130 thousand a year, plus interest.

[SCREEN: Offer A: $800K, cash, ~16 weeks, bank-dependent. Offer B: $1.3M over 10 years, ~$130K/yr, 2–4 weeks.]

So the seller gets half a million more on paper. You get a business without a deposit. And the business funds both of you. That's not a trick. It's a better structure for both sides when the numbers hold.

[SCREEN: Disclaimer: every deal and seller is different.]

That word, pension, is the whole idea. For some owners, a steady yearly payment is exactly what they want.

Now, the seller is taking a risk. They're trusting you to keep the business running so the payments keep coming. And the reason they'll take it is that you make that risk feel small. Three ways.

Credibility. When a board of experienced operators sits behind you, the seller isn't trusting a stranger. They're trusting a team. That's the next act of the series.

Involvement. Most sellers stay on for a transition, anywhere from six months to three years. They hand over staff and customers, and they see their business is in good hands.

Structure, and the order matters. First you agree the headline price. Then, once that's agreed, you ask for a payment holiday of nine to twelve months, framed honestly: it gives you time to make sure the business keeps performing, which protects their payments. If you need to, you offer a slightly higher interest rate in return. The seller still gets their full price. It just starts a bit later.

Now the language, because this is where people blow it. Don't open with structure at all. Open with the golden question: "Have you ever thought about selling?" Then listen. Ask what they want for themselves, their staff and their customers.

When you get to terms, here's the shift.

Don't say "vendor finance". Say "getting paid over time".

Don't say "I can't afford it upfront". Say "I can pay you more in total if it's paid over time".

Don't say "I need a payment holiday". Say "I'd like time to make sure the business keeps performing for you".

Same structure. Completely different conversation. One sounds like a favour. The other sounds like a plan.

And watch the signals. A seller who sends financials within a day or two of signing an NDA, asks about your timeline and talks about what they'll do afterwards is motivated. A seller who takes weeks to send basics and only talks about price probably isn't ready. Don't push. Check back in six to twelve months.
