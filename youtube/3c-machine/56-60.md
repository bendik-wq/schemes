## Episode 56 · Hire the GM before you need one

### Metadata

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

The worst time to hire a general manager is when you need one. By then you're the bottleneck, you're tired, and you'll hire the first person who sounds confident.

Daniel Iles hired two senior people, on $24,000 and $30,000 a month, and let them set their own targets. Both failed.

[SCREEN: "Two senior hires. $24K/mo and $30K/mo. They set their own KPIs. Both failed." — Daniel Iles]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: The 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → **GROW** (lit) → Exit.]

We're in Grow. Last episode, we turned customers into proof. This act is about the team that delivers it. Episode 1 of 5: the GM.

This is for people who want to own more than one business, or sell one for a proper price. It's not for people who want to buy a job and do it themselves forever. That's a fine life. It's just not what we're building.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Scorecard first. Before you advertise anything, write down the three to five numbers this person owns. Not tasks. Numbers. Daniel's rule is to set the KPIs before you hire a senior person, and never let them write their own.

Two. Search inside before outside. The best GM is often already in the building. The supervisor everyone goes to. Daniel found that existing staff who knew the business often beat expensive outside hires, with similar results on about a third of the budget.

Three. Plan the handover from the seller into the GM, not into you. The seller's transition period is when the relationships move. If they move to you, you've bought a job. If they move to the GM, you've bought a business.

[SCREEN: "Scorecard → Search → Handover"]

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and set them up so they don't need the owner on the tools. If you want to work through a deal, or the team plan for one, book a call with me below. It's me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Why does a GM matter so much in a 3C deal?

Because of where the money comes from. In a vendor-financed or bank-funded deal, the seller and the lender are paid from the business. The rule we use is that free cash flow covers your debt payments at least one and a half times. A GM costs money. So the GM has to be in the deal model from the start, not bolted on later when you're exhausted.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Let me show you. Illustrative numbers.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

Say the business makes $600,000 profit and the owner runs it. Debt payments to the seller and the bank are $300,000 a year. That's two times cover. Comfortable.

Now add a GM on $150,000. Profit drops to $450,000. Cover drops to one and a half. Right on the floor.

[SCREEN: "$600K ÷ $300K = 2.0× · add a $150K GM → $450K ÷ $300K = 1.5× (illustrative)"]

So you've got two choices. Structure the deal so the payments fit with the GM already in, a longer term, a payment holiday, a lower price. Or plan to hire the GM in year two, once you've grown profit. What you can't do is pretend you'll run it yourself forever and then find there's no room for a manager.

That's the money side. Now the hire.

**The scorecard.**

[B-ROLL: A single sheet of paper on a desk with five lines written on it.]

A scorecard is one page. At the top, the job in one sentence. "Runs the day-to-day so the business hits its numbers without the owner." Then three to five outcomes with a number against each.

For a trades or clinic business, it might look like this. Gross margin on jobs. Jobs or appointments completed on time. Staff turnover. Debtor days, meaning how fast customers pay. And one customer number, reviews or repeat work.

Daniel's KPIs for delivery roles are refunds, support tickets, client results and how often the team talks to clients. That last one he treats as an early warning. In a boring business, how often your team calls the big customers tells you about churn before it shows up in revenue.

Why does writing it first matter so much? His line: "Do not outsource your decision-making to people who do not have your best interests in mind." A senior hire who writes their own scorecard writes one they can hit.

[SCREEN: "Do not outsource your decision-making to people who do not have your best interests in mind." — Daniel Iles]

**The search.**

Start inside. In the 3C process you interview staff before you close, so you already know who people go to when something breaks. That person might be your GM, or your GM in a year.

Daniel did exactly this. He promoted an internal account manager to run client experience. The department now grows on its own, and he keeps weekly visibility through that one person.

If there's no one inside, go outside, and look at people who've done the job in a bigger, more traditional company. Daniel hires his operations people from companies with a thousand-plus staff, and he says to overpay one very high-calibre person rather than hire cheap. He also has a name for overpaying early: "the tax for being unknown." A small business nobody's heard of has to pay a bit more to get a great person.

[SCREEN: "If you don't have a reputation yet, you are going to have to overpay for your talent. That is the tax for being unknown." — Daniel Iles]

And be honest about how good you are at hiring. Daniel says he's still wrong about half the time on uncertain hires. His answer is to keep gathering information. Extra trial tasks, more interviews. And if you decide fast, reverse fast.

For a GM, I'd add a paid trial task. Give the finalists last month's numbers and ask what they'd fix first. You'll learn more in an hour than in three interviews.

**The handover.**

This is the part that's special to buying a business.

We negotiate a seller transition of six months to three years, and it's written into the deal. It's a condition of closing, along with employment agreements for the key people. Most buyers use that time to learn from the seller themselves. That's backwards if you want to own more than one.

Use the transition to hand the seller's relationships to the GM. The biggest customers. The key suppliers. The staff who only listen to the old owner. Make a list and tick it off.

[SCREEN: "Seller → GM handover list: top 20 customers · key suppliers · key staff · the things nobody wrote down"]

Daniel treats himself like an employee of his own business. He can't out-sell his best rep or out-manage his best account manager, so he runs changes past them first. Turn that into a test for any business you buy. If the owner is still the best manager in it, it isn't ready to sell, and you aren't ready to buy number two.

Look at a business like the one Saul, one of our Academy members, is in early due diligence on. An electrical and aircon business, 25 staff, around $3.8 million revenue, owner retiring in two years.

[SCREEN: "Saul (Owners Academy member): NDA signed, early DD. 25 staff, ~$3.8M revenue, owner retiring in 2 years."]

I'm not going to tell you what Saul will do. But that's the exact shape where the GM question comes first. Two years of owner still around. Twenty-five people who are used to one boss. Whoever runs that business when the owner goes needs to be named, scored and in place well before the owner walks out.

[JOSH: who runs day-to-day operations in your healthcare group, how you found them, and one thing you'd do differently]

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Checklist]

1. Write the one-sentence job for a GM in your business or your target.
2. Add three to five numbers they'd own. Gross margin, on-time jobs, staff turnover, debtor days, one customer number.
3. Name the person inside the business people go to when something breaks.
4. Rerun your deal model with a GM salary in it. Check cover stays at one and a half or better.
5. Start the handover list: top 20 customers, key suppliers, key staff.

**The turn (9:00–9:30)**

You can do all of this alone. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. At this step, here's how it goes wrong. You close, you step into the seller's shoes, and the GM never makes it into the numbers. Three years in, you're the only manager, and the bank and the next buyer both see it. ## Episode 57 · SOPs from screen recordings

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. SOPs from screen recordings
  2. Document the top 10 jobs in your business in one week
  3. Stop writing SOPs. Record them.
- **Thumbnail text:** RECORD, DON'T WRITE
- **Runtime target:** 11:30 (≈1,750 words)
- **Stage lit:** Grow (team and operations, episode 2 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Most SOP projects die in a binder. Someone spends three months writing a manual, and nobody opens it.

Here's a faster way. Ten jobs, one week, recorded while the person does them. Daniel Iles uses a tool that turns a screen recording into a step-by-step guide. You can do the same with a phone.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: The 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → **GROW** (lit) → Exit.]

Grow, team and operations, episode 2 of 5. Last time, the GM. A GM can only run what's written down, so this one is about getting it written down.

It's for owners and buyers who want the business to run without one person's memory. It's not for people who like being the only one who knows how things work. That feels like job security. To a buyer it looks like risk.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Pick the right ten. List every recurring job, then rank by how often it happens and what it costs when it goes wrong. The top ten is your week.

Two. Record, don't write. The person who does the job does it once while recording. Screen for computer work, phone video for anything physical. Then turn the recording into a checklist.

Three. Test it on someone new. If a new person can do the job from the SOP, it works. If they get stuck, fix that step. Then track one number over the year: labour cost per revenue dollar. It should fall.

[SCREEN: "Pick ten → Record → Test → Track labour cost per $ of revenue"]

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and make them worth more once they own them. If you want to look at a deal, or how to make one transferable, book a call with me below. It's me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Why do SOPs matter in a 3C deal?

Three reasons. Your lender wants to know the business won't fall over if one person leaves. Your seller is leaving, and the seller usually carries half the business in their head. And the next buyer, the one you sell to in the exit, pays more for a business that can be handed over.

Pull Daniel's lessons together and you get a list of what makes a business transferable. Documented SOPs. Specialist roles replacing the owner's tasks. Payment and collections tracking. Customer commitments recorded. And evidence the business ran for a period without the owner. SOPs are the first item because everything else sits on them.

[SCREEN: "Transferable: SOPs · specialist roles · payment tracking · recorded commitments · a period without the owner"]

**Step one: pick the ten.**

[B-ROLL: A whiteboard with a long list of jobs, tally marks next to each.]

Sit with the team for an hour. Write every job that happens at least once a month. In a trades business, it's things like taking an enquiry, writing a quote, booking a job, ordering parts, invoicing, chasing a late payment, onboarding a new tech, a safety check. In a clinic, booking, intake, billing, recalls, rostering.

Then score each one on two things. How often, from one to five. How bad when it goes wrong, from one to five. Multiply. Your top ten by score is the week.

You'll notice the top of the list is usually money. Quoting and invoicing. That's not a coincidence. That's where mistakes cost the most.

**Step two: record.**

Here's the week.

Monday, the list. Tuesday to Thursday, record three or four jobs a day. Friday, test.

[SCREEN: "Mon: list · Tue–Thu: record 3–4/day · Fri: test one"]

For computer work, a screen recorder. Daniel's team uses Scribe for auto-SOPs, Skool for training, and LastPass so passwords are shared properly instead of on sticky notes. For physical work, a phone on a tripod, and the tech talks through what they're doing.

Don't let the owner or the manager do the recording. Let the person who actually does the job do it. They know the shortcuts and the traps, and they'll take ownership of the SOP.

Then turn each recording into a checklist. One page, numbered steps, a screenshot or photo where it helps. Put the video link at the top. People will watch the video once and use the checklist forever.

One more thing. Build in a review step for anything that leaves the building. Daniel's line on his video team: "Anyone telling you that an editor should just be able to edit a video and make it go live has no clue what they're talking about." Every piece goes through needs review, in progress, approved. In a boring business, that's your quotes. Nobody sends a quote over a set size without a second set of eyes.

[SCREEN: "Anyone telling you that an editor should just be able to edit a video and make it go live has no clue what they're talking about." — Daniel Iles]

**Step three: test.**

On Friday, give one SOP to someone who has never done that job. Watch. Don't help. Every time they stop, that's a missing step. Fix it, and the SOP is done.

**And the seller.** If you've just bought the business, this is the best use of the seller's transition. Ask the seller to record the jobs only they do. The supplier they ring when stock's short. How they price the odd jobs. Which customers need a phone call instead of an email. That knowledge is worth money, and it walks out the door on the last day of the transition if it isn't recorded.

[B-ROLL: An older owner talking through a pricing spreadsheet while a phone records on a tripod.]

**Now the number.** Labour cost per revenue dollar.

Daniel calls it the McDonald's principle. Systems and SOPs make jobs easier, so the cost of labour per dollar of revenue should fall as you grow. His line: "As you scale, you should be paying your people less, not more." That's not about cutting anyone's pay. It's the business getting more revenue out of each hour, because people aren't reinventing the job every time.

Illustrative numbers.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

Year one: revenue $2 million, total wages $800,000. Forty cents of every revenue dollar goes on labour.

Year two, after the SOPs, a new hire gets productive faster and fewer jobs need redoing. Revenue $2.4 million, wages $860,000. About 36 cents.

[SCREEN: "$800K ÷ $2.0M = 40¢ → $860K ÷ $2.4M ≈ 36¢ (illustrative)"]

Four cents doesn't sound like much. On $2.4 million, that's close to $100,000 a year, and that's profit. In a business that trades at a multiple of profit, it's worth several times that when you sell. If the number is going up instead, you're adding people, not systems.

**When to do it.** Daniel's advice is to use the quiet periods. When his business was stuck for a few months, he built a queue of trained account managers with SOPs, so nothing broke when growth came back. He calls it battening down the hatches. Most boring businesses have a slow month or two. That's your SOP month.

**What not to do.** Don't buy a big new system to hold the SOPs. Daniel spent about $100,000 and six months moving his sales team to new software. They hated it, and he rolled back to Google Sheets and a simpler CRM in 14 hours. Let the people who use the tool pick it. A shared folder and a video link is enough to start.

[SCREEN: "Results not typical; records on file."]

My first deal, the physio clinic, came with a nine-month payment holiday. That's the kind of window where this work gets done, before the first payment is due.

[JOSH: one process at the physio clinic or your healthcare group that you documented early, and what changed once it was written down]

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Checklist]

1. Monday: list every job that happens at least monthly. Score how often and how bad when wrong.
2. Tuesday to Thursday: record the top ten, done by the person who does them.
3. Turn each into a one-page checklist with the video link at the top.
4. Friday: test one SOP on someone who's never done the job. Fix where they stop.
5. Work out labour cost per revenue dollar for the last 12 months. Write it down. You'll check it every quarter.

**The turn (9:00–9:30)**

You can do all of this alone. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. Here's how this step goes wrong alone. You buy the business, you're flat out in the first months, and the transition period ends before the seller's knowledge is recorded. Then you're the one who knows everything, and you can't leave. Or you do it with me, and the recording plan is part of your first 100 days before you sign.

1:1 advisory with G&L M&A Advisory: I work your strategy, structures and lender presentations with you.

Book a call with me below. Not a setter. Me. Short application first. On the call we work out your strategy, your first structure, and whether the Academy or 1:1 advisory fits.

Joining gets you the NBIO template, which a client was quoted about $7,000 for, and a 1:1 session with Jy Kingston, who built his board in under 30 days.

It's for people who'll put in 40-plus hours a week and want a roll-up exit, a legacy or flips. Cash, a business or property speeds it up. It's not for four-hour-week seekers or lifestyle-business hunters, and it's not for people who'll watch for another year without sending an offer.

**For skeptics (10:30–11:00)**

"My staff won't record anything." Ask the best one first, and give them the credit. Others follow.

"Our work is too varied for checklists." The jobs vary. The quoting, invoicing and booking don't. Start there.

"I don't have a week." Then do one job a week. That's 50 a year.

"Why would I listen to you on this?" I bought my first business at 25 with no money down and no experience. Not knowing how everything works is normal for a new owner. Writing it down is how you stop that being a problem.

## Episode 58 · Keep the key staff after the sale

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Keep the key staff after the sale
  2. The day-one conversations that stop staff walking out
  3. Retention bonuses, small equity, and who to keep when you buy a business
- **Thumbnail text:** DON'T LOSE THEM
- **Runtime target:** 11:30 (≈1,800 words)
- **Stage lit:** Grow (team and operations, episode 3 of 5)

### Script

Because the staff are watching you to decide whether to stay, and everything we've built in this act depends on them staying. Your GM, your SOPs, your bucket owners. And in a vendor-financed or bank-funded deal, the debt is paid from what those people do every day.

**What not to change.**

The 3C first-100-days rules are simple. Don't change everything immediately. Listen more than you talk. Respect what's working. Fix what's clearly broken. Communicate constantly.

Here's a test for each change you want to make. Ask: does a customer feel this, or does a staff member love this? If yes to either, leave it alone for now. The Friday barbecue. The way the senior tech sets up the van. The name of the business. These cost you nothing and they're how people know it's still their workplace.

What you can fix early is what everyone already hates. The broken tool. The roster that never works. The invoice system that makes people chase their own pay. Daniel says broken tools or processes he hasn't fixed are a cost to the staff, because they cap what people can earn. Fixing one of those in your first month buys more trust than any speech.

**Rules people can see.**

[SCREEN: "My job as employer… is to create conditions for employees to either succeed, or leave." — Daniel Iles (paraphrased from captions)]

In a family-run business, the rules usually live in the owner's head. Who gets the good jobs. What counts as late. What a good week looks like. When the owner leaves, nobody knows the rules any more.

Daniel's answer is to make them public. Publish the numbers. Set the standard by pointing at the best real performer. "Our best supervisor gets 95% of jobs done on time. That's the standard." People accept a standard someone in the room already hits.

He also warns about a culture that's stuck in the middle. Neither mission-driven nor standards-enforced. That's where weekend shifts nobody covers come from, and messages nobody answers. Pick one. In most boring businesses, clear standards and fair pay is the culture.

**How you behave.**

A few of Daniel's habits that matter more in your first year than at any other time.

Never fire shots by text. Anything that matters, say in person.

Take total responsibility. His line: "There will always be things that happen outside of your control. But that doesn't mean there have to be things that happen outside of your responsibility." When something goes wrong in your first months, it's your problem, even if the seller caused it. Staff notice who owns problems.

[SCREEN: "There will always be things that happen outside of your control. But that doesn't mean there have to be things that happen outside of your responsibility." — Daniel Iles]

And stay calm. Daniel says staff mistakes rarely upset him because none of them are bigger than mistakes he's made himself. A new owner who loses it over a botched job in week three teaches the whole team to hide problems.

**The cost of getting this wrong.** Illustrative numbers.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

A 20-person business. Say each departure costs around $30,000 once you add recruiting, training and slipped jobs. Use your own estimate.

If the change of owner goes badly and four people leave in year one, that's $120,000. If it goes well and one leaves, $30,000.

[SCREEN: "4 leave × $30K = $120K vs 1 leave × $30K = $30K (illustrative)"]

On a business making $500,000 profit, that $90,000 gap is close to a fifth of profit. In a deal where the debt is paid from profit, that's the difference between comfortable and tight.

[SCREEN: "Results not typical; records on file."]

When I bought my first business, the physio clinic, I was 25 and had zero experience. I pulled $150,000 out in year one. That's not typical. But it only works if the team keeps doing the work while the new owner learns.
