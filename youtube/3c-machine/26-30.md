Three. Find the ceiling before you push. Daniel Iles, who built a business doing around five million a month, says fulfilment is the real ceiling. His test: could you double sales next month and still deliver? In a boring business, that question tells you where to spend your first dollar.

[SCREEN: "Could you double sales right now and have fulfillment be okay?" — Daniel Iles]

*// LESSON · NOT-YOUR-FAULT + THAT'S NOT HOW IT WORKS · TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Here's what most new owners get wrong. They think day one is when you make your mark. They think the new boss has to look decisive. They think change is what they bought.

That's not how it works. You bought a machine that already runs. Your first job is to keep it running long enough to understand it.

And if you've come out of a corporate job, the urge to change everything isn't your fault. It has nothing to do with your judgement or your ambition. It has everything to do with years of being rewarded for visible change. In a business that's paying off its own purchase price, visible change is the risk.

And the reason the first 100 days matter so much in a 3C deal is the structure itself. If you bought with vendor finance, the seller is paid from the business. Same with the bank. Your whole structure rests on one thing: free cash flow covering your debt payments at least one and a half times. That's the 1.5 DSCR rule from Act 3. Break the cash flow in the first 100 days and you break the structure.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Here are illustrative numbers. Round, made up to show the shape.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

Say you bought a business doing two million in revenue and $250,000 in profit. Using the speed run from episode 11, that supports about $166,000 a year to the seller.

Now say in month two you lose your best supervisor and a customer worth 20% of revenue. That's $400,000 of revenue gone. If the business keeps about 40 cents of every dollar after direct costs, profit drops by $160,000. You're at $90,000 of profit with a $166,000 payment. Comfortable to underwater, and you haven't changed a single price or system yet.

[SCREEN: "$250K profit → lose 20% of revenue → ~$90K profit vs ~$166K owed (illustrative)"]

That's why the first job is keep.

**Keep the staff.** In the 3C process you interview staff before close, so you already know who matters. Day one, stand in front of them and say what's not changing. Pay, roles, the way the work gets done. Be specific. People fill silence with the worst story. Your key people should already be on employment agreements. That's a condition of closing, not an afterthought.

**Keep the customers.** Pull a list of the top 20 customers and what share of revenue each one is. Then book calls or visits with the seller beside you. The seller introduces you as the person who's going to look after them. If one or two customers are more than 30% of revenue, that's a red flag we check in due diligence. If you bought one anyway, those customers get the first visits and a call from you every month.

**Keep the seller.** We negotiate a transition of six months to three years, and it's written down. Here's the part people miss: when the seller is being paid over time, they want you to succeed. Their money depends on it. Book a weekly handover session. Ask them to show you the things that aren't written anywhere. Who to call when the van breaks down. Which customer pays late but always pays.

[B-ROLL: Owner and seller walking through a workshop; seller pointing at a filing cabinet.]

Now, measure. For the first 30 days, build a simple baseline. Revenue by where it came from. Gross margin, meaning what's left after the direct cost of doing the work. How full your team's week is. Cash in the bank every Friday. Without it, you're guessing, and you're guessing with borrowed money.

Then, the ceiling.

[B-ROLL: A crew loading a work van at 6am; a whiteboard schedule packed with jobs.]

Daniel says a profitable business can usually grow just by buying more customers. The thing that breaks is delivery. One month his team closed about five million dollars of contract value. Fulfilment couldn't deliver inside the seven-day money-back window, and close to two million was refunded.

[SCREEN: "~$5M closed in one month. ~$2M refunded. Fulfilment couldn't keep up." — Daniel Iles, 100-person team, 25:45]

His other line I'd tape to the wall in month one: "You can over-optimize something, break something, and then it takes weeks to fix it." He says that about a bored founder tinkering with a machine that works. A new owner is the same risk with less knowledge.

So ask the question. Could this business handle double the work next month? Write down what breaks first. The one supervisor? The scheduling? The quoting? That answer is your first hire, or your first system. It's never your first marketing campaign.

[SCREEN: "Days 1–30 Listen · Days 31–60 Measure · Days 61–100 One fix"]

First you listen, days 1 to 30. Staff, customers, seller. Change nothing big. Then, once you have the baseline, days 31 to 60, you run the double-the-work test and find the ceiling. And once you know the ceiling, days 61 to 100, you make one fix. One. Usually it's capacity, or it's the price. Price is next episode.

And the money lines up with the plan. In the 3C model the working capital line is drawn at settlement, and it covers fees and runway. The payment holiday, where we get one, should match the integration plan. My nine months weren't luck. They were a term in the deal.

[SCREEN: "Results not typical; records on file."]

On that deal I pulled $150,000 out in year one. That's not typical. But look at the shape: no deposit, a holiday before the first payment, and a business that kept running while the new owner learnt it.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Checklist]

1. Write one page called "What isn't changing" and read it to your staff.
2. List your top 20 customers and the share of revenue each one is. Book the first five calls with the seller.
3. Put the seller's handover sessions in the calendar, weekly, for the whole transition period.
4. Run Daniel's test. "Could we handle double the work next month?" Write down what breaks first.
5. Start the baseline sheet: revenue by source, gross margin, team capacity, cash every Friday.

If you haven't bought yet, do the same exercise on your target with the seller's numbers. You'll ask better questions in due diligence.

## Episode 27 · Raise prices before anything else

*// SECRET OPENER · ICP: HNW Capital Deployer*

They change one number. The price.

Daniel Iles has a worked example where a 33% price rise triples the profit on every customer.

[SCREEN: "$3K → $4K price. Net per customer $500 → $1,500." — Daniel Iles]

*// 7-MINUTE PROMISE*

**The map (0:30–0:55)**

[SCREEN: The 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → **GROW** (lit) → Exit.]

Grow, episode 2 of 5. Last episode we kept the business alive for 100 days. Now the first lever.

In the next few minutes I'll show you how one price change lifts profit, debt coverage and the value of a business at the same time, so you never have to buy growth with more staff, more hours or more risk. All without finding a single new customer.

This one's for owners and buyers who want more profit from capital that's already in the business. It's not for anyone who wants to be the cheapest in town. That's a race you win by going broke slowly.

**The recipe, fast (0:55–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Price is the biggest lever. Bigger than more customers, bigger than better sales. Daniel says it plainly: "Raising prices is almost always way easier than you think."

Two. Boring businesses are nearly always underpriced. The owner's been there 20 years, costs have gone up every year, and the price list hasn't moved. Daniel's rule: if you haven't raised prices in the last year, you're almost certainly undercharging.

Three. Profit sets the price of your business, not revenue. Every dollar of extra profit gets multiplied when you sell. So a price rise isn't just more money this year. It's a bigger cheque at the exit, and it makes your vendor payments easier from month one.

[SCREEN: "Profit is the number that determines whether your business is actually worth owning. It's not revenue." — Daniel Iles]

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and then do exactly this. If you want to work through the numbers on your deal, book a call with me below. Now the detail.

*// LESSON · WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING + NOT-YOUR-FAULT · TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Let's do Daniel's maths first, because it's the clearest I've seen.

[B-ROLL: Calculator and printed P&L on a workbench.]

[SCREEN: "Daniel's example: $3,000 price · $2,000 delivery · $500 overhead"]

A customer pays $3,000. Delivering the work costs $2,000. Overhead, the software and admin you can't pin to one customer, is $500. So gross profit is $1,000, net is $500. That's a 33% gross margin and 17% net.

There are two ways to get that gross margin to 50%. Cut delivery costs by a quarter, which in a service business means asking your people to take a pay cut. Or raise the price a third, from $3,000 to $4,000. His point: new customers don't know what you charged yesterday.

At $4,000, net profit per customer goes from $500 to $1,500. Triple. He pushes it further: double the price to $6,000, lose half your customers, and revenue stays the same with half the work. That's why he calls price the lever PE pulls first.

[SCREEN: "Price is the biggest lever, and it's the one PE pulls first."]

Now put that inside a 3C deal. These numbers are illustrative.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

You buy a trades business. Two million in revenue, $250,000 profit. You paid around four times profit, so a million dollars, mostly paid over time to the seller. Their payment is about $166,000 a year. That's the 1.5 rule. Tight but safe.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

You raise prices 10% on new quotes. Say you keep the same number of jobs. That's $200,000 more revenue, and because the work costs the same to deliver, nearly all of it lands as profit. Call it $450,000.

Your coverage on the seller's payment goes from 1.5 times to about 2.7. And at the same four times multiple, the business is now worth about $1.8 million instead of $1 million. One number.

[SCREEN: "$250K → ~$450K profit · 1.5× → ~2.7× DSCR · ~$1M → ~$1.8M at 4× (illustrative)"]

So why doesn't every owner do it? Because of what they were told.

You were told the cheapest quote wins. You were told a business grows by adding customers. You were told a price rise loses the customers you've got. And that's exactly why so many boring businesses sit on the same price list for years.

None of it holds up in the maths. Lose fewer customers than the price rise is worth, and you're ahead. Lose half at double the price, and you've got the same revenue with half the work.

And if you own one of these businesses, being cheap isn't your fault. It has nothing to do with your skill or your work ethic. It has everything to do with nobody ever putting the margin maths in front of you.

Here's how you do it in a boring business.

**First, find out if you're cheap.** Daniel's signal from his sales team: "If I have a 30% or 40% close rate, this rather indicates that the price is too low." His business sells on sales calls. A trades or services business sells on quotes, so the equivalent is your quote win rate. If you win almost every quote, you're not good at sales. You're cheap. [JOSH: confirm you'd say this. Applying Daniel's close-rate signal to quote win rate is our adaptation, not his words] And check the price history. Daniel tells the story of an owner who hadn't raised prices in six-plus years while costs rose about 50%. A 50% rise would only have kept pace.

**Second, start with new customers.** New quotes, new contracts, new jobs. Nobody has a reference point. Existing customers come next, with notice, and with the reason. Keep the letter short. Thank them for the work. Give the date, at least a month out. Give the new rate. Don't apologise and don't write three paragraphs justifying it. And have the seller beside you on the calls to your biggest accounts, because those customers trust the seller more than they trust you yet.

[B-ROLL: A one-page price-change letter on a desk; an owner on the phone with a client.]

**Third, move in jumps you can actually read.** Daniel tests by doubling a price for a day or a week and watching volume. He says five to ten percent tweaks drown in noise. In a trades business I wouldn't double overnight. But take the principle. Pick a step big enough to show up in the numbers, run it on new quotes for a few weeks, and watch your win rate. [JOSH: confirm you'd say this. The softer "don't double overnight" version of Daniel's price test is our adaptation] Daniel says even a 20 to 30% bump unlocks hidden profit.

**Fourth, one offer, one price.** Daniel removed a cheaper option sitting beside his main offer and revenue per lead went up about 40%. In a boring business, that's the "budget package" that eats your crew's time. Kill it or price it properly.

[SCREEN: "Removing the cheaper offer: revenue per lead +~40%." — Daniel Iles]

**Fifth, never discount to win.** If a customer pushes back, change the terms, not the price. Deposit up front, staged payments, a longer contract. The price stays.

And one ceiling. Daniel once raised prices until his close rate fell to 6%. It was more profitable, but his reps got about one deal a week and lost motivation, so he came back down. In your business the ceiling is your team and your customers' satisfaction.

So the order is simple. First you find out if you're cheap. Then you price new work. Once the new price holds, you move existing customers. And once both hold, you've got profit the seller never priced in.

That's the part to underline if you're deploying capital. A business that hasn't moved its prices in years, with thin margins, is the cheapest value you'll ever buy. You pay a multiple of today's profit. The price rise is profit the seller left on the table.

[SCREEN: "Results not typical; records on file."]

My first deal, the physio clinic, I bought at 25 with no deposit. I pulled $150,000 out in year one. I'm telling you that so you see the shape: a business bought with nothing down, paid for from its own profit. Every extra dollar of margin makes that shape safer.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Checklist]

2. Work out your gross margin per job using Daniel's six definitions: revenue, delivery cost, gross profit, overhead, net profit, gross margin.
3. Count last quarter's quotes and how many you won. That's your win rate.
4. Pick one new price and apply it to new quotes only, starting Monday.
5. List any cheaper "budget" option and decide: kill it or reprice it.

Number four. **Revenue by source.** Referral, outbound, ads, repeat. Daniel says attribute every sale to its source, because blended numbers hid that his ads were losing money.
