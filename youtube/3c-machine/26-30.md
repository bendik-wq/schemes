## Episode 26 · Your first 100 days as the owner

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Your first 100 days as the owner
  2. You bought the business. Now don't break it.
  3. The 100-day plan after you buy a business
- **Thumbnail text:** DON'T BREAK IT
- **Runtime target:** 11:30 (≈1,750 words)
- **Stage lit:** Grow (episode 1 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

The most dangerous person in a business you've just bought is you. Day one, you'll want to change everything. Don't.

My first deal, a physio clinic, came with a nine-month payment holiday. Nine months before the first payment. Use time like that to learn the business, not to rebuild it.

[SCREEN: "Josh's first deal: 100% vendor finance, 9-month payment holiday."]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: The 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → **GROW** (lit) → Exit.]

This is Grow, episode 1 of 5. Credibility, Capital and Closing got you the keys. This act is about what you do with them.

Who it's for: people who've bought, or are close, and plan to own for years and build to an exit. Who it's not for: anyone who wants to buy a job and coast. If you want a four-hour week, this act will annoy you.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three things for the first 100 days.

One. Keep what you bought. You paid for staff, customers and a seller who knows where everything is. Those three walk out the door faster than you'd believe.

Two. Measure before you move. Thirty days of real numbers before your first big change. Not the numbers in the sale documents. The numbers you see with your own eyes.

Three. Find the ceiling before you push. Daniel Iles, who built a business doing around five million a month, says fulfilment is the real ceiling. His test: could you double sales next month and still deliver? In a boring business, that question tells you where to spend your first dollar.

[SCREEN: "Could you double sales right now and have fulfillment be okay?" — Daniel Iles]

Keep. Measure. Find the ceiling. Then, and only then, pull the growth levers we cover in the next four episodes.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy and run businesses like this. If you want me to look at your deal, or your plan for the first 100 days, book a call with me below. It's me on the call, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Start with why the first 100 days matter so much in a 3C deal.

If you bought with vendor finance, the seller is paid from the business. Same with the bank. Your whole structure rests on one thing: free cash flow covering your debt payments at least one and a half times. That's the 1.5 DSCR rule from Act 3. Break the cash flow in the first 100 days and you break the structure.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Let me show you with numbers. These are illustrative, round, made up to show the shape.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

Say you bought a business doing two million in revenue and $250,000 in profit. Using the speed run from episode 11, that supports about $166,000 a year to the seller. Fine. Healthy.

Now say in month two you lose your best supervisor and a customer worth 20% of revenue. That's $400,000 of revenue gone. If the business keeps about 40 cents of every dollar after direct costs, profit drops by $160,000. You're at $90,000 of profit with a $166,000 payment. You've gone from comfortable to underwater, and you haven't changed a single price or system yet.

[SCREEN: "$250K profit → lose 20% of revenue → ~$90K profit vs ~$166K owed (illustrative)"]

That's why the first job is keep.

**Keep the staff.** In the 3C process you interview staff before close, so you already know who matters. Day one, stand in front of them and say what's not changing. Pay, roles, the way the work gets done. Be specific. People fill silence with the worst story. Your key people should already be on employment agreements. That's a condition of closing, not an afterthought.

**Keep the customers.** Pull a list of the top 20 customers and what share of revenue each one is. Then book calls or visits with the seller beside you. The seller introduces you as the person who's going to look after them. Customers stay for relationships, and the seller is lending you theirs.

And look at the list properly. If one or two customers are more than 30% of revenue, that's a red flag we check in due diligence. If you bought one anyway, those customers get the first visits, the best service, and a call from you every month.

**Keep the seller.** We negotiate a transition of six months to three years, and it's written down. Here's the part people miss: when the seller is being paid over time, they want you to succeed. Their money depends on it. Use that. Book a weekly handover session. Ask them to show you the things that aren't written anywhere. Who to call when the van breaks down. Which customer pays late but always pays.

[B-ROLL: Owner and seller walking through a workshop; seller pointing at a filing cabinet.]

Now, measure.

For the first 30 days, build a simple baseline. Revenue by where it came from. Gross margin, meaning what's left after the direct cost of doing the work. How full your team's week is. Cash in the bank every Friday. You'll use this baseline for every decision in this act. Without it, you're guessing, and you're guessing with borrowed money.

Then, the ceiling. This is where Daniel's lesson earns its place.

[B-ROLL: A crew loading a work van at 6am; a whiteboard schedule packed with jobs.]

Daniel says a profitable business can usually grow just by buying more customers. The thing that breaks is delivery. He learnt it the hard way. One month his team closed about five million dollars of contract value. Fulfilment couldn't deliver inside the seven-day money-back window, and close to two million was refunded.

[SCREEN: "~$5M closed in one month. ~$2M refunded. Fulfilment couldn't keep up." — Daniel Iles, 100-person team, 25:45]

His other line I'd tape to the wall in month one: "You can over-optimize something, break something, and then it takes weeks to fix it." He says that about a bored founder tinkering with a machine that works. A new owner is the same risk with less knowledge.

So ask the question. Could this business handle double the work next month? Write down what breaks first. Is it the one supervisor? The scheduling? The quoting? That answer is your first hire, or your first system. It's never your first marketing campaign.

Here's how I'd lay out the 100 days.

[SCREEN: "Days 1–30 Listen · Days 31–60 Measure · Days 61–100 One fix"]

Days 1 to 30, listen. Staff, customers, seller. Change nothing big.

Days 31 to 60, measure. Finish the baseline. Run the double-the-work test. Find the ceiling.

Days 61 to 100, one fix. One. Usually it's capacity, or it's the price. Price is next episode.

And line your money up with your plan. In the 3C model the working capital line is drawn at settlement, and it covers fees and runway. The payment holiday, where we get one, should match the integration plan. My nine months weren't luck. They were a term in the deal, and a term like that gives a new owner room to settle in before the first payment.

[SCREEN: "Results not typical; records on file."]

On that deal I pulled $150,000 out in year one. That's not typical. But look at the shape: no deposit, a holiday before the first payment, and a business that kept running while the new owner learnt it.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Checklist]

1. Write one page called "What isn't changing" and read it to your staff.
2. List your top 20 customers and the share of revenue each one is. Book the first five calls with the seller.
3. Put the seller's handover sessions in the calendar, weekly, for the whole transition period.
4. Run Daniel's test. "Could we handle double the work next month?" Write down what breaks first.
5. Start the baseline sheet: revenue by source, gross margin, team capacity, cash every Friday.

If you haven't bought yet, do the same exercise on your target with the seller's numbers. You'll ask better questions in due diligence.

**The turn (9:00–9:30)**

You can do all of this alone. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. At this stage, the loss looks like this: a new owner announces a restructure in week two, the best tech leaves, a big customer follows him, and the vendor payments start before the business has settled. Or you can do it with me. We write the 100-day plan before settlement, so the structure and the integration fit together.

To start, book a call with me below. Not a setter. Me. Short application first. On the call we work out your strategy, your first structure, and whether the Academy or 1:1 advisory fits.

When you join, you get my NBIO template. A client was quoted about $7,000 for the same document. And a 1:1 session with Jy Kingston, who built his board in under 30 days.

It's for people who can give this 40 hours a week or more, want a big outcome like a roll-up exit or a legacy business, and will act before they feel ready. It's not for four-hour-work-week seekers, lifestyle-business hunters, or anyone who'll watch for another year without sending an offer.

"I don't have the money to carry a business through a rough patch." That's what the working capital line and the payment holiday are for. They're negotiated, not saved for.

"I don't have the time." The first 100 days are the most hours you'll work in this whole journey. If you can't give it 40-plus, wait.

## Episode 27 · Raise prices before anything else

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Raise prices before anything else
  2. The first thing private equity changes after buying a business
  3. One number that multiplies profit in a boring business
- **Thumbnail text:** RAISE THE PRICE
- **Runtime target:** 11:30 (≈1,800 words)
- **Stage lit:** Grow (episode 2 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Most new owners try to grow a business by working harder. Private equity does something lazier and smarter. It changes one number.

Daniel Iles has a worked example where a 33% price rise triples the profit on every customer.

[SCREEN: "$3K → $4K price. Net per customer $500 → $1,500." — Daniel Iles]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: The 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → **GROW** (lit) → Exit.]

Grow, episode 2 of 5. Last episode we kept the business alive for 100 days. Now the first lever.

This one's for owners and buyers who want more profit without more staff, more hours or more risk. It's not for anyone who wants to be the cheapest in town. That's a race you win by going broke slowly.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Price is the biggest lever. Bigger than more customers, bigger than better sales. Daniel says it plainly: "Raising prices is almost always way easier than you think."

Two. Boring businesses are nearly always underpriced. The owner's been there 20 years, costs have gone up every year, and the price list hasn't moved. Daniel's rule: if you haven't raised prices in the last year, you're almost certainly undercharging.

Three. Profit sets the price of your business, not revenue. Every dollar of extra profit gets multiplied when you sell. So a price rise isn't just more money this year. It's a bigger cheque at the exit, and it makes your vendor payments easier from month one.

[SCREEN: "Profit is the number that determines whether your business is actually worth owning. It's not revenue." — Daniel Iles]

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and then do exactly this. If you want to work through the numbers on your deal, book a call with me below. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Let's do Daniel's maths first, because it's the clearest I've seen.

[B-ROLL: Calculator and printed P&L on a workbench.]

[SCREEN: "Daniel's example: $3,000 price · $2,000 delivery · $500 overhead"]

A customer pays $3,000. Delivering the work costs $2,000. Overhead, the software and admin you can't pin to one customer, is $500. So gross profit is $1,000, net is $500. That's a 33% gross margin and 17% net.

Now there are two ways to get that gross margin to 50%. Cut delivery costs by a quarter, which in a service business means asking your people to take a pay cut. Or raise the price a third, from $3,000 to $4,000. His point: new customers don't know what you charged yesterday.

At $4,000, net profit per customer goes from $500 to $1,500. Triple. He pushes it further: double the price to $6,000, lose half your customers, and revenue stays the same with half the work. That's why he calls price the lever PE pulls first. Change one number, multiply profit, move on.

[SCREEN: "Price is the biggest lever, and it's the one PE pulls first."]

Now put that inside a 3C deal. These numbers are illustrative.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

You buy a trades business. Two million in revenue, $250,000 profit. You paid around four times profit, so a million dollars, mostly paid over time to the seller. Their payment is about $166,000 a year. That's the 1.5 rule. Tight but safe.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

You raise prices 10% on new quotes. Say you keep the same number of jobs. That's $200,000 more revenue, and because the work costs the same to deliver, nearly all of it lands as profit. Call it $450,000.

Two things happen. Your coverage on the seller's payment goes from 1.5 times to about 2.7. You sleep better. And at the same four times multiple, the business is now worth about $1.8 million instead of $1 million. One number.

[SCREEN: "$250K → ~$450K profit · 1.5× → ~2.7× DSCR · ~$1M → ~$1.8M at 4× (illustrative)"]

Now the objection. "But I'll lose customers." Maybe some. Daniel's test is about exactly that. If you lose fewer customers than the price rise is worth, you're ahead. Lose half at double the price and you've got the same revenue with half the work.

So how do you actually do it in a boring business?

**First, find out if you're cheap.** Daniel's signal from his sales team: "If I have a 30% or 40% close rate, this rather indicates that the price is too low." His business sells on sales calls. A trades or services business sells on quotes, so the equivalent is your quote win rate. Different business, same signal. If you win almost every quote, you're not good at sales. You're cheap. And check the price history. Daniel tells the story of an owner who hadn't raised prices in six-plus years while costs rose about 50%. A 50% rise would only have kept pace.

**Second, start with new customers.** New quotes, new contracts, new jobs. Nobody has a reference point. Existing customers come next, with notice, and with the reason. Costs went up. We kept service the same. Here's the new rate from this date.

Keep the letter short. Thank them for the work. Give the date, at least a month out. Give the new rate. Don't apologise and don't write three paragraphs justifying it. And have the seller beside you on the calls to your biggest accounts, because those customers trust the seller more than they trust you yet.

[B-ROLL: A one-page price-change letter on a desk; an owner on the phone with a client.]

**Third, move in jumps you can actually read.** Daniel tests by doubling a price for a day or a week and watching volume. He says five to ten percent tweaks drown in noise. In a trades business I wouldn't double overnight. But take the principle. Pick a step big enough to show up in the numbers, run it on new quotes for a few weeks, and watch your win rate. Daniel says even a 20 to 30% bump unlocks hidden profit.

**Fourth, one offer, one price.** Daniel removed a cheaper option sitting beside his main offer and revenue per lead went up about 40%. In a boring business, that's the "budget package" that eats your crew's time. Kill it or price it properly.

[SCREEN: "Removing the cheaper offer: revenue per lead +~40%." — Daniel Iles]

**Fifth, never discount to win.** If a customer pushes back, change the terms, not the price. Deposit up front, staged payments, a longer contract. The price stays.

And one ceiling. Daniel once raised prices until his close rate fell to 6%. It was more profitable, but his reps got about one deal a week and lost motivation, so he came back down. In your business the ceiling is your team and your customers' satisfaction. Watch both.

This is also how you shop for deals. A business that hasn't moved its prices in years, with thin margins, is the cheapest value you'll ever buy. You pay a multiple of today's profit. The price rise is profit the seller left on the table.

[SCREEN: "Results not typical; records on file."]

My first deal, the physio clinic, I bought at 25 with no deposit. I pulled $150,000 out in year one. I'm telling you that so you see the shape: a business bought with nothing down, paid for from its own profit. Every extra dollar of margin makes that shape safer.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Checklist]

1. Find the date of the last price rise. If it's more than a year ago, flag it.
2. Work out your gross margin per job using Daniel's six definitions: revenue, delivery cost, gross profit, overhead, net profit, gross margin.
3. Count last quarter's quotes and how many you won. That's your win rate.
4. Pick one new price and apply it to new quotes only, starting Monday.
5. List any cheaper "budget" option and decide: kill it or reprice it.

**The turn (9:00–9:30)**

You can do all of this alone. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. Here's how this step goes wrong alone: you raise prices on everyone in one letter in month two, before the customers know you, and your biggest account walks. Or you never raise them at all because it feels risky. Or you can do it with me. We plan the price move as part of the deal model, so the seller payment and the price rise are designed together.

**The offer (9:30–10:30)**

There are two ways in.

The Owners Academy: the private community, the full course, every template and script, and daily deal discussion. Post your margin maths and the community will tell you where you're cheap.

Or 1:1 advisory through G&L M&A Advisory, where I work your strategy, your structures and your lender presentations with you.

Book a call with me below. Not a setter. Me. Short application first. On the call we work out your strategy, your first structure, and whether the Academy or 1:1 advisory fits.

Join and you get my NBIO template, a document a client was quoted about $7,000 for, plus a 1:1 session with Jy Kingston, who built his board in under 30 days.

This is for people who'll give it 40 hours a week or more and want a big outcome: a roll-up exit, a legacy, flips. It's easier if you have cash, a business or property. It's not for lifestyle-business hunters or four-hour-week seekers, and it's not for people who'll watch another year of videos without sending an offer.

**For skeptics (10:30–11:00)**

"Raising prices takes time to show up." It shows up in your first invoices on new quotes.

## Episode 28 · Buy customers, don't wait for them

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Buy customers, don't wait for them
  2. Why most boring businesses can't choose to grow
  3. Install a customer machine in a business you bought
- **Thumbnail text:** BUY YOUR CUSTOMERS
- **Runtime target:** 12:00 (≈1,850 words)
- **Stage lit:** Grow (episode 3 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Most boring businesses live on referrals. That sounds nice. It means the owner can't choose to grow this month.

Daniel Iles puts it more bluntly: you don't have a real business unless you can buy customers.

[SCREEN: "You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers." — Daniel Iles (video title)]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: The 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → **GROW** (lit) → Exit.]

This is Grow, episode 3 of 5. You've kept the business and fixed the price. Now we turn growth into something you control.

It's for owners and buyers who want growth they can turn up and down. It's not for anyone hoping a logo refresh and a few posts will do the job. And it's not for someone who wants to spend on ads before the business can deliver.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

One. Referrals are a gift, not a system. Daniel's point: businesses that live on word of mouth can't choose to grow in a given month. A real business has a way of acquiring customers where more input reliably gives more output.

His words: "I was going to stop trying to win a game that I didn't understand... and I was just going to moneyball the whole thing." He built a sheet with every number per rep, managed on it, and grew from about $1.4 million to $3 million a month in 30 days.

[SCREEN: "~$1.4M → $3M/month in 30 days, managed on the numbers." — Daniel Iles]

You're not running a sales floor. You're running a cleaning company, a clinic, an electrical contractor. The principle is the same. You stop managing on feel and start managing on facts. So here are the nine I'd check every Monday. I've grouped them into three: survive, grow, sell.

[SCREEN: "SURVIVE · GROW · SELL"]

**Survive.**

Number one. **Cash in the bank against your floor.** Pick a reserve floor and see if you're above it. Daniel's rule is to be cash-on-cash profitable every 30 days and keep a reserve. In a business with a seller to pay, this is the first number, every week.

How do you pick the floor? A simple start is a couple of months of fixed costs plus the next seller payment. If you're below it, nothing else on this list matters until you're back above it.

Number two. **Debt service coverage.** Free cash flow over your debt payments. The 3C rule is 1.5 times minimum. Track it monthly, look at it weekly, and know your headroom before the bank asks.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Number three. **Customer concentration.** What share of revenue comes from your biggest customer, and your top five. If one or two customers are more than 30% of revenue, that's a red flag we look for in due diligence. Don't own one you'd walk away from buying.

**Grow.**

Number four. **Revenue by source.** Referral, outbound, ads, repeat. Daniel says attribute every sale to its source, because blended numbers hid that his ads were losing money.

Number five. **Cost to win a customer.** Total sales and marketing spend divided by new customers. Daniel's two numbers that drive profit are this one and lifetime value.

Include everything. The ads, the agency, the salesperson's wage, the free first service. If you leave costs out, the number lies to you.

Number six. **Cost to win a customer as a share of lifetime value.** From episode 28: no more than 30%. If it creeps up, a channel's broken.

Number seven. **Gross margin.** What's left of each dollar after the direct cost of doing the work. From episode 27: it's where price shows up.

**Sell.**

Number eight. **Labour cost per revenue dollar.** From episode 29. It should fall as you grow, because systems make jobs easier.

Number nine. **Customer churn, or repeat rate.** How many customers you lost, or how many came back. Recurring, sticky revenue is what lenders and buyers love.

[SCREEN: "1 Cash vs floor · 2 DSCR · 3 Concentration · 4 Revenue by source · 5 CAC · 6 CAC ÷ LTV · 7 Gross margin · 8 Labour cost per $ · 9 Churn / repeat"]

Then the habit. One line at the bottom: what you expect next week, in numbers. Daniel's team used several forecasting models for one month. They all landed between $5.1 and $5.3 million, and they hit it. You don't need five models. You need one guess, written down, checked next Monday.

Now an illustrative example of why this matters at the exit.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

Two owners each say their business makes $500,000 profit. Owner one hands over a box of statements and says "trust me". Owner two hands over two years of these nine numbers, every week, plus tax returns that match. Who gets the cleaner offer and the faster diligence? You already know.

You've been on the other side of this. In Act 5 we asked sellers for three years of financials, matching tax returns and revenue by financial year, and we did a quality-of-earnings review. Now you're building the business a smart buyer would want to find.

Here's one thing to watch from Daniel's story: one number on its own can fool you. At one point organic content was five to ten percent of his leads and up to 60% of his revenue. If he'd only looked at lead counts, he'd have cut the channel that paid the bills. That's why revenue by source sits next to cost to win a customer. You read them together.

And keep it boring. One sheet. Same layout every week. The magic isn't in the sheet. It's in looking at it every Monday for two years.

Here's how I'd run the Monday. Twenty minutes, same time every week. Update the nine. Anything red gets one question: why, and who owns the fix? Then write next week's guess. If you have a general manager from episode 29, they run the meeting and you listen. That's owner-independence in practice: the business reports to you, not through you.
