## Episode 21 · Three documents before any offer

### Metadata

- **Thumbnail text:** NO DOCS, NO OFFER
- **Runtime target:** 11:00–12:00 (~1,650 spoken words)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: black. Three lines appear one by one: "3 years of financials." "Matching tax returns." "Revenue by year."]

Most buyers want to talk price on the first call. That's backwards. Before I put a number in front of any seller, I want three documents. And whether the seller sends them tells you more than anything they say on the phone.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Closing" lit. Label: "Act 5 · The deal · 1 of 5"]

This is Closing. Act five, the deal itself, episode one of five. Last episode was the industries banks love to fund. So say you've found one, and the owner has said "maybe". This is what happens next. It's for people putting 40-plus hours a week into buying a business. It's not for anyone after a lifestyle business they check on from the beach.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice. Use your own M&A lawyer.]

Three points.

One. Before any offer, you ask for three things. Three years of financial statements. The tax returns that match those years. And revenue broken down by financial year. Not a summary. Not a spreadsheet the owner typed up last night. The actual documents, ideally straight from their accountant.

Two. The NDA is the test. You send a confidentiality agreement, they sign it, and you ask for the three documents. A seller who's serious signs and sends within days. A seller who's just curious finds reasons. You learn their motivation without ever asking about it, and you haven't spent a cent.

Three. You read those documents for three questions. Is the profit real? Is it the same profit the tax office sees? And which way is it heading? If the answers are good, you write an offer. If the statements and the tax returns don't match, you walk. That isn't a negotiating point. That's the end of the deal.

[B-ROLL: hands sliding a signed NDA across a desk; a laptop with three PDF folders labelled FY1, FY2, FY3.]

That's the whole lesson. Documents first. The NDA as your filter. Three questions before a number. Most people who get burnt buying a business skipped at least one of these, because they fell for the business before they read it. The owner was lovely, the shop looked busy, and nobody checked whether the profit on paper was the profit in the bank.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people do this. Find the seller, read the numbers, write the offer, get it funded. If you want to work out your strategy and your first structure with me, book a call below. It's me on the call, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[SCREEN: "Document 1 · Three years of financials"]

Document one. Three years of financials. Why three? Because one year can be a fluke. A big contract, a bad winter, a one-off insurance payout. Three years shows you the pattern. You want the profit and loss statement and the balance sheet for each year. The P&L tells you what the business earns. The balance sheet tells you what it owns and what it owes. Tax owing. Staff entitlements. Equipment loans. Things that become your problem the day you settle.

[SCREEN: "Document 2 · Matching tax returns"]

Document two. The tax returns for the same three years. Here's why this one matters most. The financials are what the owner tells you. The tax return is what the owner told the government. And banks lend against what can be proven.

[SCREEN: Illustrative numbers only. P&L profit: $500K. Tax return profit: $350K. At 4× profit: $2.0M vs $1.4M. Gap: $600K.]

Illustrative numbers. Say the P&L shows $500,000 of profit and the tax return shows $350,000. One of two things is true. Either the owner has been under-reporting to the tax office, or over-reporting to you. Neither is a business you want to pay the higher number for. At four times profit, that gap is $600,000 of price.

Some owners will tell you the real number is higher because they "run a few things through cash". You can't buy that. A bank won't lend against it, and you'd be paying for income nobody can prove. My rule is simple. The business is worth what it can prove.

Sometimes the owner will ask why you need the tax returns at all. Tell them straight. "The bank lends against the returns, so if I want to pay you properly, I need them." That's not you being difficult. That's you being the buyer who can actually get funded. And if all you get is management accounts, the ones the bookkeeper runs off each month, that's a start, not a finish. Thank them, then ask for the accountant-prepared statements and the returns. Management accounts are where the optimistic numbers live.

One more thing on this document. Look at the balance sheet for anything owing to the tax office. Tax debt is a red flag on its own, and it often tells you the business has been short of cash for longer than the owner will admit on the phone.

[SCREEN: "Document 3 · Revenue by financial year"]

Document three. Revenue broken down by financial year. Ask for it split the way the business actually earns. By service line, by location, by customer group. In healthcare, ask for billings by practitioner.

[SCREEN: Illustrative. Business A revenue: $2.4M → $2.7M → $3.0M. Business B: $3.0M → $2.7M → $2.4M. Same three-year total.]

Look at these two. Same total over three years. One is growing, the other is shrinking, and the owner of the shrinking one will still quote you last year's profit. The breakdown also shows you where the risk sits. If one practitioner bills half the clinic's revenue and that practitioner is the retiring owner, half the revenue walks out with them.

[SCREEN: "The NDA test"]

Now the NDA. The sequence is: call, conversation, NDA, documents, offer. Send the NDA within a day of the "maybe". Then send something like this.

[SCREEN: "To put something sensible in front of you, I'll need the last three years of financials, the matching tax returns, and revenue by year. Your accountant can send them straight to me."]

Then watch. Motivated sellers send the documents in a day or two, reply quickly, ask about your timeline, and start talking about what they'll do after the sale. Unmotivated sellers take weeks, reschedule, and only want to talk price. That doesn't mean you delete them. It means they go back on the follow-up list, and you spend your week on the ones who sent.

[SCREEN: Saul · Owners Academy member · NDA signed, early due diligence. No deal closed yet.]

Here's that sequence working. Saul is an Owners Academy member. He found an electrical and air-conditioning business by cold call, straight to the owner. About 25 staff, around $3.8 million in revenue, owner looking to retire in two years. NDA signed, and he's now in early due diligence. Cold call, conversation, NDA, documents, then the real work. No broker, no bidding war.

Two warnings at this stage. First, don't over-ask. Three documents, not a 200-line checklist. The full checklist comes in due diligence, which is episode 24. Hand a 65-year-old owner 200 questions on day one and he'll go back to his golf.

Second, don't read them alone. Send them to your accountant, the one on your external bench from episode eight, and to your board. Ask three questions. Do the statements and returns match? Which way is revenue heading? Is there anything owing that changes the price?

[SCREEN: Red flags before an offer: statements ≠ tax returns (walk) · tax debt · one or two customers over ~30% of revenue · slipping gross margin · seller won't explain the numbers]

And here's how the documents turn into your offer. Say the profit you can prove is $400,000. Small businesses usually trade at three to five times profit, so your range is $1.2 to $2 million. Then the 1.5 rule from episode 12. $400,000 divided by 1.5 is about $266,000. That's the most your debt payments can be each year. Those two numbers frame everything in the next episode. Illustrative, round numbers, but that's the actual arithmetic.

[SCREEN: Illustrative. Proven profit $400K · 3–5× = $1.2M–$2.0M · ÷ 1.5 = ~$266K max annual debt service]

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: checklist, ticking as Josh speaks]

Five things.

One. Get an NDA template ready, and have your lawyer look at it once.

Two. Write your document request as one short paragraph, so you can send it the minute an NDA comes back.

Four. Every seller in your pipeline sitting at "maybe" gets an NDA this week.

Five. Write down how many days each one takes to send the documents. That number is your motivation score.

## Episode 22 · Writing the LOI

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Writing the LOI: 80% of the deal on two pages
  2. How to write an offer a retiring owner will sign
  3. The letter of intent, line by line (with a worked example)
- **Thumbnail text:** 80% IN THE LOI
- **Runtime target:** 11:00–12:00 (~1,550 spoken words)
- **Stage lit:** Closing (C3) · Act 5, the deal · episode 2 of 5

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: a two-page document. Most of it highlighted. Text: "80%"]

The offer letter is where the deal is won. Not the contract. If you get 80% of the deal agreed in a two-page letter, the lawyers just write down what you already agreed. If you don't, you'll pay them by the hour to argue about it.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: 3C map, "Closing" lit. Label: "Act 5 · The deal · 2 of 5"]

This is Closing, episode two of five. Last time you got the three documents and they checked out. Now you put an offer in writing. This is for people who'll put in serious hours and send real offers. If you're still waiting for the perfect deal before you write your first one, this episode is your nudge.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice. Use your own M&A lawyer.]

Three points.

One. Use a non-binding indicative offer, an NBIO, or a letter of intent, an LOI. Same job. It says what you'll pay, how you'll pay it, and what has to be true before you do. Price at three to five times proven profit, and show how you got there. The method is what makes the number believable to a seller who's never sold anything before.

Two. Front-load the economics. Price, structure, term, interest, payment holiday, conditions, exclusivity, timeline. Agree all of that here. Leave the legal detail for later. Aim to settle 80% of the deal at this stage.

Three. Check it against the 1.5 rule before you send it. If the business can't pay the debt one and a half times over, it doesn't matter how good the letter looks. Change the structure or don't send it.

[B-ROLL: Josh at a desk marking up a printed LOI with a pen; a calculator.]

And a language point. Never write "vendor finance" in a letter to a retiring owner, and never say it on the phone. Say "paid over time". It means the same thing, and it sounds like what it is: the owner getting looked after.

That's the recipe. One letter, economics first, checked against 1.5.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people write these and get them signed. If you join, you get the NBIO template I use. A client of mine was quoted about $7,000 for the same document. If you want to work out your first offer with me, book a call below. It's me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[SCREEN: "What goes in the letter" · 1 Price & method · 2 Structure · 3 Conditions · 4 Exclusivity · 5 Timeline]

Five sections. That's all a good letter needs.

Section one. Headline price and how you got there. Don't write a range. Write a number. And show the method: proven profit times a multiple. "Based on the FY24 financials and tax returns, adjusted profit of $400,000, at four times." When the owner can see your working, the conversation is about the inputs, not about whether you're being cheeky.

Section two. Structure. This is where no-money-down lives. How much is paid at settlement. How much is paid over time. The term. The interest rate. The payment holiday. Any rollover, where the seller keeps a slice. Any bank debt sitting alongside.

[SCREEN: Josh's first deal · physio clinic · 100% paid over time · 7 years · 4% principal and interest · 9-month payment holiday · rent in arrears · no deposit. Results not typical; records on file.]

Here's what that looked like on my first deal. A physio clinic. 100% vendor finance. Seven years at 4%, principal and interest. A nine-month payment holiday. Rent paid in arrears. No deposit. I was 25, unemployed, no savings, no experience. The structure did the work.

Section three. Conditions. What must be true before you're bound. Satisfactory due diligence. Finance approval, if a bank is involved. The lease assigned or renewed. Licences confirmed. Key staff on employment agreements. And the seller's transition committed to in writing. These protect you. If due diligence finds a problem, these are how you renegotiate or walk.

Section four. Exclusivity. Usually 60 to 90 days where the seller doesn't talk to other buyers while you do your diligence. Without it, you're paying accountants to check a business someone else can buy out from under you.

Section five. Timeline. Target dates for due diligence, contracts and settlement. Sellers who are tired of the business want to know when it ends. Give them a date.

[SCREEN: Illustrative worked example. Proven profit $400K · Price 4× = $1.6M · 100% paid over time · 7 years at 4% · 9-month holiday · Annual payments ≈ $267K · Coverage = $400K ÷ $267K ≈ 1.5×]

Now let's write one. Illustrative, round numbers. The business makes $400,000 of proven profit. You offer four times: $1.6 million. All of it paid over time. Seven years at 4%, with a nine-month payment holiday. Once payments start, that's roughly $267,000 a year. $400,000 divided by $267,000 is about 1.5. Right on the line. If you'd offered five times on the same terms, coverage drops to about 1.2, and you've written a letter you can't safely keep.

So when the owner wants more, what do you do? You don't break the 1.5. You change the shape. A longer term. Some rollover equity, where they keep a slice and get paid on the way up. The point from episode two still applies: paid over time, owners will often accept more than they'd get in cash today, and they'll take it in weeks instead of the months a bank takes. But only if the business can carry it.

Then the payment holiday. Agree the headline price first. Then ask for the holiday. Sellers fight hardest on the headline. Once that's settled, the holiday reads as a sensible request: it gives you time to make sure the business keeps performing, and they still get their full price.

[SCREEN: Elliot (US) · "a friend of mine" · ~40 offers → 3 LOIs signed at 100% seller finance. Results not typical; records on file.]

Now volume. A friend of mine in the US, Elliot, sent about 40 offers. Three LOIs came back signed at 100% seller finance, some on very long "pension plan" terms. As an example of the shape, $350,000 a year for life on a business with $500,000 of net operating income. Forty offers. Three signed. That ratio is normal. The people who get deals are the people who send letters.

Now, why does the 80% matter so much? Because everything you don't settle in the letter gets settled later, by lawyers, on the clock. Who pays for the stock on hand at settlement. Whether staff leave entitlements come off the price. How long the owner stays on, and what they do in that time. Whether the work vehicles are included. Each of those is a five-minute conversation now, between you and the owner. Later it's three emails between two law firms and an invoice. Put the big ones in the letter, even as one line each.

How you deliver it matters too. Don't just email a PDF and wait. Call the owner first. Walk them through it in plain English: here's the price, here's how I got there, here's how you get paid, here's when it finishes. Then send it, the same day. The owner will show it to their accountant, maybe their kids. Make sure the letter explains itself when you're not in the room. Plain words, short sentences, no jargon the owner has to look up.

Last thing. An LOI is mostly non-binding on price and terms, but some clauses, usually confidentiality and exclusivity, often are binding. Which ones depends on the wording and where you are. Your lawyer reads it before you sign. Once, quickly, with a fixed fee.

## Episode 23 · The best negotiation tactic is to walk away

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. The best negotiation tactic is to walk away
  2. "I've got other buyers": what to say when a seller bluffs
  3. How to negotiate a business purchase without raising your price
- **Thumbnail text:** NO WORRIES, GOOD LUCK
- **Runtime target:** 11:00–12:00 (~1,550 spoken words)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

[SCREEN: a phone on a desk, a message: "Another buyer has offered more." Josh's reply types out: "No worries, good luck."]

The best negotiation tactic in buying a business isn't a clever line. It's being genuinely willing to walk away. The second best is a time limit. Everything else is detail. I lost four deals before my first one closed. That's normal, and it's survivable if you've got more than one on the go.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Due diligence is about the quality of earnings. You priced the business on profit, so now you prove the profit. [SCREEN: "1 · Add-backs"]

When you're done, $50,000 survives. Real profit, $400,000. At four times, that's $1.6 million, not $2 million. [SCREEN: "2 · Customer concentration"]

Two, concentration. Ask for revenue by customer. If one or two customers make up more than about 30% of revenue, that's a red flag. If one is over half, you walk or you price it heavily. And check whether their contracts can be assigned to you. A big contract that ends when the owner sells isn't revenue you're buying.

[SCREEN: "3 · Revenue by source" · Daniel Iles, "Focus on revenue" 0:30]

Three, revenue by source. This is another Daniel lesson. At about half a million a month in his business, he found organic content was giving him 5 to 10% of his leads but up to 60% of his revenue. Blended numbers had hidden that. In another video he talks about scaling ads hard on those blended numbers, and it nearly bankrupted him. For you, the question is simple. Where does each dollar of revenue come from? Repeat customers, referrals, a contract, a single referrer, the owner's golf mates? If the honest answer is "the owner's relationships", that revenue might leave with him.

[SCREEN: "4 · Working capital"]

Four, working capital. How much cash does the business need sitting in it to pay wages before customers pay their invoices? Look at stock, debtors and creditors. If the seller strips the cash out before settlement and you have to put it back, that's a deposit by another name. Agree the level in the contract, and line up your working capital facility before close. That's next episode.

[SCREEN: "5 · Staff interviews before close"]

Then the people. Interview the key staff before you settle, with the seller's agreement. Ask what they'd change. Ask who customers actually call. Ask who's thinking of leaving. If the business depends on two or three people, offer them retention bonuses as part of the deal, say at 90 days and at one year. The worst due diligence finding is the one you get on day three, when your best technician resigns. Staff are nervous when a business changes hands. They wonder if they'll be kept, if their hours change, if the new owner is a cowboy. A calm conversation before settlement answers most of that, and it tells you who the business really runs on. Often it's not the owner. It's the office manager who's been there 15 years.

[SCREEN: "Who does what" · Accountant: quality of earnings · Lawyer: contracts, lease, licences, employment, disputes · Operator: site walk · You: timeline]

Who does what? Your accountant runs the quality-of-earnings work: profit, add-backs, working capital, tax. Your lawyer checks the contracts, the lease, the licences, employment terms and anything pending in court. One more thing worth asking for: when did the business last put its prices up? Owners who've been there 30 years often haven't raised prices in a long time. That's not a reason to pay more. It's a reason the business may be worth more to you than to them. More on that in Act 6.

[SCREEN: "6 · The diligence log" · Red-flag matrix: statements ≠ tax returns → walk · one customer > 50% → walk or heavy discount · seller won't transition → walk · issues < 10% of profit → accept or minor adjustment]

Big ones go back to the seller as a price change or a change in structure. Some end the deal. That's not failure. That's the process working.

How you take findings back to the seller matters. Don't ambush them, and don't make it personal. Sit down with the log and walk through it. "Here's what we found. Here's what it does to the profit. Here's what that does to the price, using the same multiple we agreed." Same method as the letter, new input. Most owners respect that, because you're not haggling, you're showing your working. And sometimes the fix isn't price at all. A customer-concentration problem might be solved with part of the price paid only if that customer is still there in a year. A staffing risk might be solved by the seller staying on longer. Price is one lever. Structure is the other.

The contract stage is where good deals go to die. Not because of a big problem. Because nobody's driving. My target from signed due diligence to signed contracts is four weeks. The way you get there is that you run the lawyers, not the other way round.

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

[SCREEN: Education, not legal advice. Use your own M&A lawyer.]

Three points.

One. Choose the right contract. A share purchase agreement, an SPA, means you buy the company itself, with its history. An asset purchase agreement, an APA, means you buy the parts you want: the equipment, the customer list, the name, and the liabilities you leave behind stay with the seller. **The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

[SCREEN: SPA vs APA · SPA: buy the company · contracts carry on · you inherit its history, known and unknown · more diligence · APA: buy chosen assets · leave liabilities behind · contracts, leases and licences re-done or assigned]

Start with SPA versus APA. With a share sale, everything transfers automatically. Staff, contracts, the lease, the supplier accounts. That's simpler on day one. But you also inherit everything the company did before you, good and bad, including things nobody knows about yet. That's why share deals need more diligence and stronger promises from the seller in the contract, the warranties and indemnities.

With an asset sale, you pick what you're buying and leave the old liabilities with the seller's company. The trade-off is admin. Contracts, leases and licences have to be re-done or assigned to your new company, and customers may need to be told.

[SCREEN: For no-money-down deals: asset sale often preferred for control · share sale acceptable if diligence is clean, with strong warranties · larger deals: ask about warranty & indemnity insurance]

For no-money-down deals, an asset purchase often gives you more control and less risk. If the seller strongly wants a share sale and diligence came back clean, that can be fine with proper warranties. On bigger deals, ask your lawyer about warranty and indemnity insurance. The decision is your lawyer's and accountant's call, because tax changes by country and by deal. Raise it early. If the seller assumed a share sale and you want assets, that's a conversation for the letter stage, not week three of contracts.

[SCREEN: "The vendor finance paperwork"]

Next, the paperwork for the part paid over time. Five points your lawyer should be checking. The seller's note sits behind any bank debt, so the bank is comfortable. You can pay it off early without a penalty. Your liability sits in the buying company, not your house. A default on one agreement doesn't automatically trigger the others. And the seller gets regular reporting, so they can see the business is healthy. On personal guarantees, larger deals, typically over about $5 million, often don't need one, because the business's assets are the bank's security. Typically. Every lender and every deal is different.

[SCREEN: "Fee-jacking" · Fixed scope · Prioritised issues list · Weekly call · Deadline in writing · Fees deferred or paid from working capital at settlement]

Now fee-jacking. Lawyers charge for time, and a contract without a deadline expands to fill whatever time you give it. So you set the scope before they start. You send a list of the six or seven issues that matter, in order, and tell them not to reopen what the letter already settled. That's why you put 80% of the deal in the letter. You hold a weekly call. And you push, politely, every week. From episode eight: good firms will defer or roll their fees to closing, so they want to get there too.

[SCREEN: "Minority rules from the start" · Drag-along · No veto or voting control for minority holders]

Minority rules. Your board members hold founders' equity, two to ten percent each. Good. But set up the shareholders' agreement from day one so that minority holders don't get a veto or voting control, and include a drag-along. A drag-along means when the majority agrees to sell, the minority comes along on the same terms. Without it, one person with 3% can hold up a group exit years from now. It's far easier to agree at the start than to fix later.

[SCREEN: "Settlement day" · Sign the agreements · Bank draws (if any) · Transfer funds · Keys, passwords, system access · Insurance live the same day · Working capital line drawn · Fees paid from working capital]

Settlement day. You sign the purchase agreement and the paid-over-time documents. Any bank money moves. You get the keys, passwords and system access. Your insurance goes live the same day the seller's stops, with no gap. And you draw the working capital line straight away.

[SCREEN: Illustrative. Revenue $3M · working capital line ~10% = $300K · legal and accounting fees $60K paid from it at settlement · $240K left as runway]

Illustrative numbers. A business doing $3 million in revenue. Working capital lines are often around 10% of annual revenue, so call it a $300,000 facility. Your legal and accounting fees, say $60,000, get paid from it at settlement. You walk in on day one with about $240,000 of runway, and none of it came from your savings.

[SCREEN: Cam (Cameron) · "my good friend" · 2 deals in 23 days · 100% vendor finance · interest-only 10 years, balloons in years 3 and 10 · $500K working capital from the bank. Results not typical; records on file.]

A good friend of mine, Cam, closed two deals in 23 days. 100% vendor finance, interest-only for ten years, with balloon payments in years three and ten. And $500,000 of working capital from the bank. On my own first deal, the rent was paid in arrears, so I paid for the premises after I'd used them, not before. Fast settlements need a tight letter and someone driving the lawyers.

[SCREEN: Before you settle, tick off: finance approval · lease assigned or renewed · licences confirmed · key staff agreements signed · seller's transition in writing]

Before settlement, go back to the conditions in your letter and tick every one off. Finance approved, if a bank is involved. The lease assigned or renewed in your company's name. Licences confirmed, which matters a lot in healthcare and trades. Key staff on signed employment agreements. And the seller's transition in writing: how long they stay, anywhere from six months to three years, and what they actually do in that time. A seller who wants to hand over the keys and disappear the next morning is a seller you should have worried about weeks ago.

And plan the first week before settlement, not after. Who tells the staff, and when. Which customers get a call from the seller introducing you. Which bank accounts move to your company. Settlement day is the end of Closing, but it's the first day of the next stage, and the first impression your team gets of you as the owner.
