**The recipe, fast (1:00–2:30)**

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice. Use your own M&A lawyer.]

Three points.

One. Before any offer, you ask for three things. Three years of financial statements. The tax returns that match those years. And revenue broken down by financial year. Not a summary. Not a spreadsheet the owner typed up last night. The actual documents, ideally straight from their accountant.

Two. The NDA is the test. You send a confidentiality agreement, they sign it, and you ask for the three documents. A serious seller signs and sends within days. A curious one finds reasons. You learn their motivation without ever asking about it, and you haven't spent a cent.

Three. You read those documents for three questions. Is the profit real? Is it the same profit the tax office sees? And which way is it heading? If the answers are good, you write an offer. If the statements and the tax returns don't match, you walk. That isn't a negotiating point. That's the end of the deal.

[B-ROLL: hands sliding a signed NDA across a desk; a laptop with three PDF folders labelled FY1, FY2, FY3.]

Documents first. The NDA as your filter. Three questions before a number.

**The lesson (3:00–8:40)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME*

If you've looked at a business before and it went wrong, it has nothing to do with being naive, or bad with numbers, or too trusting. No. It has everything to do with the order you were shown. Most listings put the asking price at the top and the documents somewhere after you're already attached. You were handed the wrong sequence. Here's the right one.

[SCREEN: "Document 1 · Three years of financials"]

Document one. Three years of financials. And the reason it's three is that one year can be a fluke. A big contract, a bad winter, a one-off insurance payout. Three years shows you the pattern. You want the profit and loss statement and the balance sheet for each year. The P&L tells you what the business earns. The balance sheet tells you what it owns and what it owes. Tax owing. Staff entitlements. Equipment loans. Things that become your problem the day you settle.

[SCREEN: "Document 2 · Matching tax returns"]

Document two. The tax returns for the same three years. This is the one that matters most. The financials are what the owner tells you. The tax return is what the owner told the government. And banks lend against what can be proven.

[SCREEN: Illustrative numbers only. P&L profit: $500K. Tax return profit: $350K. At 4× profit: $2.0M vs $1.4M. Gap: $600K.]

Illustrative numbers. Say the P&L shows $500,000 of profit and the tax return shows $350,000. One of two things is true. Either the owner has been under-reporting to the tax office, or over-reporting to you. Neither is a business you want to pay the higher number for. At four times profit, that gap is $600,000 of price.

Some owners will tell you the real number is higher because they "run a few things through cash". You can't buy that. A bank won't lend against it, and you'd be paying for income nobody can prove. My rule is simple. The business is worth what it can prove.

When an owner asks why you need the tax returns, tell them straight. "The bank lends against the returns, so if I want to pay you properly, I need them." And if all you get is management accounts, thank them, then ask for the accountant-prepared statements and the returns. Management accounts are where the optimistic numbers live.

[SCREEN: "Document 3 · Revenue by financial year"]

Document three. Revenue broken down by financial year. Ask for it split the way the business actually earns. By service line, by location, by customer group. In healthcare, ask for billings by practitioner.

[SCREEN: Illustrative. Business A revenue: $2.4M → $2.7M → $3.0M. Business B: $3.0M → $2.7M → $2.4M. Same three-year total.]

Look at these two. Same total over three years. One is growing, the other is shrinking, and the owner of the shrinking one will still quote you last year's profit. The breakdown also shows you where the risk sits. If one practitioner bills half the clinic's revenue and that practitioner is the retiring owner, half the revenue walks out with them.

[SCREEN: "The NDA test"]

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

Now the NDA, and the order it all runs in. First you have the conversation. Then, within a day of the "maybe", you send the NDA. Once it's signed, you send your request, something like this.

[SCREEN: "To put something sensible in front of you, I'll need the last three years of financials, the matching tax returns, and revenue by year. Your accountant can send them straight to me."]

Once the documents arrive, you read them with your team. And once you have the profit you can prove, you have your offer range. Then you watch. Motivated sellers send in a day or two, reply quickly, ask about your timeline, and start talking about what they'll do after the sale. Unmotivated sellers take weeks, reschedule, and only want to talk price. They don't get deleted. They go back on the follow-up list, and you spend your week on the ones who sent.

*// BELIEF SHIFT: PROOF THAT CHANGES THE STORY*

[SCREEN: Saul · Owners Academy member · NDA signed, early due diligence. No deal closed yet.]

Now I know what some of you are thinking. "A seller isn't going to send their tax returns to someone they've never heard of." So here's Saul. Saul is an Owners Academy member. He found an electrical and air-conditioning business by cold call, straight to the owner. About 25 staff, around $3.8 million in revenue, owner looking to retire in two years. NDA signed, and he's now in early due diligence. No broker, no bidding war. Not because Saul had a famous name. Because he followed the sequence. Cold call, conversation, NDA, documents, then the real work. The sequence works the same for you.

Two warnings. First, don't over-ask. Three documents, not a 200-line checklist; that comes in due diligence, episode 24. Second, don't read them alone. Send them to your accountant, the one on your external bench from episode eight, and to your board. Ask three questions. Do the statements and returns match? Which way is revenue heading? Is there anything owing that changes the price?

[SCREEN: Red flags before an offer: statements ≠ tax returns (walk) · tax debt · one or two customers over ~30% of revenue · slipping gross margin · seller won't explain the numbers]

And here's how the documents turn into your offer. Say the profit you can prove is $400,000. Small businesses usually trade at three to five times profit, so your range is $1.2 to $2 million. Then the 1.5 rule from episode 12. $400,000 divided by 1.5 is about $266,000. That's the most your debt payments can be each year. Illustrative, round numbers, but that's the actual arithmetic.

[SCREEN: Illustrative. Proven profit $400K · 3–5× = $1.2M–$2.0M · ÷ 1.5 = ~$266K max annual debt service]

## Episode 22 · Writing the LOI

### Script

*// SECRET OPENER · ICP: One-Business Ceiling*

**Cold open (0:00–0:35)**

[SCREEN: a two-page document. Most of it highlighted. Text: "80%"]

There's a well-kept secret in buying businesses, and only a few buyers use it. The ones who do get their offers signed, keep their cash where it is, and let the lawyers write down what's already agreed. Everyone else pays lawyers by the hour to argue.

You might think the secret is offering more. Or having a big deposit. That's not it.

The secret is that the deal is won in the offer letter. Not the contract. Get 80% of it agreed in two pages, and if you already own one business and can't see how you'd fund the second, this is usually how.

*// THE MAP + 7-MINUTE PROMISE (adapted)*

**The map (0:35–1:05)**

[SCREEN: 3C map, "Closing" lit. Label: "Act 5 · The deal · 2 of 5"]

This is Closing, episode two of five. Last time you got the three documents and they checked out. Now you put an offer in writing.

In the next few minutes I'll show you the five sections of a letter a retiring owner will sign, a worked example the business can actually carry, and the two words you never say. So you never have to argue the deal out between two law firms on the clock. And the worked example doesn't have a deposit in it.

This is for people who'll put in serious hours and send real offers. If you're still waiting for the perfect deal before you write your first one, this episode is your nudge.

**The recipe, fast (1:05–2:30)**

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice. Use your own M&A lawyer.]

Three points.

One. Use a non-binding indicative offer, an NBIO, or a letter of intent, an LOI. Same job. It says what you'll pay, how you'll pay it, and what has to be true before you do. Price at three to five times proven profit, and show how you got there. The method is what makes the number believable to a seller who's never sold anything before.

Two. Front-load the economics. Price, structure, term, interest, payment holiday, conditions, exclusivity, timeline. Agree all of that here. Leave the legal detail for later.

Three. Check it against the 1.5 rule before you send it. If the business can't pay the debt one and a half times over, it doesn't matter how good the letter looks. Change the structure or don't send it.

[B-ROLL: Josh at a desk marking up a printed LOI with a pen; a calculator.]

And a language point. Never write "vendor finance" in a letter to a retiring owner, and never say it on the phone. Say "paid over time". It means the same thing, and it sounds like what it is: the owner getting looked after.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people write these and get them signed. If you join, you get the NBIO template I use. A client of mine was quoted about $7,000 for the same document. If you want to work out your first offer with me, book a call below. It's me, not a setter. Now the detail.

*// LESSON · assumptive statements throughout*

**The lesson (3:00–8:40)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME*

If you've hit a ceiling with the business you've got, it has nothing to do with your work ethic, or your ambition, or the size of your bank balance. No. It has everything to do with a structure nobody showed you. Banks, brokers and listings all talk as if you pay cash on the day. A lot of retiring owners don't need that. What they need is a letter that shows them how they get paid.

[SCREEN: "What goes in the letter" · 1 Price & method · 2 Structure · 3 Conditions · 4 Exclusivity · 5 Timeline]

Five sections. That's all a good letter needs.

Section one. Headline price and how you got there. Don't write a range. Write a number. And show the method: proven profit times a multiple. "Based on the FY24 financials and tax returns, adjusted profit of $400,000, at four times." When the owner can see your working, the conversation is about the inputs, not about whether you're being cheeky.

Section two. Structure. This is where no-money-down lives. How much is paid at settlement. How much is paid over time. The term. The interest rate. The payment holiday. Any rollover, where the seller keeps a slice. Any bank debt sitting alongside.

*// BELIEF SHIFT: WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING*

You were told you need a deposit. You were told sellers want the biggest cash cheque. You were told nobody takes a letter seriously without a track record. And that's exactly why so many owners stop at one business.

[SCREEN: Josh's first deal · physio clinic · 100% paid over time · 7 years · 4% principal and interest · 9-month payment holiday · rent in arrears · no deposit. Results not typical; records on file.]

Here's my first deal. A physio clinic. 100% vendor finance. Seven years at 4%, principal and interest. A nine-month payment holiday. Rent paid in arrears. No deposit. I was 25, unemployed, no savings, no experience. No deposit, no big cheque, no track record. The structure did the work.

Section three. Conditions. What must be true before you're bound. Satisfactory due diligence. Finance approval, if a bank is involved. The lease assigned or renewed. Licences confirmed. Key staff on employment agreements. And the seller's transition committed to in writing. These protect you. If due diligence finds a problem, these are how you renegotiate or walk.

Section four. Exclusivity. Usually 60 to 90 days where the seller doesn't talk to other buyers while you do your diligence. Without it, you're paying accountants to check a business someone else can buy out from under you.

Section five. Timeline. Target dates for due diligence, contracts and settlement. Sellers who are tired of the business want to know when it ends. Give them a date.

[SCREEN: Illustrative worked example. Proven profit $400K · Price 4× = $1.6M · 100% paid over time · 7 years at 4% · 9-month holiday · Annual payments ≈ $267K · Coverage = $400K ÷ $267K ≈ 1.5×]

Now let's write one. Illustrative, round numbers. The business makes $400,000 of proven profit. You offer four times: $1.6 million. All of it paid over time. Seven years at 4%, with a nine-month payment holiday. Once payments start, that's roughly $267,000 a year. $400,000 divided by $267,000 is about 1.5. Right on the line. If you'd offered five times on the same terms, coverage drops to about 1.2, and you've written a letter you can't safely keep.

And when the owner wants more, the answer is never to break the 1.5. You change the shape. A longer term. Some rollover equity, where they keep a slice and get paid on the way up. The point from episode two still applies: paid over time, owners will often accept more than they'd get in cash today, and they'll take it in weeks instead of the months a bank takes. But only if the business can carry it.

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

Then the order you ask in. First you agree the headline price. Then, once that's settled, you ask for the payment holiday. Sellers fight hardest on the headline, so once it's agreed, the holiday reads as a sensible request: time to make sure the business keeps performing, and they still get their full price. And once the shape is agreed, the lawyers just write it down.

[SCREEN: Elliot (US) · "a friend of mine" · ~40 offers → 3 LOIs signed at 100% seller finance. Results not typical; records on file.]

Now volume. A friend of mine in the US, Elliot, sent about 40 offers. Three LOIs came back signed at 100% seller finance, some on very long "pension plan" terms. As an example of the shape, $350,000 a year for life on a business with $500,000 of net operating income. Forty offers. Three signed. That ratio is normal. The people who get deals are the people who send letters.

And the reason the 80% matters so much is that everything you don't settle in the letter gets settled later, by lawyers, on the clock. Who pays for the stock on hand. Whether staff leave entitlements come off the price. How long the owner stays on. Whether the work vehicles are included. Each is a five-minute conversation now, between you and the owner. Later it's three emails between two law firms and an invoice. Put the big ones in the letter, even as one line each.

How you deliver it matters too. Call the owner first. Walk them through it in plain English: here's the price, here's how I got there, here's how you get paid, here's when it finishes. Then send it the same day. The owner will show it to their accountant, maybe their kids, so make sure the letter explains itself when you're not in the room.

Last thing. An LOI is mostly non-binding on price and terms, but some clauses, usually confidentiality and exclusivity, often are binding. Which ones depends on the wording and where you are. Your lawyer reads it before you sign. Once, quickly, with a fixed fee.

Three. Replace every "vendor finance" in your templates and scripts with "paid over time".

Four. Send one letter. Not a perfect one. One.

**The recipe, fast (1:05–2:30)**

[SCREEN: Education, not financial or legal advice. Use your own M&A lawyer and accountant.]

Three points.

One. Due diligence is about the quality of earnings. You priced the business on profit, so now you prove the profit. Is it real, will it keep coming after the owner leaves, and how much cash does the business need to run? Your accountant leads this. Your lawyer covers the contracts, leases and licences.

Two. Four places where the profit hides or leaks. Add-backs, the owner's adjustments to profit. Customer concentration, where one or two customers carry the business. Revenue by source, where it actually comes from. And working capital, the cash tied up in stock and unpaid invoices.

Three. Talk to the staff before you close. The financials tell you what happened. The people tell you what's about to happen. Every finding goes in a log, and the log goes back into the price.

[B-ROLL: a spreadsheet titled "Diligence log": Issue / Found by / Impact / Action.]

Keep the whole thing to 90 days at the outside, run by your team, not by you alone at midnight.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

*// LESSON · assumptive statements throughout*

**The lesson (3:00–8:40)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME*

If you've ever been burnt on an investment like this, it has nothing to do with being careless, or bad with numbers, or too trusting. No. It has everything to do with who wrote the number. The adjusted figure was written by the one person in the deal who benefits from it being high.

*// BELIEF SHIFT: THAT'S NOT HOW IT WORKS*

Most buyers think adjusted profit is the profit. They think a clean set of accounts means a safe business. They think diligence is about finding reasons to say yes. That's not how it works. Diligence is where you find out what you're actually buying, and every dollar you prove or disprove moves the price by three to five.

[SCREEN: "Profit is the number that determines whether your business is actually worth owning. It's not revenue." Daniel Iles, "The math behind crazy profit margins" 5:40]

Daniel Iles puts it well: "Profit is the number that determines whether your business is actually worth owning. It's not revenue." He says that to people building businesses. For a buyer it's even more true. You're paying a multiple of profit.

[SCREEN: "1 · Add-backs"]

So, add-backs. Sellers adjust their profit to show what a new owner would earn. Some of that is fair. The owner's personal car running through the books. A genuine one-off legal bill. But look hard.

[SCREEN: Illustrative worked example. Reported profit $350K · Add-backs claimed $150K → "adjusted" $500K · Add-backs that survive $50K → real $400K · At 4×: $2.0M vs $1.6M · Difference $400K]

Illustrative numbers. Reported profit $350,000. The seller claims $150,000 of add-backs, so "adjusted" profit is $500,000. You test each one. The "one-off" legal bill turns up every year, so it's not one-off. The owner adds back his own salary, but someone has to do his job after he leaves, and that person costs money. When you're done, $50,000 survives. Real profit, $400,000. At four times, that's $1.6 million, not $2 million. That's a $400,000 conversation, and it's why your letter had due diligence as a condition.

[SCREEN: "2 · Customer concentration"]

Two, concentration. Ask for revenue by customer. If one or two customers make up more than about 30% of revenue, that's a red flag. If one is over half, you walk or you price it heavily. And check whether their contracts can be assigned to you. A big contract that ends when the owner sells isn't revenue you're buying.

[SCREEN: "3 · Revenue by source" · Daniel Iles, "Focus on revenue" 0:30]

Three, revenue by source. Another Daniel lesson. At about half a million a month in his business, he found organic content was giving him 5 to 10% of his leads but up to 60% of his revenue. Blended numbers had hidden that. In another video he talks about scaling ads hard on those blended numbers, and it nearly bankrupted him. For you, the question is simple. Where does each dollar of revenue come from? Repeat customers, referrals, a contract, a single referrer, the owner's golf mates? If the honest answer is "the owner's relationships", that revenue might leave with him.

[SCREEN: "4 · Working capital"]

Four, working capital. How much cash does the business need sitting in it to pay wages before customers pay their invoices? Look at stock, debtors and creditors. If the seller strips the cash out before settlement and you have to put it back, that's a deposit by another name. Agree the level in the contract, and line up your working capital facility before close. That's next episode.

[SCREEN: "5 · Staff interviews before close"]

Then the people. Interview the key staff before you settle, with the seller's agreement. Ask what they'd change. Ask who customers actually call. Ask who's thinking of leaving. If the business depends on two or three people, offer them retention bonuses as part of the deal, say at 90 days and at one year. The worst due diligence finding is the one you get on day three, when your best technician resigns. A calm conversation before settlement answers most of their nerves, and it tells you who the business really runs on. Often it's not the owner. It's the office manager who's been there 15 years.

[SCREEN: "Who does what" · Accountant: quality of earnings · Lawyer: contracts, lease, licences, employment, disputes · Operator: site walk · You: timeline]

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

And here's who does what, in order. First your accountant runs the quality-of-earnings work: profit, add-backs, working capital, tax. Then your lawyer checks the contracts, the lease, the licences, employment terms and anything pending in court. Alongside that, your operator, the industry person on your board, walks the site and tells you what the accountant can't see. Worn-out equipment. A roster that only works because the owner fills the gaps. You run the timeline. And once every finding is in, you take it back to the seller.

[SCREEN: "6 · The diligence log" · Red-flag matrix: statements ≠ tax returns → walk · one customer > 50% → walk or heavy discount · seller won't transition → walk · issues < 10% of profit → accept or minor adjustment]

The log is where that happens. Every issue goes in a spreadsheet. What it is, who found it, what it costs, what you'll do. Small issues, under about 10% of profit, you accept or adjust for. Big ones go back to the seller as a price change or a change in structure. Some end the deal. That's not failure. That's the process working.

And the way you take findings back matters. Don't ambush the seller, and don't make it personal. Sit down with the log. "Here's what we found. Here's what it does to the profit. **The lesson (3:00–8:50)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME*

If a deal of yours has ever stalled at contracts, it has nothing to do with not being a legal person, or not having a big budget, or picking the wrong firm. No. It has everything to do with nobody being in charge of the timeline. Lawyers are trained to find risk, not to finish. Someone has to finish. That's you.

[SCREEN: SPA vs APA · SPA: buy the company · contracts carry on · you inherit its history, known and unknown · more diligence · APA: buy chosen assets · leave liabilities behind · contracts, leases and licences re-done or assigned]

Start with SPA versus APA. With a share sale, everything transfers automatically. Staff, contracts, the lease, the supplier accounts. That's simpler on day one. But you also inherit everything the company did before you, good and bad, including things nobody knows about yet. That's why share deals need more diligence and stronger promises from the seller in the contract, the warranties and indemnities.

With an asset sale, you pick what you're buying and leave the old liabilities with the seller's company. The trade-off is admin. Contracts, leases and licences have to be re-done or assigned to your new company, and customers may need to be told.

[SCREEN: For no-money-down deals: asset sale often preferred for control · share sale acceptable if diligence is clean, with strong warranties · larger deals: ask about warranty & indemnity insurance]

For no-money-down deals, an asset purchase often gives you more control and less risk. If the seller strongly wants a share sale and diligence came back clean, that can be fine with proper warranties. On bigger deals, ask your lawyer about warranty and indemnity insurance. The decision is your lawyer's and accountant's call, because tax changes by country and by deal. Raise it early. If the seller assumed a share sale and you want assets, that's a conversation for the letter stage, not week three of contracts.

[SCREEN: "The vendor finance paperwork"]

Next, the paperwork for the part paid over time. Five points your lawyer should be checking. The seller's note sits behind any bank debt, so the bank is comfortable. You can pay it off early without a penalty. Your liability sits in the buying company, not your house. A default on one agreement doesn't automatically trigger the others. And the seller gets regular reporting, so they can see the business is healthy. On personal guarantees, larger deals, typically over about $5 million, often don't need one, because the business's assets are the bank's security. Typically. Every lender and every deal is different.

[SCREEN: "Fee-jacking" · Fixed scope · Prioritised issues list · Weekly call · Deadline in writing · Fees deferred or paid from working capital at settlement]

*// BELIEF SHIFT: BEFORE YOU KNOW / AFTER YOU KNOW (Before I Knew, adapted)*

Now fee-jacking. Before most buyers know how this stage works, they hand the contract over and wait. They answer the emails. They approve another round of drafting. They watch the bill climb and the seller go quiet. And every time, the same result.

Once you know that a contract without a deadline expands to fill whatever time you give it, you stop waiting. You set the scope before they start. You send a list of the six or seven issues that matter, in order, and tell them not to reopen what the letter already settled. That's why you put 80% of the deal in the letter. You hold a weekly call. And you push, politely, every week. From episode eight: good firms will defer or roll their fees to closing, so they want to get there too.

[SCREEN: "Minority rules from the start" · Drag-along · No veto or voting control for minority holders]

Minority rules. Your board members hold founders' equity, two to ten percent each. Good. But set up the shareholders' agreement from day one so that minority holders don't get a veto or voting control, and include a drag-along. A drag-along means when the majority agrees to sell, the minority comes along on the same terms. Without it, one person with 3% can hold up a group exit years from now. It's far easier to agree at the start than to fix later.

[SCREEN: "Settlement day" · Sign the agreements · Bank draws (if any) · Transfer funds · Keys, passwords, system access · Insurance live the same day · Working capital line drawn · Fees paid from working capital]

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

Settlement day runs in order. First you sign the purchase agreement and the paid-over-time documents. Then any bank money moves. Once it has, you get the keys, passwords and system access. Your insurance goes live the same day the seller's stops, with no gap. And then you draw the working capital line.

[SCREEN: Illustrative. Revenue $3M · working capital line ~10% = $300K · legal and accounting fees $60K paid from it at settlement · $240K left as runway]

Illustrative numbers. A business doing $3 million in revenue. Working capital lines are often around 10% of annual revenue, so call it a $300,000 facility. Your legal and accounting fees, say $60,000, get paid from it at settlement. You walk in on day one with about $240,000 of runway, and none of it came from your savings.

On my own first deal, the rent was paid in arrears, so I paid for the premises after I'd used them, not before. Fast settlements need a tight letter and someone driving the lawyers.

[SCREEN: Before you settle, tick off: finance approval · lease assigned or renewed · licences confirmed · key staff agreements signed · seller's transition in writing]

Before settlement, go back to the conditions in your letter and tick every one off. Finance approved, if a bank is involved. The lease assigned or renewed in your company's name. Licences confirmed, which matters a lot in healthcare and trades. Key staff on signed employment agreements. A seller who wants to hand over the keys and disappear the next morning is a seller you should have worried about weeks ago.
