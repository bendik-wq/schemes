## Episode 51 · Answer every enquiry in 5 minutes

### Metadata

  3. Speed to lead for a clinic or a trades business
- **Thumbnail text:** 5 MINUTES OR LOST

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

The cheapest growth in a business you've just bought isn't more leads. It's answering the ones it already gets.

Daniel Iles quotes a study that found contacting a lead within five minutes made a sale about 100 times more likely. Most small businesses call back tomorrow.

[SCREEN: "Contact within 5 minutes: a sale ~100× more likely." — HubSpot/MIT study, as cited by Daniel Iles]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: The 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → **GROW** (lit) → Exit.]

This is Grow. New act: sales and marketing, episode 1 of 5. Last episode we got the cash in faster. Now we go one step earlier, to the moment someone asks for help.

This one's for people who own a business, or are about to, and want growth that doesn't start with a bigger ad budget. It's not for anyone who thinks a new website fixes a phone nobody answers.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Five minutes, three channels. Daniel's team contacts every booking within five minutes by text, email and a real phone call. Not one of them. All three.

Two. Staff the hours when enquiries actually arrive. In his world a lot of enquiries land between 6 and 8pm, when the team has gone home at 5. In a trades business it's worse. The person who should answer is on a roof.

Three. Measure it. Every enquiry gets a time stamp, a first-response time, and an outcome. If you don't measure speed, it drifts back to "I'll call them after this job".

Here's the honest bit. Daniel sells a high-ticket service on video calls. His leads book a call a few days out. A burst pipe doesn't wait a few days. So in a clinic or a trade, speed matters more than it does for him, not less. He says it himself: home services should call back within the hour.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy boring businesses with little to no money down, and then fix exactly this sort of leak. If you want help with yours, book a call with me below. It's me on the call, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Start with why a buyer should care.

When you buy a business, you buy its enquiry flow. Most sellers have never measured how much of it they lose. That's upside you're not paying for. In due diligence, I want to know how many enquiries come in, from where, and what happens to them.

[JOSH: one line on whether you checked enquiry handling before buying the physio clinic, or what you found when you looked at it after settlement]

Here's the simplest check. Mystery-shop the business. Call it at 7:30 on a Tuesday night. Fill in the website form on a Sunday. Send a message through the Google profile. Write down how long each takes to get a reply. Most owners are surprised. Some are embarrassed.

[B-ROLL: Phone on a desk, missed-call notifications stacking up; a clock on the wall moving from 5:00pm to 7:30pm.]

Now, the system. Five parts.

**Part one: map every door.** Phone, website form, email, Google Business messages, Facebook and Instagram messages, the after-hours voicemail. For each one, write down who answers it and how fast. A lot of small businesses have a door that nobody is responsible for.

**Part two: give speed one owner.** Daniel calls his setters "glorified receptionists" doing speed to lead. That made me laugh, because a boring business already has a receptionist. Or an office manager, or the owner's partner doing the books. Give one person the job of first response, and give them the number they're measured on: minutes to first contact.

[SCREEN: "Setters are glorified receptionists doing speed to lead." — Daniel Iles]

**Part three: three channels inside five minutes.** An automatic text the moment the form comes in, so the customer knows a human saw it. An email with what happens next. Then a real phone call. Daniel's data: dialling alone reaches about 60% of leads. Add text and email and it's about 80%.

[SCREEN: "Contact rate: ~60% dialling alone, ~80% with SMS and email." — Daniel Iles]

**Part four: staff the hours that matter.** One of Daniel's clients stopped calling leads during school pickup and reached a 91% contact rate. In the same video, changing schedules alone lifted revenue about 30%. Nobody hired anyone. They just moved the hours.

[SCREEN: "Schedule changes alone lifted revenue ~30%." — Daniel Iles]

In a trades business, that might mean an office person who works 11 to 7 instead of 8 to 4. Or a call-answering service after hours. In a clinic, it's someone answering online booking requests in the evening, because that's when patients sit down and search.

One caution. Speed doesn't mean ringing someone at 9pm. Daniel points out that entry-level staff miss legal exposure like that. Calling-hour rules exist. Build them into the system, don't trust memory.

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**Part five: chase the ones who go quiet.** Daniel's cadence for people who enquire but don't book: contact within five minutes, again later that day, then once every 24 hours for three days, then into a longer follow-up list. Cancels and no-shows get a message a day for five days to rebook. And every message carries a reschedule link, because without one people cancel and never come back.

[SCREEN: "5 minutes → same day → daily ×3 → nurture. Reschedule link in every message." — Daniel Iles]

Then watch who replies. Across more than 1,000 of his closes, a lead who opened the confirmation email and replied to the rep's text showed up qualified about 42% of the time. One who did neither: about 3%. That's his data on sales calls, not patients or homeowners. But the idea travels. A customer who replies "yep, see you Thursday" turns up. One who goes silent needs a call.

[SCREEN: "Opened email + replied to text: ~42%. Neither: ~3%." — Daniel Iles, sales-call data]

Don't overdo it. Daniel tested a heavy pre-call contact system and found that sending five to eight emails in 24 hours reduced interest. A 70-year-old physio patient doesn't want eight emails. One text, one reminder, done.

Now let's put a number on it. Illustrative numbers only.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

Say you bought a physio clinic that gets 100 new-patient enquiries a month. Forty of them book. A new patient is worth about $600 over their course of care. If faster response lifts bookings from 40 to 50, that's ten more patients, about $6,000 a month, roughly $72,000 a year. No extra marketing. If the clinicians have spare capacity, a big part of that is margin.

[SCREEN: "100 enquiries → 40 → 50 booked × $600 = +$6,000/month (illustrative)"]

And here's why the next buyer pays for it. Profit gets multiplied when you sell. A leak you plug in year one gets valued again at the exit.

There's one more lesson, from a guest on Daniel's channel, Matt from 7th Level. They had about 50,000 leads a month and were reaching about 8% of them. The instinct was to buy more leads. Matt now says he'd have cut lead flow by 60% and fixed speed to lead first. That's the order for you too. Answer what you've got. Then buy more. Episode 54 is the buying.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Checklist]

1. Mystery-shop the business five times: phone after hours, web form, Google message, email, social message. Time each reply.
2. List every door and the one person responsible for it.
3. Turn on an instant auto-text for every web enquiry.
4. Start an enquiry log: time in, first response, booked or not, source.
5. Move one person's hours so someone is answering between 5 and 7pm.

**The turn (9:00–9:30)**

You can do all of this alone. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. At this step, alone usually means buying expensive software nobody opens, or telling the receptionist to "be faster" and never checking. Or skipping it completely and spending money on ads that ring a phone nobody answers. Or you can do it with me. We look at enquiry flow during due diligence, so it's in the plan before you settle.

**The offer (9:30–10:30)**

There are two ways to work with me.

The Owners Academy: a private community, the full course, every template and script, and daily deal discussion.

Or 1:1 advisory with G&L M&A Advisory, where I work your strategy, your structures and your lender presentations with you.

Book a call with me below. Short application first. Then it's you and me, no setter. We work out your strategy, your first structure, and which of the two fits you.

When you join you also get my NBIO template, which a client was quoted about $7,000 for, and a 1:1 session with Jy Kingston, who built his board in under 30 days.

This is for people who can give it 40-plus hours a week, want a big outcome like a roll-up exit, a legacy business or flips, and will act before they feel ready. Cash, a business or property makes it faster. It's not for the four-hour week. It's not for a lifestyle business. And it's not for people who'll watch another year without sending an offer.

**For skeptics (10:30–11:00)**

"I can't afford more staff." You're not adding staff. You're moving hours and adding a text message.

"I don't have time." Then give speed to one person and measure them. That's the point.

"Do you actually do this?" I made about 1,000 cold calls before my first deal. I know what speed and persistence are worth.

"My business is referral-only." Referrals enquire too. A referral you ring back tomorrow has already called someone else.

## Episode 52 · Give the business a sales process

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Give the business a sales process
  2. Most quotes die in silence. Here's the fix.
  3. A sales script your team can actually run
- **Thumbnail text:** THE QUOTE DIED
- **Runtime target:** 12:00 (≈1,800 words)
- **Stage lit:** Grow (Act 11, episode 2 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

In a lot of small businesses, the quote goes out and nobody ever calls again. The job doesn't go to the cheapest. It goes to whoever followed up.

Daniel Iles says some of his reps close up to half their deals in follow-up.

[SCREEN: "Some reps close up to half their deals in follow-up." — Daniel Iles]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: The 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → **GROW** (lit) → Exit.]

Grow, sales and marketing, episode 2 of 5. Last time we answered every enquiry fast. Today: what happens after you answer.

This is for owners who want sales to work without them being the only one who can close. It's not for anyone who wants a slick pressure script. Daniel says it plainly: high pressure doesn't work on people with money. It doesn't work on a 60-year-old getting a quote for a new roof either.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Write the stages down. Enquiry, triage, visit or consult, quote, follow-up, close and deposit. If it isn't written down, every staff member runs their own version.

Two. Ask, don't pitch. Daniel built a six-question flowchart over a year and thousands of calls. New reps learn it in a few hours. I'll translate it for a quote visit.

Three. Close and collect on the spot, then follow up properly. Contract and deposit before you leave, like we set up in episode 50. If they don't sign, a planned follow-up for a month, not "let us know".

Here's where his world and yours differ. Daniel sells a high-ticket service on a 45-minute video call. A plumber sells at the kitchen bench. A physio doesn't "close" at all. They recommend a care plan. Same bones, different clothes. I'll show both.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

I help people buy businesses with little to no money down, then build this kind of system so the business doesn't depend on one person. If that's what you want, book a call with me below. You'll be talking to me. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

First, why it matters for a buyer.

In a lot of small businesses the owner is the sales process. He knows which customers to call back and what to say. When he retires, that walks out the door with him. Saul, an Owners Academy member, is in early due diligence on an electrical and aircon business: 25 staff, about $3.8 million in revenue, owner retiring in two years. In a business like that, one of the first things I'd ask is who wins the work, and whether it's written down anywhere.

[SCREEN: "Saul (Owners Academy member): NDA signed, early DD, electrical/aircon, 25 staff, ~$3.8M revenue."]

Daniel says the CEO has to stay close to sales, because two businesses running the same ads get very different results depending on what happens on the call. And he took every call himself for about two months before he hired anyone, because otherwise he couldn't write the process, set targets or know what to pay. So step zero: as the new owner, go on 20 quote visits or sit in on 20 new-patient consults yourself. Write down every question, every objection, every reason someone said no.

[SCREEN: "Founder sells first: ~2 months before hiring." — Daniel Iles]

Now the questions. Here's Daniel's flowchart, translated for a trades quote. Illustrative example: a ducted air conditioning install.

**Open broad.** Daniel's opener: "What prompted you to reach out?" At a kitchen bench: "What made you call us now?" Broad questions get real answers.

**Authority.** "Is it just you deciding, or is someone else involved?" Partner, landlord, body corporate. Daniel's team added "who else needs to be on this call?" to every call and closes went up team-wide. For a tradie: book the visit when both partners are home. You'll stop hearing "I need to talk to my wife" at the end.

[SCREEN: "Who else needs to be on this call?" — added to every call, closes went up. Daniel Iles]

**Need, with a number.** "What's not working? How long has it been like that? What's it costing you?" The unit that keeps tripping the breaker. The power bill. The bedroom nobody sleeps in over summer.

**What they've tried.** Daniel's favourite: "What's been your strategy so far?" At the bench: "Have you had anyone else out? What did they suggest?" Either they haven't, or they have and something didn't sit right. Both help you.

**What good looks like.** "If this was done right, what would be different?" Let them describe it.

**Timing, last.** "When do you need it done by?" Daniel asks this near the end, because asked early it sounds like pressure.

Then you recommend one option. Daniel's biggest single change was cutting a cheaper downsell. Same number of sales at the higher price, and revenue went from about $1.4 million to $3 million the next month. A second price makes people "go home and think".

[SCREEN: "Removed the downsell: ~$1.4M → $3M the next month." — Daniel Iles]

Honest caveat. Lots of trades quote good, better, best, and some do well with it. I wouldn't rip it out on day one. Test it. Have one estimator quote a single recommended option for a month and compare.

Then close. Daniel: never ask the customer "what do you think we should do?" You're the expert. Say what you'd do, give the price plainly, and ask for the go-ahead. No fake urgency. "Price goes up Friday" is a lie customers can smell. If they say yes, take the deposit and the signature then and there. Daniel's point: more deals are lost to "I'll pay right after" than are won in follow-up.

[SCREEN: "Collect payment and the contract on the call." — Daniel Iles]

Now the clinic version. A physio isn't selling. They're recommending the care the patient needs, and pressure has no place in healthcare. But the bones are the same: ask good questions, explain the plan in plain English, and book the next appointment before the patient leaves the room. A lot of clinics lose patients after visit one, simply because nobody booked visit two.

[JOSH: optional — one sentence on how your clinic handles booking the next appointment, if you're happy to share it]

Follow-up. If they don't sign, the follow-up is planned and tied to their objection. "Did you get a chance to talk to your partner?" "We've got finance options now." Any sign of interest, you ring. You don't start an email thread. Daniel runs this for about a month.

And every lost job gets a reason. Daniel tags every lost call as budget, authority, need or timing, then fixes the biggest one in the offer. If most of your lost quotes are "too expensive", that might be a value problem in how you explain it, not a price problem.

Worked example. Illustrative numbers.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative numbers"]

Say the business sends 100 quotes a month and wins 25, at about $8,000 a job. A written process and a one-month follow-up lifts wins from 25 to 30. That's five more jobs, about $40,000 more revenue a month, from quotes you'd already paid to produce.

[SCREEN: "100 quotes → 25 → 30 wins × $8,000 = +$40,000/month revenue (illustrative)"]

One warning on close rates. Daniel says if he closes 30–40% of his calls, his price is too low. That's for his high-ticket calls. For quotes, compare to your own history, not his number.

Last, make it runnable. Write the questions on one page. Record calls where the law and the customer allow it, so you can train from the best ones. Keep the tools simple. Daniel spent about $100,000 and six months moving to HubSpot. His reps hated it, and they rolled it back within 14 hours to spreadsheets and a simple CRM.

[SCREEN: "$100K HubSpot migration rolled back in 14 hours." — Daniel Iles]

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

[SCREEN: Checklist]

1. Write the stages from enquiry to deposit on one page.
2. Do or sit in on five quotes or consults yourself, and write down every question and objection.
3. Turn the six questions into your own words for your business.
4. Pull every open quote from the last 30 days and call each one.
5. Start a lost-job log: budget, authority, need or timing.

**The turn (9:00–9:30)**

You can do all of this alone. Most people who do it alone take years and lose deals. Here, alone looks like a new owner who copies a pushy script off the internet, upsets long-time customers and loses staff who've been there 15 years. Or the opposite: changing nothing, so the process retires with the seller. Or you can do it with me. We work out who actually wins the work during due diligence, and we plan the handover around it.

**The offer (9:30–10:30)**

Two ways to work with me.

The Owners Academy. The community, the full course, every template and script I use, and deals discussed every day.

Or 1:1 advisory through G&L M&A Advisory. I work on your strategy, your structures and your lender presentations alongside you.

Book a call with me below. Not a setter, me. There's a short application first. On the call we work out your strategy, your first structure, and whether the Academy or 1:1 suits you.

You also get my NBIO template, which a client was quoted about $7,000 for, and a 1:1 session with Jy Kingston, who built his board in under 30 days.

It's for people putting in 40-plus hours a week who want a roll-up exit, a legacy business or flips, and who'll move before they feel ready. Cash, a business or property helps. It's not for anyone chasing a four-hour week or a lifestyle business, and it's not for anyone who'll keep watching instead of making offers.

**For skeptics (10:30–11:00)**

"My customers hate being sold to." So do I. Good questions aren't selling. They're how you find the right job.

"My team won't follow a script." Daniel's reps memorise the script first, so they can listen instead of thinking about what to say next. Start with one page, not a binder.

"Isn't this just for big companies?" A sales process is one page. A small business needs it more, because it can't afford to lose the jobs.

"Do you know sales?" I made about 1,000 cold calls before my first deal. Every one was a sales conversation with a stranger.

Start with the 3C link, because proof is the whole model.

In Season 1 we talked about stealing credibility. I had no track record at 25, so I borrowed it. I built a board of credible operators, and banks and sellers took me seriously. Proof in a business works the same way. A homeowner who's never heard of you borrows trust from 300 neighbours who left a review.

[JOSH: one line on what proof the physio clinic had when you bought it (reviews, testimonials, nothing) and what you did with it]

Five steps.

**Step one: ask at the moment of the win.** The tradie finishes the job, the customer's happy, the hot water's back on. That's when you ask. Not in a survey three months later. Send a text with a direct review link before the van leaves the street. In a clinic, the moment is when a patient says "I can finally lift my grandkid". Daniel's version is a company-wide wins channel. Yours can be a group chat where the crew posts every good job with a photo.

**Step two: record the "before".** Daniel records baselines when a client starts, so the result has something to compare against. For a trade, that's a before photo. Every job, same angle, every time. For a commercial cleaner, it's the audit score before you took the contract. For a clinic, it's the patient's own measures at the first visit, kept in their record. And be careful with health information. Consent and privacy rules apply.

**Step three: package each result.** Daniel's one-slide format has five parts: the customer's name and face, what they do, a before and after, one headline result, and supporting numbers as "free dessert". For a trades business: "Sarah, Ringwood. Ducted system that kept tripping. Installed in a day. Power bill down." With her permission.

[SCREEN: "Name + face · who they are · before/after · one headline · supporting stats" — Daniel Iles]

He also packages one result four ways, so case studies don't all sound the same: a raw number, a percentage, a multiple, or tied to time. "Installed in one day" hits differently to "saved 30% on power" for different customers.

**Step four: match the proof to the problem.** A customer who says "show me one exactly like mine" usually wants the outcome, not the same suburb. Daniel calls it the "I'm not a plumber" objection. Pick the case that matches their pain. Worried about mess: show the clean-up photo. Worried about price: show the power bill. Worried about trust: show the review count.

And never hand out customers' phone numbers as references. Daniel had 15 prospects say a client call would make them "100% in". One signed. His line: "I can't give you my clients' contact info, but I promise I won't give yours out either."

**Step five: guard the reputation.** The objections you never hear are the ones that hurt. People look you up, see a bad review, and never ring. Daniel's rule: a senior person, not junior support, answers bad reviews in public. In a small business, that's the owner. Calm, specific, no arguing.

Now, where the fit is different, and I want to be honest about it.

Daniel ties his account managers' bonuses to case studies and reviews. Be careful copying that. Platforms like Google generally don't allow incentives for reviews, and consumer law in Australia and the US goes after fake or cherry-picked reviews. Reward the team for asking every customer. Never pay the customer for a good review, and never hide the bad ones.

And in healthcare, it's stricter again. In Australia, health practitioners are generally restricted from using testimonials in advertising. So a physio clinic can't run patient testimonials as ads the way a plumber can. Clinics often lean on education, the team's credentials and the patient's experience instead. Check the rules for your profession before you publish anything.
