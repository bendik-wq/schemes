**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points before the detail.

One. The terms carry the deal, not the price. My clinic was 100% vendor finance. That means the seller got paid over time, out of the business, instead of in a lump sum from me. Seven years, 4% interest, principal and interest. No deposit.

Two. Time is a term. I had a nine-month payment holiday and rent in arrears. That gave the business room to breathe while I learnt how to run it. It's the line most first-time buyers forget to ask for.

Three. The deal before the deal. Before this one closed I had four deals fall over and made around a thousand cold calls. The clinic didn't happen because I was clever. It happened because I'd already been told no enough times to know what yes looked like.

The seller funded the whole purchase. I didn't walk in with a cheque. I walked in with a plan for how they'd get paid.

**Line three: seven years at 4%, principal and interest (4:50)**

Seven years means the seller is paid off in a reasonable window. Four percent means the interest doesn't eat the cash flow. Principal and interest means the debt actually goes down every year, instead of sitting there waiting to bite me later.

Let's put round numbers on it. These are illustrative. They are not my clinic's numbers.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not Josh's clinic. Not a forecast."]

Say the price is $1 million. Seven years at 4%, principal and interest. The repayment works out at roughly $167,000 a year.

Now say the business makes $300,000 a year in free cash flow. $300,000 divided by $167,000 is about 1.8. That's your debt service coverage ratio. My rule is 1.5 minimum. So this one passes, with some room to spare.

If the business made $200,000, you'd be at 1.2. Too tight. You'd go back and change the term, the rate or the price until it passed.

[SCREEN: "$1M · 7 yrs · 4% P&I ≈ $167K/yr · $300K FCF ÷ $167K ≈ 1.8× DSCR (min 1.5×)" / "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

**Line four: the nine-month payment holiday (5:50)**

This is the line I promised you. For the first nine months, no repayments.

Why does that matter so much? Because the first months after you buy a business are the riskiest. Staff are nervous. Patients are watching. You're learning the systems. A holiday means the cash the business makes in that window stays in the business. It builds a buffer before the first repayment ever goes out.

How the holiday is written matters. Sometimes interest still builds up during it. Sometimes it doesn't. Get your lawyer to spell it out.

**Line five: rent in arrears, no deposit (6:30)**

Rent in arrears means paying rent at the end of the period instead of up front. Small line. But when you have no money, every dollar you don't have to find on day one counts.

No deposit means exactly that. Nothing down from me.

**Why this structure worked (8:00)**

Put it together. The seller got paid in full, over time, at a fair rate. The business carried its own debt. The holiday protected the first year. And I didn't need money I didn't have.

Every line solved a problem. That's how you read any term sheet. Ask of each line, what problem does this solve, and for whom?

**How to ask for each line (8:10)**

People ask me how you even bring this stuff up with a seller. You don't lead with the terms. You lead with what they want.

Find out first. Do they want to retire? Do they want the staff looked after? Do they want to stay on for a while? Then each term becomes an answer to something they told you.

"You said you want to step back but keep an eye on things. If you're paid over seven years, you've got a reason to stay close and help it go well."

"You said the first year matters to you because of the team. A few months before the first payment means the business has a cushion while everyone settles in."

Same terms. Framed as their benefit, not your lack of money. And then you put it all in writing in an offer, so nobody remembers it differently a month later.

If a seller says no to one line, don't panic. Trade. A shorter holiday for a slightly better rate. A longer term for a higher price. The maths from line three tells you what you can afford to give.

Four. Make ten cold calls. The clinic came after about a thousand. Start the count.
