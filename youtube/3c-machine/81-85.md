**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why owners are selling (3:00)**

The usual picture. Not always.

A founder, maybe an engineer or a tradesperson, started making one thing in a garage. Thirty years later it's 30 staff, a leased factory and a customer list built on handshakes. The kids went into other careers. The business is too small for most private equity firms, and plenty of individual buyers get scared off by the machinery.

And the reason that matters is simple. The buyer pool can be thin. A thin buyer pool and a tired owner is the exact moment "getting paid over time" sounds good. They get a price, they get looked after, and they don't wait years for a cash buyer who may never turn up.

[B-ROLL: an older owner walking the factory floor, hand on a machine]

**What the bank looks for (4:00)**

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME · THAT'S NOT HOW IT WORKS*

If a bank has ever knocked you back, it had nothing to do with your age, your CV or your savings. It had everything to do with asking a cash-flow lender a question only an asset lender answers.

Most people think a bank lends on profit and nothing else. They think no track record means no loan. That's not how it works. There are two kinds of lending conversation, and you want both.

The first is a cash-flow lender. They look at profit and ask whether it covers the repayments. Our rule is 1.5. Free cash flow has to cover all debt service at least one and a half times.

The second is an asset-based lender. They ask a different question: if this goes wrong, what can we sell?

Debtors. A lender may advance a percentage of the invoices customers owe you. Usually only the good ones. Old or disputed invoices are often left out.

Stock. Often advanced at a lower percentage, because it's harder to sell. Finished goods and raw materials can be treated differently to half-made product.

Plant and equipment. Depends on the valuation, the age, and how easy the machine is to resell.

No percentages from me. Ask every lender for their own advance rates. They vary a lot.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

Two more things banks care about here. Personal guarantees: on bigger deals, roughly over $5 million, banks often don't need one, because an asset-rich business gives them their security. And if a lender asks for security over everything, you've got fallbacks. Security on specific assets only. Or partial security, say on the equipment but not the debtors and stock.

And credibility. A lender wants to see someone on your board who's run a plant before. That's a board seat, not a salary.

**The three numbers (5:15)**

[SCREEN: "1. Biggest customer % · 2. Gross margin, 3 years · 3. Maintenance spend vs depreciation"]

Number one. The biggest customer's share of revenue. In manufacturing it's common to have one big contract. If one or two customers are more than about 30% of revenue, that's a red flag. Then check whether their contract survives a change of owner. Some don't.

Number two. Gross margin over three years. Material costs move. If margin is slipping, and an accounting change is hiding it, walk away or price it in. Ask for margin by product line, not one blended number.

Number three. Maintenance. An owner who's planning to sell can stop spending on the machines. Profit looks better. Then the machine dies in your third month. Compare what they've spent on equipment over three years with the depreciation, and pay an engineer to walk the floor before you sign.

**A worked example (6:15)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Illustrative, round numbers, not a real deal.

A manufacturer turns over $5 million and makes $600,000 profit after paying a manager to run the floor. You offer 4 times profit. $2.4 million.

Say an asset-based lender funds $1.2 million against debtors and stock. The seller is paid the other $1.2 million over time.

Now the 1.5 rule. $600,000 divided by 1.5 is $400,000. That's the most all your repayments can be in a year. Say the bank piece costs $250,000 a year and the seller's piece $150,000. That's $400,000. Exactly on the line.

[SCREEN: "$600K ÷ 1.5 = $400K max debt service · Bank $250K + Seller $150K = $400K"]

No headroom. So you'd go back and ask for a payment holiday on the seller's piece, a longer term, or a lower price. The maths tells you what to negotiate.

**What usually goes wrong (7:15)**

A common trap here. Debtors and stock are your working capital. If you use them to fund the purchase, that same facility can't also carry you through a slow month. You've borrowed your buffer. So line up a working capital line at settlement separately, and make sure it's actually there on the day.

The other usual problems. The big customer leaves because their relationship was with the owner. The one operator who can set up the old machine retires too. So interview the staff before close, and think about retention bonuses for the key people.

**How to grow it after (7:50)**

First lever: price. It's common to find small manufacturers that haven't repriced properly in years. Daniel Iles teaches this with his own numbers: at a $5,000 price and $3,500 delivery cost, a 20% price rise lifts profit 67%, and a 20% cut drops it 70%. His example is a service business, but the maths is the same on a factory floor.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · '$36K 1-1 training' · 2:14:38 · link in description"]

*// TIMELINE LANGUAGE*

So the order goes like this. First you fix price. Then, once margin is back where it should be, you add a salesperson, because many of these businesses have never had one. The owner's phone was the sales team. And once you have a sales process working, you buy the small competitor down the road. That's the roll-up.

Three. Find one person who's run a factory. Message them about a board seat.

Four. Write the three numbers on one page and take it to every conversation.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. Plenty of transport businesses were started by an owner-driver with one truck. Decades later there might be 20 trucks, and the owner is still answering the phone at four in the morning. That's a seller who's tired, and tired sellers listen to "getting paid over time."

Two. The bank will look hard at the fleet and the contracts. How old the trucks are, what's still owing on them, and whether the work is written down anywhere. Fleet finance that's already in place counts against the same profit you're using to pay the seller.

Three. The three numbers. Margin after fuel, drivers and repairs. The age of the fleet and the debt on it. And how much of the work sits under contracts that pass on fuel costs.

The headline: in a thin-margin business, small moves in cost are big moves in profit. You buy it on profit, never on revenue.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

**Why owners are selling (3:00)**

The common story goes something like this.

The owner started driving decades ago. Built it truck by truck. Knows every customer by first name. And it's a hard business to run. Drivers can be hard to find and keep, rules on safety and fatigue keep getting heavier, and the owner is often the dispatcher, the mechanic's boss and the debt collector all at once.

Their kids have watched how hard it is, and a lot of them don't want it. So the owner has a fleet, a customer list and nobody to hand it to.

That's the burden of ownership. They don't want to wait months for a bank buyer. They want out, and they want the business looked after. Getting paid over time gives them both.

[B-ROLL: an owner in a hi-vis vest at a whiteboard of runs and driver names]

**What the bank looks for (4:00)**

A lender looks at three things here.

The fleet. How old it is, what it's worth on the open market, and what's still owed. Trucks are often bought on equipment finance. When you buy the business, that finance either comes with it or has to be paid out.

The contracts. Is the work under written agreements, or is it a handshake with a customer who's used them for twenty years? A handshake isn't nothing. But a bank can't lend against a handshake.

The compliance record. Safety, fatigue, maintenance logs. In regulated freight, a bad record is a risk to the whole business, not just a fine.

[SCREEN: "Typically; every lender and deal is different. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

The fleet is security, so this is closer to the asset-rich end of the spectrum. On bigger deals, roughly over $5 million, a personal guarantee often isn't needed for that reason. Typically. Ask each lender.

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME · WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING*

If you've ever overvalued a business, it had nothing to do with being bad at maths. It had everything to do with how businesses get talked about. Owners lead with revenue. Listings lead with revenue. Everyone quotes the top line.

You were told a bigger top line means a bigger business. You were told the trucks in the yard are assets. You were told a twenty-year customer is a safe customer. None of that is true until you check it. Here's what you check.

**The three numbers (5:00)**

[SCREEN: "1. Margin after fuel, drivers, repairs · 2. Fleet age + debt owing · 3. Contract cover + fuel pass-through"]

Number one. Margin after the big three costs: fuel, drivers and repairs. Ask for it by customer or by run. You'll often find one or two customers the business is quietly losing money on.

Number two. Fleet age and the debt on it. Old trucks mean repairs now and replacement soon. Get every finance agreement and every payout figure. That's part of what you're really paying.

Number three. Contract cover. How much of revenue is under written contracts, how long they run, and whether they let the business pass on fuel increases. Add the usual concentration test: if one or two customers are over about 30% of revenue, that's a red flag.

**A worked example (5:45)**

[SCREEN: "Illustrative example. Round numbers. Not a real deal."]

Illustrative. Round numbers. Not a real deal.

A transport business turns over $4 million. Profit is $400,000. That's 10%. Thin margins are common in this industry, so let's assume thin.

The 1.5 rule. $400,000 divided by 1.5 is about $266,000. That's the most all your debt can cost each year.

Now the bit people miss. The trucks are on finance, and those repayments are $150,000 a year. That's debt service too. So only $116,000 a year is left for the seller. Over ten years that's about $1.16 million.

[SCREEN: "$400K ÷ 1.5 = ~$266K · minus $150K truck finance = ~$116K/yr to the seller"]

That's your ceiling, not your opening offer. The truck loans are part of the price.

Now one shock. Say fuel is $1.2 million a year, and it goes up 5% with no way to pass it on. That's $60,000. Profit drops from $400,000 to $340,000. A 5% move in one cost wiped out 15% of profit. That's why number three matters.

[SCREEN: "Fuel $1.2M × 5% = $60K → profit $400K → $340K (−15%)"]

**What usually goes wrong (7:00)**

The usual ones. The buyer prices the deal on revenue because it sounds big. The fleet debt gets found late in due diligence. The best customer was loyal to the owner, not the business. Or the key drivers leave in the first month because nobody spoke to them.

Two fixes. Meet all the staff before close and talk to the senior drivers yourself. And negotiate a payment holiday on the seller's piece. The reason that matters is the first few months are when you're learning the business, and a slow patch then shouldn't break you. Payments delayed, full price still paid. Sellers tend to agree once the headline price is settled.

Customers in this industry often pay on 30 days or more, while you pay fuel and wages every week. So you want a working capital line in place at settlement. My good friend Cam got a $500,000 working capital line from the bank on his deals. Different industry, same principle: don't start owning a business with an empty tank.

*// NOT-YOUR-FAULT FRAME · WHAT YOU WERE TOLD WAS LYING*

If you've spent months unable to choose, it had nothing to do with not being smart enough or not having read enough. It had everything to do with having no way to compare one industry with another.

You were told to buy what you love. You were told to wait for the perfect industry. You were told to read everything first. None of that tells you whether a bank will fund it or whether it can grow. This does.

[SCREEN: the scoring sheet. Eight rows, each scored 1 to 5. Total out of 40]

Eight lines. Score each one from 1 to 5. Five is good.

Part one is buyable. Five lines.

**1. Bankable (3:15)**

Will a lender fund a business like this? Asset-rich businesses give the bank security. Steady cash flow gives it comfort. Don't guess. Ask three lenders about their appetite for this sector. That's 20 minutes on the phone and it's the most important line on the sheet.

**2. Boring (3:40)**

Traditional, stable, still needed in ten years. Daniel Iles put it well: "New is fun, but fun will keep you broke… boring is where real wealth gets built." Score down anything technology is about to replace, or anything that's a fad.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'Made $3M in a month' · 20:10 · link in description"]

**3. Fragmented (4:05)**

Lots of small owners, nobody dominant. That's what gives you a roll-up later: buy at small-business multiples, combine, sell the group at a higher one. An industry already owned by three giants scores a 1.

**4. Retiring owners (4:25)**

Are a lot of owners in this industry in their fifties and sixties with no successor? That's the burden of ownership, and it's what makes "getting paid over time" land.

**5. Margins (4:40)**

Healthy, steady profit. A useful floor is about 15% net. Thin margins aren't automatically a no. Transport often runs thin. But they leave less room for the 1.5 rule.

Part two is growable. Daniel's three caps.

**6. Location cap (5:00)**

Daniel's first business was real estate. Twenty units by his early twenties. But his local market capped him at about three to five deals a year. Location was the ceiling.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · 'You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers' · 0:30 · link in description"]

So ask: can this business only ever serve one suburb, one building, one town? A single childcare centre scores low on its own. A group of them doesn't, because you get past the cap by buying more sites.

**7. Key-person cap (5:35)**

His second was a creator business. Big following, sponsored videos. But the money was seasonal and he was the bottleneck. Key person was the ceiling.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · same video · 2:02"]

So ask: does revenue in this industry usually depend on one person? The owner who quotes every job. The star practitioner patients ask for by name. If yes, score it low, or plan how you'll fix it in the first year.

**8. Platform cap (6:05)**

His third was a publishing business on Snapchat. It got to about $250,000 a month, then the platform cut pay in half and froze new shows. The platform was the ceiling.

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles · same video · 4:37"]

In the businesses we buy, the platform is often one big customer, one funder, one supplier or one referral source. A manufacturer living off one contract. A training college living off one funding stream. Score it low.

And one question to settle ties, straight from Daniel's rule: can revenue be bought with ads or outbound? If yes, you can grow on purpose.

**Kill rules (6:35)**

[SCREEN: "Kill rules: Bankable = 1 → stop · Any cap = 1 with no fix → stop · Under 28/40 → keep looking"]

Kill rules. If bankable scores a 1, stop. No lender, no deal. If any cap scores a 1 and you can't see a fix, stop. And under about 28 out of 40, keep looking. That number is my rule of thumb, not science. Set your own, then stick to it.

Illustrative, and it'll be different in your city.
