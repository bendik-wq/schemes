### Script

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**

[SCREEN: a tally counter running from 0 to 1,000.]

The business you want to buy probably isn't for sale. It's not on a website and it's not with a broker. It's owned by someone nobody has asked yet. Before I bought my first business I made about a thousand cold calls and watched four deals fall over. Then one didn't.

**[0:20–0:45] The map**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. Credibility and Capital ticked. "Closing" lit, sub-label "Finding deals, 1 of 5".]

This is the 3C map. We've done Credibility and we've done Capital. Last episode was debt over dilution: how you fund a deal without handing your company to investors. None of that matters until there's a seller on the phone. So this is Closing, finding deals, episode 1 of 5.

It's for people who'll give this 40 hours a week and pick up the phone before they feel ready. If you want a listing to land in your inbox while you sleep, this one isn't for you.

**[0:45–2:30] The recipe, fast**

Three things.

One. Go off-market. Direct to the owner. The owner you call hasn't listed, hasn't hired a broker and isn't running an auction. You're the only buyer on the line. That's where good terms come from.

Two. Volume before judgement. A thousand calls is the unit. Not fifty. Most people make fifty calls, hear no forty times, rewrite the script, buy a new list and start again. Fifty calls tells you nothing. A thousand tells you most of what you need to know.

Three. Run it like a system, not a mood. A list. A fixed calling block every day. A simple tracker. And follow-up for six to twelve months, because a lot of your deals will come from owners who said "not yet" the first time.

[SCREEN: "1. Off-market, direct to owner. 2. 1,000 calls is the unit. 3. List, block, tracker, follow-up."]

That's the lesson. The rest of this video is how.

**[2:30–3:00] Soft pitch**

I help people do this. Strategy, list, scripts, first offers, either in the Owners Academy or one-on-one. If you'd rather not do your first thousand calls on your own, book a call with me below. It's me on the call, not a setter. Now the detail.

**[3:00–8:30] The lesson**

*Why off-market*

There's a generation of owners in traditional industries who are ready to retire. Clinics, plumbers, cleaning companies, electricians. They've carried the business for twenty or thirty years. That's the burden of ownership. They want out, and they want their people looked after.

Most of them have never listed. Listing means a broker, a glossy information memorandum, months of tyre-kickers and a bank process that can drag on for sixteen weeks. Plenty of owners would rather not.

When you call one directly, three things are true. There's no auction, so you're not bidding against cash buyers or private equity. You can talk about the seller getting paid over time, which a listed sale rarely starts with. And you set the pace. A deal where the seller is paid over time can close in two to four weeks instead of six to sixteen.

[SCREEN: "Listed: auction, cash buyers, 6–16 weeks. Off-market: one buyer, terms, 2–4 weeks."]

One rule while we're here. Never say "vendor finance" to a seller. Say "getting paid over time." Same thing. One sounds like a loan. The other sounds like a pension.

*My thousand*

When I started I was 25. Unemployed, no savings, zero experience. No network to introduce me to deals. So I called. Around a thousand calls before the first deal, and four deals that fell over first.

The one that closed was a physio clinic. 100% vendor finance, seven years at 4% principal and interest, a nine-month payment holiday, rent in arrears, no deposit. I pulled $150K out in the first year.

[SCREEN: "Physio clinic: 100% vendor finance · 7 yrs at 4% P&I · 9-month payment holiday · no deposit · ~$150K out in year one. Results not typical; records on file. Education, not financial or legal advice."]

I'm not telling you that to sound clever. The thousand calls and the four failures are the part nobody puts in the thumbnail. They were the training.

*Volume before judgement*

Daniel Iles built a business doing around five million dollars a month, mostly from paid ads. His rule for ads: no account runs profitably in 2026 with fewer than about 100 creatives. Get to 100, then launch. Below that, he says, you're "essentially doing nothing."

[SCREEN: "Daniel Iles: ~100 creatives before you launch." Source: "I scaled to $3M/mo with social media. Ask Me Anything", 7:18. Link in description.]

Different channel, same law. You don't judge an ad account on three ads, and you don't judge cold calling on fifty calls. Your first hundred calls you'll read off the page and sound nervous. By about three hundred you'll sound like a person. By six hundred you'll hear an owner's tone in the first sentence and adjust. You can't skip to six hundred. You can only get there.

He's got another line I like. Hate losing more than you want to win, because wins are rare. If the only thing you count is deals, you'll quit in week three. Count calls.

*Build the list*

Pick one industry and one city. Not five industries. One. You want to sound like you know the space by week two.

Then build a list of one to two thousand businesses. Industry directories, associations, map searches, company registers. Owner's name, phone, email, suburb and a rough revenue guess. Aim for businesses doing somewhere between half a million and ten million in revenue. Under about a million in value, a deal rarely throws off enough cash to pay the debt and pay you.

*Set the block*

Calls happen in a fixed block, same time every day. Mornings suit most owners. Forty to fifty calls in a three-hour block is a realistic pace once the list is in front of you.

Afternoons are for follow-up. The emails you promised. The "call me Thursday" people. A LinkedIn connection to every owner you spoke to. Last thing, update the tracker and set tomorrow's list.

*The tracker*

A spreadsheet is fine. Columns: business, owner, phone, first contact, last contact, status, next action date, notes. Status runs Cold, Warm, Hot, Financials, Offer, Due diligence, Close.

[SCREEN: tracker with the seven statuses and a highlighted "Next action date" column.]

The column that makes money is "next action date." Every owner who says "not now" gets a date. Six weeks, three months, six months, a year. Owners' lives change. Health. A partner wants out. The kids say no to taking it on. The person who calls back in the right month gets the deal.

*A worked example*

Round numbers, to show the shape. Illustrative, not a forecast.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only: 50 calls a day × 4 days = 200 a week. 1,000 calls = 5 weeks."]

Fifty calls a day, four days a week, is two hundred calls a week. A thousand calls is five weeks. People spend longer than that picking a company name.

In episode 19 we'll do the maths on what comes out of a thousand calls: conversations, NDAs, offers. For now, know that the input is five weeks of work, not a lucky break.

*What it looks like when it works*

Saul is an Owners Academy member. He found an electrical and air-conditioning business by cold call, direct to the vendor. Twenty-five staff, about $3.8 million in revenue, owner retiring in two years. He's signed the NDA and he's in early due diligence.

[SCREEN: "Saul, Owners Academy member: NDA signed, early due diligence. ~$3.8M revenue, 25 staff. Found by cold call. Results not typical; records on file."]

No broker. No auction. One phone call that turned into a conversation.

**[8:30–9:00] Do this this week**

[SCREEN: checklist, ticking as Josh reads.]

One. Pick one industry and one city. Write them down.

Two. Build the first two hundred names of your list. Owner name and phone at minimum.

Three. Put a calling block in your calendar four mornings this week. Treat it like a meeting you can't move.

Four. Set up the tracker with the seven statuses and a next action date.

Five. Make your first fifty calls. Write down every objection word for word. Don't fix the script yet.

## Episode 17 · "Have you ever thought about selling?"

### Metadata

- **Title options**
  1. "Have You Ever Thought About Selling?" The Question That Finds Deals

  3. What to Say When a Business Owner Picks Up
- **Thumbnail text:** ASK IN 20 SECONDS
- **Runtime target:** 11–12 min (about 1,650 words spoken)
- **Stage lit:** Closing, finding deals, episode 2 of 5.
- **Picks up from:** 16, 1,000 cold calls. **Hands off to:** 18, Why we don't buy from brokers.

### Script

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**

[SCREEN: white text on black: "Have you ever thought about selling?"]

Six words. It's the most useful sentence I know for buying a business. Most people bury it under two minutes of small talk and a speech about themselves. By the time they get to it, the owner's gone. Ask it inside twenty seconds.

**[0:20–0:45] The map**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. "Closing" lit, sub-label "Finding deals, 2 of 5".]

Back on the 3C map. Closing, finding deals, episode 2 of 5. Last episode was the thousand calls: why off-market, and how to run outreach as a system. This one is what you actually say when someone picks up.

It's for people who'll be making those calls this month. If you'd rather email a broker and wait, skip this one. Episode 18 explains why that's slower.

**[0:45–2:30] The recipe, fast**

Three things.

One. Earn ten seconds, then ask. Your name, your company, one line on what you're building, then the question. No "how are you going today." The owner knows that line means a sales call.

Two. Then stop talking. Discovery before pitch. On the first call you don't mention a price, a multiple or a structure. The less you say about the deal, the more leverage you keep.

Three. Find out who decides, and leave with a next step. Spouse, business partner, the kids, the accountant. Someone else nearly always has a say. And you hang up with either an NDA going out today or a date to call back.

[SCREEN: "1. Ten seconds, then the question. 2. Discovery before pitch. 3. Who decides + next step."]

That's the call. Now the words, and a worked example.

**[2:30–3:00] Soft pitch**

I help people do this, from the first script to the signed offer. Some in the Owners Academy, some one-on-one. If you want someone listening to your calls and telling you what to fix, book a call with me below. Me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**[3:00–8:30] The lesson**

*The words*

Here's the opener, word for word.

[SCREEN: "Hi [Name], this is [your name] with [company]. I'm building a company in [industry] and wanted to introduce myself. Have you ever thought about selling [business name]?"]

Four parts. Your name and company, so you sound like a business, not someone selling solar panels. "I'm building a company in your industry," so you sound like a buyer with a plan. That line only works if it's true, which is why Credibility comes first on the map. "Wanted to introduce myself," which tells them this is a relationship, not a one-off. Then the question.

Why "ever thought about"? Because it's soft. You're not asking "do you want to sell." Nobody has to commit to anything. It's easy for an owner to say, "Yeah, actually, I have." And some do.

*The three answers*

You'll get one of three.

"Yes, actually." Don't pitch. Ask, "What's got you thinking about it?" Then listen.

"Not right now." Say, "No problem at all. I'm building relationships in the industry. Mind if I check back in six to twelve months?" Then put a date in the tracker. Saul, one of our Owners Academy members, found an electrical and air-conditioning business by cold call. The owner is retiring in two years, not tomorrow. Saul's still in early due diligence now, with the NDA signed. A "not yet" owner is still an owner.

[SCREEN: "Saul, Owners Academy member: NDA signed, early due diligence. Found by cold call, owner retiring in 2 years. Results not typical; records on file."]

"No," or the click. Say, "No worries, thanks for your time." Mark it in the tracker. Next call.

*When calls die early*

Daniel Iles had Matt Ryder on his channel. Matt has run big setter teams, and his advice on phone calls is to track the ones that end in under fifteen seconds, because that means the opener needs work. And there's a point in there I like: "I'm busy" is usually a brush-off, because busy people don't answer unknown numbers.

[SCREEN: Matt Ryder on Daniel Iles' channel, "Scaling 9 figure sales teams (Raw Conversation)", 17:31. Link in description.]

So count them. If a lot of your calls die in under fifteen seconds, the problem is your first sentence, not the industry. The same conversation flags calls that die around two minutes. That means you're not getting from the opener to anything real. On a seller call, that's usually because you started talking about yourself.

*Seconds twenty to sixty: discovery*

Daniel teaches his reps that they have the most leverage in discovery, before they've said anything about the program, the price or the start date. After the pitch, the prospect has it.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "How To Overcome Timing Objections", 1:01.]

Same with an owner. The moment you say a number, that's the number. So on call one you ask, you don't tell. A few questions that work:

[SCREEN: the questions appear one by one.]

"What's got you thinking about it?" Keep it broad and let them tell you.

"How long have you been running it?"

"Is anyone in the family keen to take it on?"

"If you did step back, what would matter most to you? The staff, the name, the customers?" That's the burden of ownership talking. Most owners want to be looked after and want their people looked after.

And the one most people skip: "Who else would have a say in this? A partner, your spouse, your accountant?" Daniel's first question on his own sales script is about authority. Is it just you, or is there a team? If someone else decides, you want them on the next call, not hearing about it secondhand over dinner.

Don't fire these off like a form. Daniel says calls with business owners should feel like two business people talking. Owners spot a rigid script, and once they see the process, trust drops. Learn the questions, then have a conversation.

*What not to say*

Don't say "vendor finance." Say "getting paid over time." Don't quote a multiple. Don't explain the 3C model. And don't say you've got no money.

If they ask how you'd pay for it, try: "We'd look at a few options once I've seen the numbers. Some owners like getting paid over time, some want more up front. It depends what suits you."

[SCREEN: "Education, not financial or legal advice."]

*A worked example*

This is an illustrative call. Not a real seller.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative call. Not a real seller."]

"Hi Gary, this is Josh with [company]. I'm building a company in commercial cleaning here in Melbourne and wanted to introduce myself. Have you ever thought about selling the business?"

"Funny you ask. My wife's been on at me about it."

"Yeah? What's got you both thinking about it?"

"I'm sixty-three. Back's had it. Kids aren't interested."

"Fair enough. If you did step back, what would matter most?"

"The staff. Some of them have been with me fifteen years."

"That makes sense. Would your wife want to be part of the conversation if we sat down?"

"Probably, yeah."

"Good. I'll send through an NDA today so you're comfortable sharing a few numbers, and we'll find a time next week for the three of us."

Look at what I didn't say. No price, no structure, no "vendor finance," nothing about me. Gary did most of the talking. I found out why he'd sell, what he cares about, and who else decides. And I left with a next step.

*The next step*

Send the NDA within the hour. Not tomorrow. Whether they sign it, and how fast, is your first read on how serious they are. We'll go deep on that in episode 21.

**[8:30–9:00] Do this this week**

[SCREEN: checklist.]

One. Write your opener using the four parts. Say it out loud twenty times until it sounds like you.

Two. Record yourself on five calls and listen back. Count how many words you said versus the owner.

Three. Make fifty calls. Tally every call that ends in under fifteen seconds.

Four. On every "yes," ask who else has a say. Write it down.

Five. Send every NDA within an hour of the call.

At this step it goes wrong in small ways that cost big. They pitch on the first call. They quote a multiple to sound smart, and the owner anchors on it forever. Add-backs doing a lot of work. If "adjusted" profit is miles above the profit on the tax return, ask why.

Financials and tax returns that don't match. That's a walk-away, listed or not.

"We've got other buyers interested." Sometimes true. Often it isn't. Either way, don't let it rush you. Episode 23 is about walking away.

Artificial deadlines. "Offers by Friday." You don't do your best work on someone else's clock.

The listing has sat for six months or more. The cash buyers have had their look and passed. The seller is more open now than on day one.

The broker is open to structure. Some are, once they know you're serious and have a credible team behind you.

Even when you don't buy from them, brokers know things you want to know. What's selling in your industry. What multiples are getting paid. Which owners have been asking about value but never listed. Take them for a coffee. Ask what they're seeing. You're building a picture of the market, and a broker who likes you might send the odd stale listing your way before anyone else sees it.

Just remember whose side they're on. They work for the seller. Be friendly, be straight, and never tell a broker your walk-away number.

Commercial services. Cleaning, logistics, waste. Contracts, vehicles, equipment, repeat work.

Professional services. Accounting practices and similar. Recurring clients, steady fees.

What they share: traditional, fragmented, lots of owners nearing retirement. Fragmented matters for later, because it's what makes a roll-up possible. Buy several at small-business prices, combine them, and the group is worth more. That's Act 7.

*Daniel's three caps*

Daniel Iles had three businesses hit a ceiling before one worked. A real estate portfolio, bought off-market with creative finance, capped by location: a small local market meant about three to five deals a year. A creator business, capped by him: he was the bottleneck, and sponsor money was seasonal. And a publishing business on Snapchat, capped by the platform, when it cut pay in half.

[SCREEN: "Daniel's caps: location · key person · platform." Source: "You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers", 0:30, 2:02, 4:37.]

His rule after that: never get serious about a business where you can't control how much revenue it makes. His point: a real business has a system for acquiring customers, where more input reliably brings more output. A business that lives purely on referrals can't decide to grow this month.

So ask of every target: can revenue be bought, with ads or outbound? Or is it capped?

Here's the nuance for buyers. Plenty of great boring businesses run on referrals today. That's fine. You're buying the cash flow, and installing a way to buy customers is something you do after closing. We'll cover that in Act 6. The cap that should worry you most is the owner. If the owner is the product, the customers might leave with them.

*The scorecard*

Seven yes-or-no questions. Score an industry first, then each business.

[SCREEN: the scorecard, each line ticking.]

One. Traditional, and trading for ten years or more?

Two. Owners nearing retirement, with no obvious successor?

Three. Assets a bank can lend against?

Four. Repeat or recurring revenue?

Five. Healthy margins. Something like fifteen percent net or better.

Six. No single customer bigger than about thirty percent of revenue?

Seven. Can revenue be bought, or is the cap fixable?

You can answer most of these from the outside before you ever call. How long the business has been trading. How many vans in the photos. Reviews that mention the same staff for years. Whether the website talks about contracts or one-off jobs. Whether the owner's name is on everything. Ten minutes of homework per business is enough to score it.

And a size check. Under about a million dollars in value, a deal rarely has enough cash flow to pay the debt and pay you. Five or more yeses, keep going. Fewer, move on.

*Red flags*

The ones that make me walk, or at least slow right down.

Financials and tax returns that don't match. One or two customers making up a big share of revenue. A seller who wants out next week with no handover. Licensed businesses with compliance problems. Industries being eaten by technology. Commodity businesses on thin margins. And, again, an owner who is the business.

*A worked example*

Two businesses. Made up. Round numbers. Illustrative.

[SCREEN: "Illustrative only. Not real businesses." Two cards side by side.]

Business A. Commercial cleaning. $300K profit. Forty contracts, none more than ten percent of revenue. Vans and equipment. Owner is sixty-four and happy to hand over across eighteen months.

Business B. A boutique marketing agency. Also $300K profit. Two clients make up sixty percent of revenue. The owner is the face of the business. The assets are laptops.

Same profit. Which one does a bank lend against? Which one does a seller agree to be paid over time on, knowing the business will keep paying them? It's A, both times. B might be a fine business. It's just not a first acquisition with little to no money down.
