# 21. The cold call that finds off-market deals

The best acquisition you'll ever make probably starts with a 20-second phone call.

Most owners never make it. Or they make it badly: two minutes of small talk, then a price to sound serious.

Here's the call, word for word.

"Hi [name], this is [your name] with [your company]. I'm building a company in [industry] and wanted to introduce myself. Have you ever thought about selling [business]?"

That's it.

Name and company, so you sound like a business. "Building a company in your industry", so you sound like a buyer with a plan. "Wanted to introduce myself", which says relationship, not one-off.

Then the question. It's soft on purpose.

"Are you looking to sell?" is a yes-or-no about today, and today the answer is nearly always no.

"Have you ever thought about selling?" lets them tell you about the bad year, the sore back, the kids who don't want it.

Three answers come back.

Yes, actually. Don't pitch. Ask "What's got you thinking about it?" and stop talking.

Not right now. "No problem. I'm building relationships in the industry. Mind if I check back in six months?" Put the date in your diary.

No. "No worries, thanks for your time." Next call.

And never say a number on the first call. The first number you say becomes the number.

If you already run a $1M+ business in the industry, you're the most credible person who could make this call. You're not a broker or a fund. You're the obvious successor.

# 23. How to pick the industry for a roll-up

The industry you pick decides most of your roll-up before you buy anything.

Pick well and banks lend, sellers say yes, and the group sells at a higher multiple. Pick badly and you fight the industry every year.

Here's what I look for.

1. Lots of small owners. You want a fragmented industry with hundreds of businesses doing $1M to $10M. That's your pipeline.

2. Owners getting ready to retire. A generation in traditional industries is ready to step back, with no succession plan. They're the sellers who say yes to being paid over time.

3. Boring, steady demand. Health, trades, accounting, maintenance. People need it in a good year and a bad one. Banks love that.

4. Something to lend against. Equipment, vehicles, receivables, contracts. Asset-rich businesses give a lender security without a personal guarantee from you.

5. A reason the group is worth more than the parts. Shared back office, shared buying, a brand, a manager layer. That's what lets you buy at around 4 times profit and sell the group for more.

6. You understand it. If you already run a business in the industry, you know what a good one looks like from the inside. That's worth more than any spreadsheet.

Most owners already sit in an industry that ticks four or five of these. They just think of their competitors as competition, not as a pipeline.

I built my group in healthcare. The same logic works in plumbing, HVAC, accounting, vet clinics, cleaning and plenty more.

# 24. Buy your competitor

The fastest way to double your business is usually sitting a few kilometres away.

It's your competitor.

Buying a competitor means their revenue, their team and their customers join yours in one move. No five-year plan. One deal.

And it's often cheaper than it looks, because two businesses in the same industry share more than you think.

Where the money comes from:

- One back office. One payroll, one bookkeeper, one software stack instead of two.
- Better buying. Twice the volume with the same suppliers.
- Fuller schedules. Your team and theirs, routed together.
- Cross-selling. Their customers buy your services, yours buy theirs.
- A higher multiple. A bigger, more diversified business is usually valued at more times profit than either one alone.

How to approach them:

Not as a rival. As a successor. "I'm building a group in the industry. Have you ever thought about what happens to the business when you step back?"

Competitors often respect each other more than they let on. Many would rather hand over to someone who knows the trade than to a fund.

How it's paid for:

Senior debt from a bank, seller finance paid over time, and seller rollover where they keep a stake. Little or none of your own cash. The business you buy has to carry its own repayments, about 1.5 times over, so it never leans on yours.

The owners who grow fastest stopped seeing competitors as competition. They see them as the pipeline.

# 25. Questions to ask a seller before you make an offer

The questions you ask a seller decide what you'll pay more than any negotiation ever will.

Ask these before you say a number. Five areas.

1. Why are you selling?
Retirement, health, burnout, a partner leaving. The real reason tells you how flexible they'll be on price, timing and how they get paid. "I'm tired" is a very different seller to "I need the cash by June."

2. How reliable are the earnings?
Three years of financials and the matching tax returns. If the two don't agree, ask why before you go any further.

3. How much does the business depend on you, and on any one customer?
Who do the top clients call? Who quotes the jobs? What share of revenue is the biggest customer? Above about 30% is a risk you'll need to deal with in the structure.

4. What's going on that I can't see yet?
Leases, disputes, contracts that end if the business changes hands, equipment that's overdue for replacement, staff entitlements owed.

5. How would you like to be paid?
This is the one most buyers skip. Plenty of owners care more about the right successor, their staff and a steady income than a lump sum on day one. If they'd consider being paid over time, or keeping a stake, the deal changes completely.

Then listen. The seller will tell you how to buy their business if you let them.

The mistake is going in with a price. The win is going in with questions.

# 26. Never name a price before these 3 documents

The P&L says $500K profit. The tax return says $350K.

At four times profit, that gap is $600K of price.

Which is why I have one rule when buying a business: no documents, no number.

The first number you say becomes the anchor for the whole deal. Say it too early and every conversation after is you climbing down from it.

Before any price, ask for three things.

1. Three years of financials. Profit and loss plus balance sheet. One year can be a fluke: a big contract, a bad winter, an insurance payout. Three shows the pattern. The balance sheet tells you what the business owes: tax, staff entitlements, equipment loans. Those become your problem on settlement day.

2. The matching tax returns. This is the one that sets the price. The financials are what the owner tells you. The tax return is what the owner told the government. If they don't match, one of them is wrong, and banks only lend against what can be proven.

3. Revenue broken down by year. By service line and by customer if you can get it. It shows you what's growing, what's shrinking, and how much depends on one client.

And the way a seller sends them tells you more than anything they say on the phone. Fast and complete: a serious seller. Slow and partial: a story you'll be paying for.

Most buyers think naming a number early shows they're serious. It doesn't. It shows the seller you'll pay for a story.

# 27. Steal this offer structure

The offer that gets a 100% seller-financed deal signed fits on two pages.

Not a 40-page contract. Two pages.

Here's what's on them.

1. The price. One number, based on profit you've verified, not the seller's adjusted figure.

2. How it's paid. This is where most buyers get it wrong. They write "$1.5M" and stop. Write the shape instead: how much at settlement, how much paid over time, over how many years, at what rate, and whether there's a payment holiday at the start.

3. The security. What the seller holds until they're paid out, usually security over the business itself. This is what makes "paid over time" feel safe to them.

4. The handover. How long they'll stay, in what role, and what they're paid for it.

5. Conditions and timing. Due diligence, finance, a target settlement date, exclusivity while you do the work.

Now the bit that gets it signed.

Size the payments to the profit. Take the business's profit and divide by 1.5. That's what it can pay each year and still have a buffer. A business making $250K profit can pay about $166K a year. Over ten years, $1.66M. Often more than a cash buyer would offer.

And never write "vendor finance" in the letter. Write "paid over time". One sounds like a risk. The other sounds like a pension.

My own first deal was this shape: seven years at 4%, a nine-month payment holiday, no deposit.

# 28. The letter of intent

The letter of intent is the most important document in a business purchase, and most buyers treat it as a formality.

An LOI is a short document where buyer and seller agree the main terms before full due diligence and contracts. Price, funding, timing, exclusivity, key conditions.

Most of it isn't binding. But it sets the frame for everything after it. Whatever isn't in the LOI, you'll be fighting for later, from a weaker position.

What goes in mine:

- Price and how it's calculated. A multiple of verified profit, so if diligence finds less profit, the price moves with it.
- The funding shape. Bank debt, seller finance, seller rollover. The seller should see exactly how they get paid.
- Working capital. What level of debtors, stock and creditors comes with the business, so the seller can't empty it before settlement.
- Exclusivity. 60 to 90 days where they can't shop your deal to someone else. This part is usually binding.
- Conditions. Due diligence, finance approval, key staff and lease transfers.
- The handover. How long the seller stays and what they do.
- Confidentiality. Also usually binding.

Here's why it matters so much. An LOI with clear structure gets you a seller who's mentally sold, a lender who can see the deal, and 60 to 90 days of exclusivity to do the work properly.

A vague LOI gets you a seller who's still shopping.

# 29. Due diligence red flags

Due diligence isn't about finding a perfect business. There isn't one.

It's about finding out what the business is actually worth before you've paid for what the seller says it's worth.

The red flags I look for first:

1. Earnings that don't match the bank statements. If the P&L says one thing and the deposits say another, stop.

2. Add-backs with no evidence. "That was a one-off" should come with paperwork. If it happens every year, it isn't a one-off.

3. One customer carrying the business. Above about 30% of revenue from one client is a risk you price or structure for.

4. The owner is the business. If the top clients call the owner's mobile, the relationships may walk out with them.

5. Contracts that end on a sale. Some customer contracts and leases have change-of-control clauses. Read them.

6. Deferred spending. Vans that should have been replaced two years ago. That cost lands on you in year one.

7. Debts you weren't told about. Tax owed, staff leave owed, equipment finance, disputes. On settlement day, they're yours unless the deal says otherwise.

8. Profit that isn't turning into cash. If debtor days are stretching every year, the profit is sitting in other people's bank accounts.

A red flag isn't always a reason to walk. Often it's a reason to change the price, the structure, or who carries the risk.

That's the skill. Not spotting the problem, but knowing what to do with it.

# 30. Three negotiation mistakes that cost $500K

Two buyers look at the same business. $300K profit. The seller asks both for $2.5M.

One pays it. The other says "No worries. Good luck." and ends up with the better deal.

On this business, $500K of price is the difference between covering the repayments 1.5 times and 1.2 times. At 1.5, a slow year is uncomfortable. At 1.2, you're borrowing to pay the seller.

Here are the three mistakes that cost the first buyer half a million.

1. One deal in the pipeline.
He'd spent three months on it. Told his partner. Pictured day one. When the seller asked for more, he heard himself say "I'll see what I can do."

A buyer with five live deals can leave, and sellers can tell. Never fewer than three live conversations.

2. No number before the meeting.
Negotiating isn't a personality. The buyer who wins did the maths first: profit divided by 1.5 is the most the business can repay each year. That sets your ceiling. Write it down before you walk in.

3. No time limit.
The best tactic in a negotiation is walking away. The second best is a deadline. "This offer stands for 24 hours." Open-ended offers get shopped to the next buyer.

Walk-away power isn't a tactic on the day. It's a position you build before the meeting: a pipeline, a number, and people around you who'll say "this one's not it" when you're too close.

# 35. The structure most retiring owners like

There's one deal structure that retiring owners say yes to more than any other.

70/20/10.

70% of the price is commercial debt from a bank. 20% is seller finance, paid over a few years from the business's cash flow. 10% is seller rollover: the seller keeps a 10% stake.

You put in nothing.

Why sellers like it:

- 70% in cash on settlement day. Most of their money, straight away.
- 20% paid over time, with interest. A steady income for the next few years.
- 10% still theirs. A share of the profit, and a second payday when the business sells again.

Why banks like it:

- They're funding 70%, not 100%.
- The seller has 30% still riding on the business, so they want the handover to work.

Why you like it:

- No deposit.
- The seller stays motivated through the handover.
- Lenders often add a working-capital line on top, so the deal is over-financed and the business starts with cash.

The rule that holds it together: the business you buy has to earn about 1.5 times its yearly repayments to the bank and the seller combined. If it doesn't, change the split or the term before you sign.

We use three structures with owners: 70/20/10, 60/40, and 100% vendor finance. Which one fits depends on the seller, the lender and the business. Picking the right one is half the deal.

# 36. A 10% price rise vs 10% more customers

On a $2M business, 10% more customers adds about $80K of profit.

A 10% price rise adds about $200K.

Same revenue. More than double the profit.

Here's the maths.

Option A: win 10% more customers. Revenue goes from $2M to $2.2M. But every new job has a delivery cost. At a 40% gross margin, $200K of new revenue leaves $80K. Then you pay to win those customers. Say 40 new customers at $2K each to acquire: another $80K. In year one, the volume play nets close to nothing.

Option B: raise prices 10%. Same $2.2M of revenue. Same jobs, same crews, same materials. The extra $200K lands almost entirely as profit. You go from $250K to about $450K.

And for the sceptic: at a 40% gross margin, a 10% price rise still breaks even if one in five customers walks. Most businesses lose far fewer.

Now the part that matters if you're buying businesses.

Profit sets the price of a business, not revenue. At four times profit, $200K of extra profit is $800K of extra value. And it makes every repayment to the bank and the seller safer from the first month.

That's why professional buyers look at the price list before they look at marketing. A lot of boring businesses are sitting on prices from years ago while costs rise every year. Buy one of those, fix the price list, and the deal you did at four times looks a lot cheaper.

# 37. One customer is 30% of the revenue

You find a great business. Good margins, good staff, $600K of profit.

Then you see it. One customer is 30% of the revenue.

Most buyers do one of two things. They walk away, or they ignore it and hope.

Both are wrong. Concentration isn't a reason to walk. It's a reason to structure.

Here's why it matters. If that customer leaves, a business with $600K of profit can fail its debt repayments the month they go. The bank knows it. So should you.

Three checks first.

1. Top-two customer share. Above about 30%, it's a risk you deal with in the deal.
2. Contract and term left. Is the big customer under a signed contract, and for how many months?
3. Who holds the relationship. Does the customer call the business, or the owner's mobile?

Then structure for it.

- Tie part of the price to the customer staying. If they renew, the seller gets paid in full. If they leave, the price adjusts.
- Keep the seller in. A rollover stake, or a paid handover where the seller introduces you properly. The person who holds the relationship has a reason to keep it.
- Size the debt to the business without that customer. If the deal still covers its repayments in the bad case, you can sleep.

Done well, the risk the seller is worried about becomes the reason they accept your structure.

# 38. Profit doesn't pay the seller. Cash does.

You can buy a profitable business and still run out of money in month three.

Because profit doesn't pay the seller. Cash does.

Here's where it hides.

Take a business with $2M of revenue where customers take 60 days to pay. At any moment, they owe the business about $330K.

Get that down to 30 days and they owe about $165K.

That's $165K of cash released, once, without winning a single new customer. If you're paying the seller around $16,700 a month, that's about ten months of payments sitting in your customers' bank accounts.

How to get it back:

1. Invoice the day the job's done, not at the end of the month.
2. Shorter terms for new customers. 14 days where the market allows.
3. Deposits on bigger jobs.
4. Someone who owns collections, with a weekly list of who owes what.
5. Card or direct debit for repeat customers.

None of it is clever. Most owners just never had the time to do it, because they were busy running the business.

This is one of the first things I look at after an acquisition. It's quiet, customers barely notice, and it turns into cash that makes every repayment safer.

It's also why a good deal is over-financed with a working-capital line on top. The business starts with cash in the bank, not a hole in it.

# 39. Decide at 60%

Your biggest problem probably isn't skills. It's waiting to feel ready.

There is never a perfect moment to buy a business.

The market's uncertain. Rates might move. Your team's stretched. The kids are young. Next year will be better.

Next year has the same list.

Here's the rule I use: decide at 60%.

At 60% of the information, you can make a good decision. Waiting for 100% means the deal's gone, or someone else bought it.

And there are two kinds of risk.

Active risk is the one you can see. You buy a business and it might not work. That one you can manage: due diligence, the 1.5 rule, a structure that doesn't lean on your existing business, people around you who've done it before.

Passive risk is the one nobody talks about. You don't buy anything. You keep growing one customer at a time. Five years pass. A competitor buys the business you should have bought. Your business is a bit bigger and you're a lot more tired.

Most owners only count the first one.

The owners I've seen do well weren't the smartest or the richest. They were the ones who made a decision before they felt ready, and then made it work.
