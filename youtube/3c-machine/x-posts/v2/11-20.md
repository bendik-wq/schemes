The ones who own a business. And the ones who own a company that owns the businesses.

The second type has the same work ethic, but every business sits in its own box. And when it's time to sell, they're selling a group, not a job.

Most owners are told a holding company is something for the big end of town. Accountants' stuff. Something to sort out later.

That's not how it works.

3. The exit is cleaner. A buyer can purchase one business, or the whole group. Private equity likes buying a group with one structure, one board and one set of numbers.

4. Shared costs get cheaper. One finance team, one payroll, one IT setup serving several businesses. That's margin you didn't have to sell for.

Most owners set this up too late, after the second deal, when restructuring costs money and tax.

The better order is to decide what the group looks like before you buy, and buy into that structure from day one.

It's not premature, because the cheapest time to set it up is before the second deal, not after. It costs far less now than restructuring later. And one more business is exactly when it starts to matter, because that's when one problem can reach the other.

If you've been running everything through one company, that has nothing to do with being careless. It has everything to do with nobody showing you the order things should happen in.

1. The price is covered by three sources: commercial debt from a bank, seller finance paid over time, and seller rollover where the seller keeps a stake.

2. Then the lender adds a working-capital line on top.

3. So the total funding is more than the price. The extra pays the closing costs (lawyers, accountants, stamp duty) and leaves working capital in the business from day one.

In our example, the bank, the seller's note and the rollover cover the $1.5M. The working-capital line covers costs and gives the business a buffer for its first few months under you.

You put in nothing, and you start with cash.

# 19. Make the Seller Your Partner

The Cheapest Money in Any Acquisition Isn't the Bank's (it's the seller's)

I talk to owners every week who say some version of the same thing.

"Josh, I'd love to buy the bloke down the road. But even with a loan, I can't fund the whole price."

Fair enough. Most people are taught that you fund the whole price. Cash, a loan, or both. Every dollar of it.

That's not how the good deals work.

The cheapest money in an acquisition isn't the bank's. It's the seller's stake.

It's called seller rollover. Instead of taking the whole price in cash, the seller keeps a slice of the business, say 10% or 40%, and rolls it into the new company.

"Get creative with the structure," people say. Great. Creative how? ...crickets.

So here's exactly how it works for everyone at the table.

1. For you: that part of the price doesn't need to be funded. No cash, no extra debt.

2. For the bank: the person who built the business still owns part of it, so the handover is in their interest too.

3. For the seller: they keep a share of the profit, stay involved as long as they like, and get a second payday when the business sells again.

That last one is the part sellers light up about. If you grow the business and sell the group at a higher multiple, their slice can be worth more than the cash they gave up.

See how that works? You need less money. The bank is more comfortable. And the seller has a reason to want you to win.

Now, two questions owners always ask.

...Can I buy them out later?

Yes. Build in an option, for example after three years at a fixed price or a fixed multiple. It can trigger on time, or on the size of the business.

...Can a minority holder block me?

Not if it's set up properly. Drag-along rights in the shareholders' agreement, and no voting control or veto for the minority. Do it on day one, with a good lawyer.

And there's usually a third question, the one owners don't say out loud...

...What if the seller and I don't get on?

That's what the option and the shareholders' agreement are for. You keep control from day one, and you have a way to buy them out later. The seller stays a partner, not a boss.

This is already happening. My friend Jason Nagy's first deal was a $5.5M radiology business, done with zero down on a 60/40 structure. Seven years later he exited to private equity for $200M.

Not every deal ends like that. But the structure is the same one available to you: part bank, part seller.

If you've been stuck thinking you need to fund every dollar, it has nothing to do with your bank balance or your borrowing power. It has everything to do with never being told the seller can be part of the funding.

If you're someone who'd rather have the seller in your corner than across the table, you already think like a buyer.

Done right, rollover turns the seller from someone you're paying off into a partner who wants you to win.

That's the kind of structure we build with owners, and we have the shareholders' agreement in mind from the first conversation.

You can keep trying to fund the whole price on your own. Or you can let the person who built the business help you buy it.

"Just buy cash flow," they say. Great. How much cash flow, against how much debt? Then the thread ends.

So here's something different. One number.

1.5.

It's the debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR. The business's cash profit divided by its yearly loan repayments.

At 1.5, the business earns $1.50 for every $1 it owes. The deal pays for itself, with a 50% buffer for a bad month, a lost customer or a slow quarter.

Here's how to use it.

1. Take the profit of the business you want to buy.

2. Divide by 1.5. That's the most it can repay each year, across the bank and the seller combined.

3. Work out the yearly repayments on the deal you're considering.

4. If they come in under that number, the deal works. If not, change the terms or walk.

Say the business makes $500K profit. It can carry about $333K a year in repayments.

Now work backwards. If you agree $1.5M, split between the bank, a seller note and a seller stake, do the yearly repayments come in under $333K? If yes, the deal works. If not, you change the terms (a longer seller note, more rollover, a payment holiday) or you walk.

What you don't do is make up the gap from the business you already own.

That's the mistake. Owners buy a second business and quietly prop it up with the first. Now neither is safe.

You might be thinking...

...Isn't 1.5 too conservative?

...What if the deal I love only comes in at 1.2?

...And won't I lose deals to buyers who are less fussy?

It isn't too conservative, because it's the buffer that lets you survive a bad quarter without touching your first business. A deal at 1.2 is a deal with the wrong terms, so you change them. And losing a deal that would have sunk you isn't a loss.

At 1.5, the new business stands on its own. Lenders are comfortable. Sellers get paid. And the surplus is yours.

I've bought two businesses with 100% seller finance, and four deals fell over before my first one closed. Seller finance only works if the business can carry the payments, and 1.5 is how you know it can.

If you've been nervous about buying, that has nothing to do with being risk-averse or lacking ambition. It has everything to do with never having a number that tells you when a deal is safe.

Now you have one. You can't go back to guessing.

It's the one rule we never break with owners.

So you can keep buying on gut feel and hope. Or you can buy with a number that protects both businesses.
