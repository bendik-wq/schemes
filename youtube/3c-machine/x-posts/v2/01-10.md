# 5. The 60/40 Deal

The 60/40 Deal: No Deposit, No Seller Finance, No Personal Guarantee

There's a well-kept secret among owners who buy big businesses.

The few who know it buy without a deposit, without seller finance and without signing a personal guarantee.

The rest watch them do it and assume they must have had the money.

You might think they had investors. You might think they had family money behind them.

That's not it.

The secret is a structure called 60/40.

Here's how it works.

60% of the price is commercial debt from a bank. 40% is seller rollover: the seller keeps a 40% stake in the business instead of taking that part in cash.

You put in nothing.

"Get creative with your deal structure," they say. Great. Which structure? What do I say to the bank? ...crickets.

So here's what each side gets, and why both say yes.

The bank is comfortable for three reasons.

1. It's only funding 60%, not 90%.
2. It takes security over 100% of the business's assets.
3. The seller still owns 40%, so they're motivated to make the handover work.

The seller is comfortable because they get a big cheque on day one, keep a share of the profit, and get a second payday when the business sells again, usually bigger.

A friend of mine, Jason Nagy, bought his first business this way. A $5.5M radiology business, zero down, 60/40. Seven years later the group sold to private equity for $200M.

That's the part people miss. The 40% stake isn't a cost. It's a partner with skin in the game while you grow.

Now, if you're like most owners, the next thoughts go like this.

...But why would a seller keep 40% instead of taking the cash?

...Won't they get in my way?

...And how do I ever own the whole thing?

The seller keeps 40% because they get the cheque now, a share of the profit, and a second payday later. They don't get in your way when the paperwork is set up properly from day one. And you own the whole thing when you build in the option to buy them out.

So there are two things to get right.

- Control. The shareholders' agreement needs drag-along rights, and no voting control or veto for the minority. Set it up properly from day one.
- The exit. You can build in an option to buy the seller out later, say after three years at a fixed price or multiple.

Get those two right and you've bought a business with a partner who wants it to work, and a bank that's only carrying 60%.

So it's possible. Sellers rolling part of their stake into the next owner's company is how a lot of deals get done.

It's been done by someone who started with zero down. That's Jason's first deal.

And it's possible for you, because a seller looking for the right partner wants exactly what you've got: someone who already runs a business and knows the industry.

That's what the owners who know this have that you don't yet. A business twice the size, a partner with skin in the game, and a bank that's comfortable, all without a cent of their own on the table.

If you've only ever thought about buying with cash or a big loan, it has nothing to do with your balance sheet or your ambition. It has everything to do with the fact that nobody ever put this structure in front of you.

Here's why smart owners give a seller 40% instead of giving a bank a personal guarantee.

# 8. Rich vs Wealthy

Same industry. Both run businesses doing over $1M a year. Both are good at what they do, and both take home a great income.

The second owner takes the same four weeks off. Revenue holds. The team handles it. He comes back to a report.

Same work ethic. Same kind of business. Very different position.

The first owner is rich. The second is wealthy.

Rich is income. Wealthy is assets that pay you whether you turn up or not.

So here's the test for you. Take four weeks off. No calls, no email.

If revenue holds, you own an asset. If it drops, you own a job that pays well.

Most $1M to $10M businesses are the second kind. Great income, built around the owner. Which means two things.

You can't stop. And when you go to sell, a buyer pays less, because the profit walks out the door with you.

"Work on the business, not in it," they say. Great. How, when the business needs you in it every day? ...crickets.

Wealthy owners think in a different order. Here's the move, step by step.

1. Run the four-week test honestly.
2. Change the question. Stop asking "how do I make more this year?" Start asking "what is this whole thing worth, and how do I make it worth more?"
3. Buy a second business and add its profit to yours.
4. Build a team that runs both.
5. Let the group be valued at a higher multiple than either business alone.

Your income goes up. More importantly, the asset goes up.

That's the move from rich to wealthy. From a business that pays you to a group that's worth something without you.

# 9. Why Most Acquisitions Fail

The 4 Reasons Acquisitions Blow Up (and none of them is money)

Morning,

How come owners who've built a profitable business, run a team for years and know their industry inside out still buy a business that blows up on them?

After all, they've got experience, a track record and cash flow going for them.

But despite all that, some of them sign a deal that eats their profit, then their cash, then their sleep.

Everyone assumes it was the price. They paid too much.

That's not how it works.

Most acquisitions that fail don't fail because of the price. They fail for one of four reasons, and all four are visible before you sign.

"Just do your due diligence," people say. Great. On what, exactly? ...crickets.

So here are the four, and what to look for.

1. The debt was too heavy. The buyer agreed a price the business couldn't carry. Repayments ate the profit, then the cash, then the owner's sleep. The fix is one rule: the business has to earn about 1.5 times its yearly repayments. If it doesn't, change the terms or walk.

2. The profit wasn't real. "Adjusted" profit included the owner's car, a one-off that happens every year, and a price rise that hadn't happened yet. Buy on the profit that survives due diligence, not the profit on the sales sheet.

3. The owner was the business. The relationships, the quotes and the know-how sat in one head. When they left, so did the revenue. Find out who the customers call before you sign.

4. The buyer couldn't absorb it. They bought something their team couldn't run alongside the existing business. Six months later, both businesses are worse.

Notice what's not on the list. Not having enough cash.

Cash is the problem everyone worries about, and it's the one the structure solves. Seller finance, seller rollover and bank debt fund the price. The deal is over-financed so working capital and closing costs are covered.

# 10. Your Valuation Is Probably Wrong

Your Business Is Worth Less Than You Think (or more, but almost never what you think)
