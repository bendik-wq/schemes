# 27. Steal This Offer Structure

The Two-Page Offer That Gets a Seller to Say Yes to Being Paid Over Time

Morning,

There are two types of buyers who write offers.

The ones who write a number, and the ones who write a shape.

That's it.

The first lot write "$1.5M" at the top of a page and stop. Then they wonder why the seller goes quiet, or the bank won't touch it.

The second lot get 100% seller-financed deals signed on two pages.

Not a 40-page contract. Two pages.

Everyone tells you a serious offer means a big cheque. They're wrong. A serious offer means a seller can see exactly how they get paid, and believe it.

Here's what's on those two pages.

1. The price. One number, based on profit you've verified, not the seller's adjusted figure.

2. How it's paid. This is where most buyers get it wrong. Write the shape: how much at settlement, how much paid over time, over how many years, at what rate, and whether there's a payment holiday at the start.

3. The security. What the seller holds until they're paid out, usually security over the business itself. This is what makes "paid over time" feel safe to them.

4. The handover. How long they'll stay, in what role, and what they're paid for it.

5. Conditions and timing. Due diligence, finance, a target settlement date, exclusivity while you do the work.

Now the bit that gets it signed.

Size the payments to the profit. Take the business's profit and divide by 1.5. That's what it can pay each year and still have a buffer.

A business making $250K profit can pay about $166K a year. Over ten years, that's $1.66M. Often more than a cash buyer would offer.

See how that works? The seller gets a higher number, you put no deposit down, and the business pays for itself with room to spare.

And never write "vendor finance" in the letter. Write "paid over time". One sounds like a risk. The other sounds like a pension.

...Won't they just take the cash buyer?

Because a lot of retiring owners care more about a steady income, their staff and the right successor than a lump sum. The business can make the payments because you sized them at 1.5 times cover before you wrote a word. And paid over time, the total is often more than a cash buyer would offer.

My own first deal was this shape: seven years at 4%, a nine-month payment holiday, no deposit.

If you've never had an offer accepted, it has nothing to do with your bank balance or your credit history. It has everything to do with writing a number when the seller needed to see a shape.

That's why smart owners write the payment plan instead of the cheque.

So you can keep writing offers like the first type of buyer, and keep waiting on replies. Or you can write the two pages the second type writes.

# 29. Due Diligence Red Flags

Eight Red Flags That Tell You What a Business Is Really Worth

Morning,

I talk to owners every week who've found a business they love.

"Josh, the numbers look great, the owner's lovely, I just want to get it done."

That sentence is where good buyers start overpaying.

Everyone thinks due diligence is about finding a perfect business. That's not how it works. There isn't one.

Due diligence is about finding out what the business is actually worth before you've paid for what the seller says it's worth.

"Make sure you do your due diligence," they say. Great. Looking for what? ...crickets.

So here are the red flags I look for first.

1. Earnings that don't match the bank statements. If the P&L says one thing and the deposits say another, stop.

2. Add-backs with no evidence. "That was a one-off" should come with paperwork. If it happens every year, it isn't a one-off.

3. One customer carrying the business. Above about 30% of revenue from one client is a risk you price or structure for.

4. The owner is the business. If the top clients call the owner's mobile, the relationships may walk out with them.

5. Contracts that end on a sale. Some customer contracts and leases have change-of-control clauses. Read them.

6. Deferred spending. Vans that should have been replaced two years ago. That cost lands on you in year one.

7. Debts you weren't told about. Tax owed, staff leave owed, equipment finance, disputes. On settlement day, they're yours unless the deal says otherwise.

8. Profit that isn't turning into cash. If debtor days are stretching every year, the profit is sitting in other people's bank accounts.

Now here's the part most people miss. A red flag isn't always a reason to walk.

Often it's a reason to change the price, the structure, or who carries the risk. A big customer becomes a payment tied to that customer staying. Old vans become a lower price. Unknown debts become a clause that leaves them with the seller.

That's the skill. Not spotting the problem, but knowing what to do with it.

I can hear the doubts...

...What if I miss something?

...What if I find a red flag and lose the deal?

...I'm not an accountant, how would I even spot this?

Same Business, Same Price, $500K Apart (Three Mistakes the First Buyer Made)

Two buyers look at the same business. $300K profit.
