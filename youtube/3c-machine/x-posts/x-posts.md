# 1. Stop growing one customer at a time

If your business does somewhere between $1M and $10M, the next $1M of profit probably won't come from growth.

It'll come from a purchase.

Here's the trap.

Every new dollar of revenue costs you more ads, another hire and more of your own hours. Margins don't scale the way they did at the start. Next year looks like this year, a bit bigger and a bit more tiring.

Most owners think there are two ways out.

Grind harder: more spend, more staff, hope revenue outruns costs. It works, slowly, over years.

Raise money: bring in investors, give away a slice of what you built, and answer to someone else.

There's a third way, and it's the one I use.

You buy a business that's already built. A competitor, or an owner in your industry who wants to retire.

Its customers, its team and its profit join yours on day one.

And the purchase is paid by the bank and the seller, out of that business's own cash flow. Not your savings. Not your current business.

Say a competitor makes $500K a year in profit and you agree $1.5M. A lender funds most of the price. The seller carries part as a note, paid back over time from the profit. The lender adds a working-capital line on top. You end up with more funding than the price.

One rule we don't break: the business you buy has to earn about 1.5 times what it owes each year. So the deal pays for itself with a buffer, and it never leans on the business you already own.

That's how owners double, sometimes triple, in one move instead of five years.

The hard part isn't the money. It's knowing which business to buy, how to approach the owner, and how to structure the paper so the bank says yes.

# 2. The first 90 days after you buy

Most acquisitions don't fail at the deal.

They fail in the first 90 days after it.

The paperwork is the easy part. You sign, the money moves, the keys change hands.

Then Monday comes. Twenty people you didn't hire are wondering if their jobs are safe. The best one is taking a recruiter's call. Customers are waiting to see if anything changes.

Here's what I'd focus on, in order.

**1. Keep the people who carry the relationships.** Find the two or three staff the customers actually call. Meet them in week one. Tell them what stays the same. Give them a reason to stay through the handover. The money you spend keeping one key person is nothing next to the revenue that leaves with them.

**2. Change nothing customers can see for 90 days.** No new pricing, no new branding, no new systems on the front line. Customers leave over change, not ownership.

**3. Fix the back office first, quietly.** Payroll, invoicing, debtors, suppliers. That's where the cash is, and nobody outside notices.

**4. Make the hard calls once.** If someone has to go, decide in the first 90 days, do it properly and do it once. Slow, drip-fed cuts kill a culture faster than one clear decision.

**5. Build the layer under you.** The goal of buying a second business isn't to work twice as hard. It's to build a management layer so the group doesn't depend on you.

This is why integration planning starts on day one of our process, not after settlement. We help owners pick a business their team can actually absorb, before they sign anything.

# 4. Why a big loan can be easier than a small one

This sounds backwards, but a bank will often move faster on a big acquisition loan than a small one.

Here's why.

A bank spends roughly the same time and money assessing a $500K loan as a $5M one. Same credit team, same valuations, same lawyers. So the bigger loan is simply a better use of their time.

Bigger businesses are also easier to lend against.

- They tend to own more: equipment, vehicles, stock, receivables. That's security.
- They don't depend on one person. If the owner leaves, there's a team.
- They have years of clean financials, not a shoebox of receipts.
- On larger deals, the lender will often take security over the business's assets instead of asking you for a personal guarantee. It depends on the lender and the deal, but it's common.

Now flip it around.

If you own a $1M+ business, you're not a first-time buyer with a dream and a spreadsheet. You're an operator with a track record, buying something in your own industry.

That's the profile banks actually want to fund.

The question isn't whether a bank will lend. It's whether the deal is structured so the bank can say yes. That comes down to one number: the business you buy has to earn about 1.5 times its yearly repayments.

Get that right, add seller finance and a seller who keeps a stake, and the bank is funding the safest part of the deal.

# 5. The 60/40 deal

The 60/40 deal has no deposit, no seller finance and no personal guarantee.

Here's how it works.

60% of the price is commercial debt from a bank. 40% is seller rollover: the seller keeps a 40% stake in the business instead of taking that part in cash.

You put in nothing.

The bank is comfortable for three reasons.

1. It's only funding 60%, not 90%.
2. It takes security over 100% of the business's assets.
3. The seller still owns 40%, so they're motivated to make the handover work.

The seller is comfortable because they get a big cheque on day one, keep a share of the profit, and get a second payday when the business sells again, usually bigger.

A friend of mine, Jason Nagy, bought his first business this way: a $5.5M radiology business, zero down, 60/40. Seven years later the group sold to private equity for $200M.

That's the part people miss. The 40% stake isn't a cost. It's a partner with skin in the game while you grow.

Two things to get right:

- **Control.** The shareholders' agreement needs drag-along rights, and no voting control or veto for the minority. Set it up properly from day one.
- **The exit.** You can build in an option to buy the seller out later, say after three years at a fixed price or multiple.

This is one of three structures we use with owners. Which one fits depends on the seller, the lender and the business.

# 6. The jackpot exit

Billionaires don't get rich from profit. They get rich from the multiple.

Here's the maths most owners never run.

A small business with $500K profit might sell for 3 to 4 times profit. Call it $1.5M to $2M.

A group of businesses with $5M profit, proper management, and no single owner holding it together can sell for 8, 10, sometimes more times profit.

Same type of business. Same industry. Different multiple.

So if you buy businesses at around 4 times profit, combine them, build the team that runs them, and sell the group, every dollar of profit you bought is suddenly worth twice as much or more.

That gap is the jackpot. It's called multiple arbitrage, and it's how private equity has made money for decades.

You don't need ten businesses to start using it.

Your first acquisition does two things at once. It adds the new business's profit to yours. And a bigger, more diversified business is usually valued at a higher multiple than a small one.

So you're adding profit and lifting what every dollar of it is worth, in the same move.

That's why an acquisition can grow your exit value far faster than organic growth ever could.

The owners who win this game don't work harder than you. They just stopped thinking about next year's profit and started thinking about what the whole thing sells for.

# 7. 100% seller finance, step by step

You can buy a business where the seller funds the whole price.

No bank. No investors. No cash from you.

It's called 100% seller finance, or vendor finance. The seller is paid over time out of the business's own cash flow.

Here's how it gets done.

**Step 1. Find the right seller.** It's an owner who cares more about who takes over, their staff and a steady income than about a lump sum on day one. Usually someone close to retirement, with no succession plan.

**Step 2. Never say "vendor finance".** Say "getting paid over time". Same thing, but one sounds like a risk and the other sounds like a pension.

**Step 3. Size the payments to the profit.** Take the business's profit and divide by 1.5. That's the most you can pay each year and still have a buffer. A business making $250K profit can pay the seller about $166K a year. Over 10 years, that's $1.66M. Higher than most cash offers.

**Step 4. Put it in writing.** A short offer letter: price, term, rate, any payment holiday, what security the seller holds. Two pages, not twenty.

**Step 5. Protect both sides.** The seller usually takes security over the business until they're paid out. You build in a payment holiday at the start so the business can absorb the change.

My first deal was exactly this: seven years at 4%, a nine-month payment holiday, no deposit.

The reason owners don't do this isn't the money. It's that nobody showed them how to have the conversation, or how to put a number in front of a seller that's higher than they expected.

# 9. Why most acquisitions fail

Most acquisitions that fail don't fail because of the price.

They fail for one of four reasons, and all four are visible before you sign.

**1. The debt was too heavy.** The buyer agreed a price the business couldn't carry. Repayments ate the profit, then the cash, then the owner's sleep. The fix is one rule: the business has to earn about 1.5 times its yearly repayments. If it doesn't, change the terms or walk.

**2. The profit wasn't real.** "Adjusted" profit included the owner's car, a one-off that happens every year, and a price rise that hadn't happened yet. Buy on the profit that survives due diligence, not the profit on the sales sheet.

**3. The owner was the business.** The relationships, the quotes and the know-how sat in one head. When they left, so did the revenue. Find out who the customers call before you sign.

**4. The buyer couldn't absorb it.** They bought something their team couldn't run alongside the existing business. Six months later, both businesses are worse.

Notice what's not on the list. Not having enough cash.

Cash is the problem everyone worries about, and it's the one the structure solves. Seller finance, seller rollover and bank debt fund the price. The deal is over-financed so working capital and closing costs are covered.

The four real risks are judgement calls. They're what you need someone in the room for.

# 10. Your valuation is probably wrong

Your business is probably worth less than you think. Or more. Almost never what you think.

Here's why.

Owners value their business on what the accounts say plus what they know. Buyers value it on what survives due diligence.

Take a business doing $4.2M revenue with $560K of reported profit. The owner adds back their own salary, their spouse's wage, the cars, a legal bill, a bad debt and a price rise from next month.

Their adjusted profit: about $968K.

A buyer goes line by line.

- The owner's salary? Someone has to run the business. Only the amount above a manager's wage comes back.
- The spouse? She does the books. Replacing her costs money.
- The bad debt? Same customer is still 21% of revenue.
- The price rise? Not earned yet.
- And the owner is on the tools two days a week, so that's a technician to hire.

The buyer's number: about $614K.

At four times profit, that's a $1.4M gap on one business.

Now here's why this matters to you as a buyer, not a seller.

Every business you look at will come with a seller's number. The skill isn't negotiating the multiple. It's knowing which add-backs are real before you make an offer.

Get that right and you pay for the business you're actually buying. Get it wrong and you've overpaid by a seven-figure sum before day one.

# 15. What brokers won't tell you

When a business goes to a broker, the price goes up and your options go down.

That's not a criticism of brokers. It's their job.

- An auction. You're competing against other buyers, sometimes private equity.

# 16. Why the wealthy use holding companies

Wealthy owners almost never own their businesses directly.

They own a company that owns the businesses.

Here's why that matters more as soon as you buy your second one.

**Risk stays where it belongs.** Each business sits in its own company. If one has a bad year, a lawsuit or a lender problem, it doesn't drag the others down.

**Cash moves where it's needed.** Profit from one business can fund the next acquisition, or a slow month somewhere else, without you taking it out personally first.

**The exit is cleaner.** A buyer can purchase one business, or the whole group. Private equity likes buying a group with one structure, one board and one set of numbers.

**Shared costs get cheaper.** One finance team, one payroll, one IT setup serving several businesses. That's margin you didn't have to sell for.

Most owners set this up too late, after the second deal, when restructuring costs money and tax.

The better order: decide what the group looks like before you buy, and buy into that structure from day one.

# 17. Start with money in the bank

The best acquisitions start with money in the bank, not a hole in it.

# 18. Buying without a personal guarantee

Most owners assume buying a business means putting their house on the line.

It doesn't have to.

A personal guarantee is the bank asking: "If the business can't pay, can you?" They ask because they don't feel secure enough without it.

So the way to avoid one is simple in principle: give the lender better security somewhere else.

Three ways that usually happens:

1. **Security over the business's assets.** The lender takes a charge over 100% of what the business owns: equipment, vehicles, receivables, stock.
2. **Less senior debt.** If the bank funds 60% instead of 90%, its risk drops a lot.
3. **A seller who stays in.** When the seller keeps a meaningful stake, the bank knows the person who built the business still wants it to work.

That's exactly what our 60/40 structure does: 60% commercial debt, 40% seller rollover, security over the business's assets, and no personal guarantee from you.

Larger deals often don't need one at all, especially in asset-rich businesses. It depends on the lender and the deal, but it's far more common than owners think.

Why this matters: the fear of losing the house is the main reason good operators never buy a second business. They'd rather grow slowly than risk everything.

You don't have to choose between the two.

# 19. Make the seller your partner

The cheapest money in an acquisition isn't the bank's.

It's the seller's stake.

It's called seller rollover. Instead of taking the whole price in cash, the seller keeps a slice of the business, say 10% or 40%, and rolls it into the new company.

Here's why it works for everyone.

**For you:** that part of the price doesn't need to be funded. No cash, no extra debt.

**For the bank:** the person who built the business still owns part of it, so the handover is in their interest too.

**For the seller:** they keep a share of the profit, stay involved as long as they like, and get a second payday when the business sells again. If you grow it and sell the group at a higher multiple, their slice can be worth more than the cash they gave up.

Two questions owners always ask.

"Can I buy them out later?" Yes. Build in an option, for example after three years at a fixed price or a fixed multiple. It can trigger on time or on the size of the business.

"Can a minority holder block me?" Not if it's set up properly. Drag-along rights in the shareholders' agreement, and no voting control or veto for the minority. Do it on day one, with a good lawyer.

Done right, rollover turns the seller from someone you're paying off into a partner who wants you to win.

# 20. The 1.5 rule

There's one number that decides whether an acquisition makes you money or keeps you up at night.

1.5.

It's the debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR. The business's cash profit divided by its yearly loan repayments.

At 1.5, the business earns $1.50 for every $1 it owes. The deal pays for itself, with a 50% buffer for a bad month, a lost customer or a slow quarter.

Here's how to use it.

Take the profit of the business you want to buy. Divide by 1.5. That's the most it can repay each year, across the bank and the seller combined.

A business making $500K profit can carry about $333K a year in repayments.

Now work backwards. If you agree $1.5M, split between the bank, a seller note and a seller stake, do the yearly repayments come in under $333K? If yes, the deal works. If not, you change the terms (longer seller note, more rollover, a payment holiday) or you walk.

What you don't do is make up the gap from the business you already own.

That's the mistake. Owners buy a second business and quietly prop it up with the first. Now neither is safe.

At 1.5, the new business stands on its own. Lenders are comfortable. Sellers get paid. And the surplus is yours.

It's the one rule we never break with owners.
