*// LESSON · Not-Your-Fault + What You Were Told Was Lying + Timeline Language*

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Here's what most people are told. Work hard, grow revenue, stay in business long enough, and one day it'll be worth a lot. That's not how buyers think. It has nothing to do with how hard you work, how many years you've put in, or how big the revenue looks. It has everything to do with how sure the buyer is about next year's profit. Nobody teaches that in a corporate job, so if it's new to you, that's not on you.

And the reason the four things matter is that each one answers the same question for the buyer: will this profit still be here after the owner leaves?

Revenue that repeats. Contracts, maintenance plans, memberships, retainers. In a lot of traditional businesses the work already repeats, it just isn't written down. Same customers every year, on a handshake. A contract is revenue a buyer can count. A handshake is revenue they have to guess at. Episode 48 showed you how to turn one into the other.

Daniel adds a second test. He says referral and word-of-mouth businesses can't decide to grow in a given month. Real businesses have a system where more input reliably gives more output. A buyer pays for that knob.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers", 10:51.]

Owner-independence. This is the biggest one. If the owner wins the work or does the work, the buyer sees the profit walking out the door. So they cut the price, or tie the owner in on an earn-out. Episode 74 covers those.

Daniel's own business is the example. He says it now runs without him and his wife. The playbook's advice to sellers is the same: hire the general manager twelve to twenty-four months before the sale, write the processes down, and show a period where the business ran without you.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "I'll invest in your business", 0:30.]

And here's the part that should interest you. If you buy from a retiring owner, the business is owner-dependent the day you get it. That's partly why it's priced at three to five times. Fixing it is how you earn the gap.

Numbers a buyer can check. Revenue by customer, by source, by month. Gross margin. Add-backs. Concentration. If one customer is a third of revenue, they'll price it in. Clean numbers also shorten diligence, and a short diligence is a deal that's less likely to die. That's next episode.

Size. Daniel's rough numbers: about a million in profit might sell around four times. Ten million might get six to eight. Part of the reason, he says, is more customer data and more stability. More problems already solved.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Why $10k per month is a dangerous place to be", 3:35. "You're so scared of being broke that you will never be rich", 5:06.]

That's the roll-up in one sentence. Buy at small-business multiples, combine, sell the group at a bigger one.

Now a worked example. Round numbers, illustrative only.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical.]

Business B. A general manager runs it. Sixty per cent of revenue is on maintenance contracts. No customer over ten per cent. Monthly accounts reconcile to the bank. A buyer might offer five times. Five million, most of it at settlement.

*// LESSON · Not-Your-Fault + That's Not How It Works + Timeline Language*

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

If you've looked at deals and felt you couldn't tell a good one from a bad one, it has nothing to do with your intelligence or your experience or how much you've read. It has everything to do with the fact that nobody showed you what a complete set of documents looks like. Once you know the seven folders, you can see a gap in a deal in an afternoon.

A lot of people think diligence is the buyer's problem. That's not how it works. Diligence is where the price you agreed gets tested, and every missing document is either a delay, a price cut or a reason to walk. When you bought, you ran the quality-of-earnings review on them. One day someone runs it on you.

So here's what goes in. Seven folders.

[SCREEN: The 7-folder data room. 1 Financials · 2 Tax · 3 Customers and revenue · 4 Contracts and leases · 5 People · 6 Licences and compliance · 7 Corporate and legal.]

Folder one: financials. Three years minimum of annual accounts, plus monthly profit and loss and balance sheet for at least the last two years, reconciled to the bank. Plus your add-backs, each with the document that proves it. No document, no add-back.

Folder two: tax. Returns that match the accounts. If they don't match, that's a red flag on the way in, and on the way out.

Folder three: customers and revenue. Revenue by customer, by service line and by source, every month. Your top twenty customers and their share. Who left, and why.

Daniel has a lesson here. One of his biggest early mistakes was not attributing every sale to its source. He says blended numbers hid that his ads were losing money. A buyer has the same problem. If you can't show where revenue comes from, they can't see what's driving growth, so they won't pay for it.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Top 5 mistakes when hitting $1M / month", 0:30.]

Folder four: contracts and leases. Every customer contract, supplier contract and lease, marked: can it be assigned to a new owner, or does it need consent.

Folder five: people. An org chart. Employment agreements for every key person. Pay, entitlements, contractors and employees. In licensed industries like healthcare, every practitioner's registration.

Folder six: licences and compliance. Every licence and permit the business needs to trade. Any issue, and how it was fixed.

Folder seven: corporate and legal. Company records, shareholders' agreement, board minutes, any disputes. This is where your day-one structure pays off. Drag-along rights. No veto for minority shareholders. If someone with three per cent can block the sale, the buyer finds it here.

And the reason the KPI pack ties it together is that it's the story the folders prove. Revenue per customer, gross margin, cost to win a customer against what that customer is worth, churn and concentration. Daniel runs his own business that way. He talks about "moneyballing" his team, managing on objective numbers instead of feel.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Every number I tracked to hit $3m/mo", 0:31.]

If you've been doing the Monday numbers from episode 30, you have this already. Put twelve months of it in the folder.

Here's why it matters, in round numbers. Illustrative only.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical.]

A buyer agrees to pay six million, subject to diligence. Diligence was meant to take ninety days.

Without a data room, every request takes two weeks. Your accountant digs. Your old bookkeeper doesn't answer. Ninety days becomes six months. In month five, revenue dips in a slow season. The buyer's model now shows lower profit, and they come back at five and a half. You've waited too long to start again, so you take it. Half a million dollars, lost to time.

[SCREEN: Agreed $6.0M · 6 months of diligence · Re-cut to $5.5M · Lost: $0.5M.]

With a data room, most requests are answered the same day. Ninety days stays ninety days. The price you agreed is the price you get.

So here's the timeline. First, the week you settle, you make the folders and drop in what the purchase gave you: the sale agreement, the financials you diligenced, the leases, the staff list. Then you add to them every month. Once you're a year out from selling, you get your own quality-of-earnings review done, so nothing in the buyer's surprises you.

And access. Signed NDA first. Serious buyers only. Some documents, like customer names and staff pay, only once there's a signed LOI. Same rule we use as buyers: the NDA is the first test of who's serious.

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

One. Make the seven folders. Today. Empty is fine.

If you haven't bought yet, use the seven folders as your checklist on every target.

*// FROM-TO OPENER · ICP: One-Business Ceiling*

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Building a group of businesses is a phenomenal thing. You get the cash flow, the team, the control. Until it gets big enough that the next step needs more money and people than you have. That's when it goes from "my business" to "my ceiling". And most owners think there are only two ways out: stay stuck, or sell the lot. There's a third.

[SCREEN: "Sell 60%. Keep 40%. The second bite."]

*// 7-MINUTE PROMISE*

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Exit" lit.]

This is Exit, episode three of five. So far: what a buyer pays for, and the data room that proves it. In the next few minutes I'll show you how a partial sale works, so you can take real money off the table and still own a piece of what comes next. All without handing over your rights by accident. If you want one clean cheque and to never think about the business again, a full sale suits you better, and this one's not for you.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

If you feel trapped in your own business, it has nothing to do with being a bad manager or not working hard enough or picking the wrong industry. Confidentiality. Your numbers don't go to competitors who might be fishing.

Security over the business, so you can step back in if payments stop.

Covenants: a minimum level of cover, or limits on new debt, so they can't borrow ahead of you.
