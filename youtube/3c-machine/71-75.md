Two businesses can make exactly the same profit and sell for very different prices. The buyer isn't paying for last year. They're paying for how sure they are about next year. My friend Jason Nagy bought one radiology business with zero down, and seven years later private equity bought the group.

Welcome to Season 3. If you're new, here's the whole model on one line. Credibility: a board and a professional bench that make lenders and sellers take you seriously. Capital: the seller gets paid over time, plus bank debt, inside a 1.5 cover rule. Closing: off-market deals, an LOI, diligence, legals. Then you grow it. Then you sell it. The first three Cs get you the business. The last two are why it's worth having.

This is Exit, episode one of five. It's for people who want to buy with little or no money down and one day sell for a lot more than they paid. It's not for people after a four-hour work week.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. A buyer pays a multiple of profit. Not revenue. Daniel Iles says it plainly: profit is the number that decides whether a business is worth owning. So a dollar of profit you can prove is worth several dollars at the sale. A dollar of revenue with no profit behind it is worth close to nothing.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "The math behind crazy profit margins", 5:40. "Profit is the number that determines whether your business is actually worth owning. It's not revenue."]

Two. The multiple is a measure of risk. The less risk the buyer sees, the higher it goes. Four things move it more than anything else: revenue that repeats, a business that runs without the owner, numbers they can check, and size.

Three. You build those four things from the day you buy. Not the year you sell. Every decision in your first hundred days either adds to the exit price or takes from it.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's the lesson. I help people do this: buy a profitable business with little to no money down, then build it into something a serious buyer will pay for. If you want your first deal structured with the exit already in mind, book a call below. It's me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Let's take the four, one at a time.

Revenue that repeats. A buyer wants to know where next year's money comes from before next year starts. Contracts, maintenance plans, memberships, retainers. In a lot of traditional businesses the work already repeats, it just isn't written down. The same customers come back every year, on a handshake. Episode 48 was about turning those into contracts. Do it, because a contract is revenue a buyer can count. A handshake is revenue they have to guess at.

And Daniel adds a second test. Can the business choose to grow? He says referral and word-of-mouth businesses can't decide to grow in a given month. Real businesses have a system where more input reliably gives more output. A buyer pays for that knob, because it's how they'll get their return.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "You don't have a real business unless you can buy customers", 10:51.]

Owner-independence. This one is the biggest, and it's the most common reason small businesses sell cheap. If the owner is the one who wins the work, or the one who does the work, the buyer sees the profit walking out the door with them. So they cut the price, or they make the owner stay for years on an earn-out. Episode 74 is all about earn-outs.

Daniel's own business is the example here. He says it now runs without him and his wife, and that's what freed up their time. The playbook's advice to any seller is the same: hire the general manager twelve to twenty-four months before the sale, write the processes down, and be able to show a period where the business ran without you.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "I'll invest in your business", 0:30.]

Here's the good news for a buyer. If you bought from a retiring owner, the business was owner-dependent the day you bought it. That's partly why you got it at three to five times. Fixing it is how you earn the gap.

Numbers they can check. A buyer's accountants will test every line. Revenue by customer, by source, by month. Gross margin. Add-backs. Customer concentration. If one customer is a third of your revenue, that's a risk they'll price in. Clean numbers don't just raise the price. They shorten diligence, and a short diligence is a deal that's less likely to die. That's the whole of next episode.

Size. Daniel puts rough numbers on it. A business doing about a million in profit might sell around four times. Ten million in profit might get six to eight. Part of the reason, he says, is that a bigger business has more customer data and more stability. More problems already solved.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Why $10k per month is a dangerous place to be", 3:35. "You're so scared of being broke that you will never be rich", 5:06.]

That's the roll-up in one sentence. Buy small businesses at small-business multiples, combine them, and sell the group at a bigger one.

Now a worked example. Round numbers, illustrative only.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical.]

Two businesses. Both make a million a year in profit.

Business A. The owner does all the selling. One customer is thirty-five per cent of revenue. Nothing's on contract. The books are done once a year for tax. A buyer might offer three times, so three million, with part of it held back in an earn-out until the owner proves the customers stay.

Business B. A general manager runs it. Sixty per cent of revenue is on maintenance contracts. No customer is more than ten per cent. Monthly accounts reconcile to the bank. A buyer might offer five times. Five million, most of it at settlement.

[SCREEN: A: $1M × 3 = $3M (part deferred) · B: $1M × 5 = $5M · Same profit. $2M apart.]

Same profit. Two million dollars apart. And nothing in Business B is clever. A GM, contracts, a spread of customers, monthly books. All of it is work you can do in the years you own it.

What about Jason? I'm not going to tell his seven years for him. That's his story. What his result shows is that the structure from episode 14, a lender plus the seller staying in, can be the start of something a private equity buyer pays nine figures for. The first deal doesn't cap where you end up.

[SCREEN: Jason Nagy is Josh's friend, not a student. Results not typical; records on file.]

**Do this this week (8:30–9:00)**

Score your business, or your target, on the four. One to five each.

## Episode 72 · Build the data room before anyone asks

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Build the data room before anyone asks
  2. The folder that takes months off a sale
  3. What a buyer's accountant will ask for (have it ready)
- **Thumbnail text:** THE FOLDER BUYERS WANT
- **Runtime target:** 11:30 (≈1,750 words)
- **Stage lit:** Exit (Act 15, The exit, episode 2 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Most sales don't die on price. They die slowly, in diligence, while the buyer waits for documents nobody can find. Daniel Iles, who plans to buy companies himself, says acquisitions take one to two years of audit and deal structuring. A data room is how you make yours shorter.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Business lessons I learned scaling to 3 million a month", 45:55.]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Exit" lit.]

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. A data room is one organised place with every document a buyer will ask for. Financials, tax, contracts, leases, licences, staff, legal. Online, with folders, with access you control.

Two. Start it the day you buy, not the day you decide to sell. You already built half of one. When you bought the business, you asked for three years of financials, matching tax returns and revenue by year. That was the seller's data room. Now keep yours.

Three. Clean numbers shorten diligence. That's straight from the playbook's advice to sellers. Every question a buyer can answer from your folder is a week they don't spend wondering what you're hiding. And a short diligence keeps the price where you agreed it.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's the lesson. I help people buy businesses with little to no money down, and run them so they're ready to sell. If you want help setting up your first deal so the exit's easy, book a call below. It's me on the call. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

Think about diligence from the other side. When you bought, you ran the quality-of-earnings review. You checked whether the revenue would last, whether the add-backs were real, whether one customer carried the place. You kept a log of every issue, and you used it to adjust the price. Episode 24.

One day someone does that to you. Their accountants, their lawyers, their lender. And every gap in your documents is either a delay, a price cut or a reason to walk.

So here's what goes in. Seven folders.

[SCREEN: The 7-folder data room. 1 Financials · 2 Tax · 3 Customers and revenue · 4 Contracts and leases · 5 People · 6 Licences and compliance · 7 Corporate and legal.]

Folder one: financials. Three years minimum of annual accounts, and monthly profit and loss and balance sheet for at least the last two years. They should reconcile to the bank. Plus your add-backs, each with the document that proves it. No document, no add-back.

Folder two: tax. Returns that match the accounts. If your accounts say one thing and your tax return says another, that's a red flag on the way in, and it's a red flag on the way out.

Folder three: customers and revenue. Revenue by customer, by service line and by source, every month. Your top twenty customers and their share. Churn: who left, and why.

Daniel has a lesson here. One of his biggest early mistakes was not attributing every sale to its source. He says blended numbers hid that his ads were losing money. A buyer has the same problem. If you can't show where revenue comes from, they can't see what's driving growth, so they won't pay for it.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Top 5 mistakes when hitting $1M / month", 0:30.]

Folder four: contracts and leases. Every customer contract, supplier contract and lease. Check which ones can be assigned to a new owner and which need consent. A key customer contract that can't be transferred is something a buyer will want fixed before they sign.

Folder five: people. An org chart. Employment agreements for every key person. Pay, entitlements, who's on contract and who's an employee. In licensed industries like healthcare, every practitioner's registration.

Folder six: licences and compliance. Every licence, permit and registration the business needs to trade. Any compliance issue, and how it was fixed. In a regulated industry, this is where buyers spend a lot of time.

Folder seven: corporate and legal. Company records, the shareholders' agreement, board minutes, any disputes. And this is where your structure from day one pays off. Drag-along rights. No veto for minority shareholders. If someone with three per cent can block the sale, the buyer will find it here.

Now, the numbers that tie it together. The playbook calls it a KPI pack: revenue per customer, gross margin, cost to win a customer against what that customer is worth, churn and concentration. Daniel runs his own business that way. He talks about "moneyballing" his sales team, managing on objective numbers instead of feel.

[SCREEN: Daniel Iles, "Every number I tracked to hit $3m/mo", 0:31.]

If you've been doing the Monday numbers from episode 30, you have this already. Put twelve months of it in the folder.

Here's why it matters, in round numbers. Illustrative only.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical.]

A buyer agrees to pay six million for your business, subject to diligence. Diligence was meant to take ninety days.

Without a data room, every request takes two weeks. Your accountant digs. Your old bookkeeper doesn't answer. Ninety days becomes six months. In month five, revenue dips in a slow season. The buyer's model now shows a lower profit, and they come back at five and a half. You've waited too long to start again, so you take it. Half a million dollars, lost to time.

[SCREEN: Agreed $6.0M · 6 months of diligence · Re-cut to $5.5M · Lost: $0.5M.]

With a data room, most requests are answered the same day. Ninety days stays ninety days, or less. The price you agreed is the price you get.

That's the real job of the folder. Not to impress anyone. To take time out of the deal, because time is where deals go wrong.

When do you start? The same week you settle. Your purchase gives you most of it: the sale agreement, the financials you diligenced, the leases, the staff list. Make the folders, put those in, and add to them every month. A year before you plan to sell, get your own quality-of-earnings review done, so nothing in the buyer's surprises you.

And one more thing. Don't open the data room to everyone who asks. Signed NDA first. Serious buyers only. Some documents, like customer names and staff pay, only once there's a signed LOI. Same rule we use as buyers: the NDA is the first test of who's serious.

## Episode 73 · Sell 60%, keep 40%

### Metadata

- **Title options:**
  1. Sell 60%, keep 40%: the second bite
  2. How to sell most of your business and keep the upside
  3. Recapitalisation in plain English
- **Thumbnail text:** THE SECOND BITE
- **Runtime target:** 11:45 (≈1,800 words)
- **Stage lit:** Exit (Act 15, The exit, episode 3 of 5)

### Script

**Cold open (0:00–0:20)**

Selling a business doesn't have to mean selling all of it. You can sell most of it, take real money off the table, and keep a piece that might end up worth more than everything you sold. People call it the second bite of the apple.

[SCREEN: "Sell 60%. Keep 40%. The second bite."]

**The map (0:20–0:45)**

[SCREEN: the 3C map. Credibility → Capital → Closing → Grow → Exit. "Exit" lit.]

This is Exit, episode three of five. So far: what a buyer pays for, and the data room that proves it. Today: the partial sale. It's for people building a group they'd like to keep growing with a bigger partner. If you want one clean cheque and to never think about the business again, a full sale suits you better. That's fine. This one's not for you.

**The recipe, fast (0:45–2:30)**

Three points.

One. In a recapitalisation, or recap, a buyer, often private equity, buys a majority of your company. Say sixty per cent. You keep the rest. You get paid for the sixty per cent now.

Two. Your forty per cent rides along. The buyer brings money, people and systems to grow the business, usually by buying more businesses. Then a few years later, the whole thing is sold again, hopefully bigger and at a higher multiple. Your forty per cent gets paid then. That's the second bite.

Three. You stop being the boss. You become a minority shareholder. Every rule we set for our own minority shareholders, drag-along, no veto, no voting control, now applies to you. So you negotiate your rights before you sign, not after.

**Soft pitch (2:30–3:00)**

That's the lesson. I help people buy businesses with little to no money down and build them toward an exit like this one. If you want your structure set up for it from the first deal, book a call below. It's me, not a setter. Now the detail.

**The lesson (3:00–8:30)**

If this sounds familiar, it should. It's the mirror image of the structure from episode 14.

When you buy with sixty-forty, a lender funds most of the price and the seller rolls part of their value into equity in your company. They stay in. They keep skin in the game. In a recap, you're the seller who stays in. Same idea, other side of the table.

[SCREEN: Buying: 60% lender + 40% seller rollover. Selling: 60% to the buyer + 40% you keep. Education, not financial or legal advice.]

Why would a buyer want you to keep forty per cent? Because it keeps you motivated, and it tells them you believe the numbers. If the owner wants all their money out on day one, a buyer asks why.

Why would you want to keep it? Because of the multiple. Remember the roll-up maths: buy small businesses at around three to five times, combine them, sell the group at eight, ten, maybe more. If you sell everything now, you get today's multiple on today's profit. If you keep a piece, you get a share of tomorrow's multiple on tomorrow's profit.

Here it is in round numbers. Illustrative only.

[SCREEN: Illustrative example. Round numbers. Results not typical. Before debt, fees and tax.]

Your group makes four million a year in profit. A buyer values it at eight times. Thirty-two million.

They buy sixty per cent. That's nineteen point two million for your sixty per cent, before paying off debt, fees and tax. You keep forty per cent, worth twelve point eight million on paper.

Over the next five years, the buyer adds businesses and the group's profit doubles to eight million. It sells at ten times. Eighty million. Your forty per cent is now worth thirty-two million.

[SCREEN: Bite 1: 60% of $32M = $19.2M · Bite 2: 40% of $80M = $32M · Illustrative, before debt, fees and tax.]

The second bite is bigger than the first. That's the case for a recap.

Now the case against, because there is one.

It might not go up. Profit could fall. The market could turn. The buyer could overpay for the businesses they add, or load the group with debt. Three points.

Five. Monthly reporting, so nobody's surprised at the end of the year.

Now flip it. One day you're the seller, and a buyer offers you an earn-out. When do you say yes?

Earn-outs are a kind of performance deal. In a small business, the measurement is fuzzy, the people are close, and the money matters a lot to both sides. That's why the rules matter.
